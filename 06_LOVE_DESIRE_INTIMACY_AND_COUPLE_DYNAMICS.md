# Love, Desire, Intimacy and Couple Dynamics

## Purpose of this file

This file asks how Freudian and post-Freudian thought can illuminate adult love without turning a partner into a diagnosis or a relationship into a closed explanatory system. Its focus is the couple as a reciprocal psychological field: two people with separate minds, bodies, histories, wishes and limits who also create patterns together.

The central proposition is simple but demanding. Intimate relationships are never only about what happened externally, and they are never only about internal fantasy. They are lived at both levels at once. A delayed message may really be a delayed message; it may also activate fear of abandonment. A criticism may be unfair; it may also touch shame or a vulnerable self-image. Sexual withdrawal may express resentment, but it may equally reflect fatigue, pain, medication, hormonal change, differing desire, stress, or loss of attraction. Psychoanalytic interpretation earns its place only when it remains answerable to reality.

The governing discipline is therefore epistemic as well as psychological. In applied reflection, keep separate:

1. the observed event;
2. each person's subjective experience;
3. inferred meaning;
4. a psychodynamic hypothesis;
5. a hypothesis about the partner;
6. the reciprocal or systemic pattern;
7. what remains unknown.

A theory may suggest a useful question without establishing that its explanation is true. The absent partner's unconscious motives cannot be known from one person's report. Reciprocal influence does not mean equal responsibility for harm, coercion, betrayal, abuse or boundary violation. Internal dynamics must not be used to erase actual incompatibility, neglect, cruelty, deception, exhaustion, stress, illness, practical constraints or justified anger.

The earlier corpus files established the main theories. Freud supplies a dynamic unconscious organised by wish, conflict, repression, ambivalence, narcissism, identification, repetition and transference. Ego and defence theory asks how danger is managed. Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott and Bion offer different accounts of internal objects, dependency, splitting, projective processes, environmental provision and thinking. Kohut and Kernberg offer sharply different theories of narcissism and love. Mitchell recasts psychoanalysis in a relational matrix. Benjamin makes recognition between subjects central. Bowlby treats attachment as a distinct biologically rooted behavioural system. Fonagy and colleagues place mentalization at the centre of understanding minds under affective pressure. Tavistock couple theory makes the relationship itself an object of inquiry and adds concepts such as unconscious beliefs about couplehood, marital fit, projective gridlock, the creative couple and the couple state of mind.

These approaches overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A repeated quarrel can be understood as compromise formation, preservation of an internal object tie, an attachment protest, a relational configuration, a struggle for recognition, a collapse of mentalization, or a couple belief being enacted. The work is to compare these possibilities, not to collapse them into one generic idea of "the unconscious".

The practical shift this file seeks is from:

> What is wrong with my partner?

Toward:

> What is happening between us, what happens inside me, what may be happening inside the other person, and what remains uncertain?

## The many levels of a couple relationship

An intimate relationship operates on several levels simultaneously. No single level explains the whole relationship, and different psychoanalytic schools give different weight to each.

| Level | Main question | What belongs here | Main risk if ignored |
|---|---|---|---|
| External reality | What actually happened? | words, actions, timing, agreements, money, sex, work, health, practical constraints, deception, care, harm | psychologising away facts |
| Subjective experience | How did each person experience it? | fear, anger, hurt, desire, shame, relief, disappointment, bodily arousal | assuming that behaviour reveals feeling directly |
| Internal relational world | What older expectation or inner relationship may be active? | internal objects, fantasy, memory, attachment expectation, transference-like meanings | treating the present partner as only a screen |
| Defensive organisation | What became hard to feel or know? | repression, projection, withdrawal, intellectualisation, splitting, devaluation, turning against self | moralising about protection rather than asking its function |
| Self-regulation | What happened to the sense of self? | self-esteem, shame, humiliation, cohesion, need for mirroring or admiration | calling ordinary hurt pathology |
| Reciprocal interaction | What did each do in response to the other? | pursue, criticise, reassure, withdraw, appease, control, protest, retaliate | locating the whole problem in one person |
| Shared couple system | What pattern does the relationship repeatedly create? | roles, unconscious beliefs, collusion, enactment, marital fit, relationship-as-third | assuming all role divisions are unconscious or equally chosen |
| Mentalization | Can both minds remain thinkable? | curiosity, uncertainty, capacity to distinguish feeling from fact, self from other | certainty about motives under high arousal |

A useful sequence for reflection is:

**event -> perception -> affect -> impulse -> defence -> fantasy or expectation -> behaviour -> partner response -> interpretation of that response -> reinforcement or revision of the original expectation**

This is a map, not a causal law. Any link can be wrong. For example, a partner's withdrawal may follow shame, exhaustion, anger, sensory overload, fear of engulfment, a wish to punish, or simply a need to sleep. The sequence helps slow interpretation by locating where observation ends and inference begins.

The distinction between inner and outer reality is especially important in couples. Klein's internal object is not the external person. Bowlby's internal working expectations are not the same construct as a Kleinian internal object. Benjamin's other subject cannot be reduced to an object representation. Mitchell's relational matrix includes both real and fantasied relations. Tavistock formulations explicitly attend to the shared system, but even there a shared unconscious belief remains an inference rather than an observable fact.

The relationship itself can also become psychologically represented as something that belongs to neither person alone. Ruszczynski, Morgan and other Tavistock writers describe the couple relationship as an object that can carry hopes, fears, prohibitions and ideals. Nathans (2017) describes the therapist's "couple state of mind" as the capacity to remain involved with both people while also standing outside the dyad enough to think about the relationship. This is primarily a clinical stance, but it offers a useful reflective analogue: in conflict, can either partner think not only "me" and "you" but also "what is happening to us"?

That third perspective does not dissolve individuality. In the Tavistock model, Morgan's creative couple depends on separateness as well as intimacy. Benjamin similarly argues that recognition requires an other who remains a subject in their own right. Healthy couple thinking therefore requires two moves at once: participation in the relationship and enough psychological distance to observe it.

## Why intimate relationships become psychologically intense

Couple relationships combine several domains that are emotionally potent even when considered separately: attachment, sexuality, dependency, recognition, self-esteem, aggression, idealisation, rivalry, practical interdependence and the possibility of loss. The partner can become at once a loved object, attachment figure, sexual other, witness, critic, source of reassurance, rival, co-parent, practical collaborator and representative of the relationship itself.

Freud's model makes intimate love a site of desire and conflict. Object-relations theory adds the reactivation of internal relational worlds. Bowlby shows why attachment systems become activated under threat and why threatened bonds can evoke anxiety, anger and jealousy even in adults. Kohut highlights the regulatory importance of responsiveness and selfobject experience. Benjamin makes recognition by a separate subject central. Kernberg treats mature sexual love as requiring erotic desire, tenderness, idealisation, commitment and the tolerance of normal ambivalence. Tavistock couple theory adds the possibility that two internal worlds form a durable fit in which each person's defences and expectations become part of the other's environment.

Intimacy therefore promises safety, recognition, erotic excitement and belonging, but can simultaneously threaten dependency, exposure, loss, humiliation, engulfment, loss of autonomy and disappointment. The same person who can reassure me can reject me. The same bond that enlarges my life can make me vulnerable to grief. The same sexual encounter that offers pleasure can expose bodily insecurity, dependence on response, and difference in desire.

This double quality helps explain why couple conflict can become disproportionate to the immediate event. A forgotten task may become evidence of "I do not matter". A request for space may become "you are leaving me". A refusal of sex may become "I am undesirable". Conversely, a request for closeness may become "you are taking me over". None of these meanings is automatically true. Their intensity is a clue that several psychological levels may have become active at once.

## Love as ambivalence

Psychoanalysis does not treat mature love as a state without aggression. Freud repeatedly places love alongside conflict and ambivalence. Klein makes the integration of loved and hated aspects of the same object central to the depressive position. Winnicott links the capacity for guilt and concern to tolerance of love and hate toward the same person. Kernberg (1995) explicitly argues that mature sexual love includes tenderness arising from the integration of libidinal and aggressive self-object representations, with love predominating but ordinary ambivalence remaining.

This gives a demanding definition of maturity. Mature ambivalence means that disappointment does not automatically erase affection, and affection does not require denial of anger. The partner can be loved and frustrating, desired and resented, generous and limited. One's own aggression can be acknowledged without assuming that hostility cancels love.

This differs from splitting. In a split state, contradictory qualities become difficult to hold together. The loved partner may become "all bad" during conflict or "all good" during reunion. Idealisation may preserve the good object from anger, while devaluation may make dependency easier to bear. In ordinary anger, by contrast, a person can remain angry while retaining access to the partner's complexity and to the history of the bond.

Chronic devaluation is different again. It is not simply acknowledging faults. It is a recurrent reduction of the other person's value, subjectivity or contribution, often serving some regulatory function. It may protect against dependency, envy, shame, disappointment or fear of being controlled, but it can also be straightforward contempt or a response to genuine deterioration in the relationship. The function must be investigated rather than presumed.

Klein's concept of reparation becomes relevant when ambivalence is tolerable enough that aggression and love concern the same valued object. Concern then becomes possible: "I was angry, I did something that hurt, and the person I hurt is also someone I care about." This is psychologically different from persecutory guilt, in which the self feels globally bad or threatened with punishment, and from compulsive repair, in which the rupture must be erased immediately to restore a good self-image.

Love that survives conflict is therefore not love without aggression. It is love with sufficient integration to prevent aggression from becoming the whole truth about the partner or the self.

## Idealisation, disappointment and devaluation

Idealisation appears in several theories but does different work in each. Freud links idealisation to the elevation of the object and to narcissistic processes. Klein describes idealisation as closely associated with splitting and persecutory anxiety: the good object may be exaggerated to protect against the bad or persecutory one. Kohut's idealising selfobject relation concerns the use of an admired figure to support the cohesion and ambitions of the self. Kernberg distinguishes normal romantic idealisation from pathological idealisation organised by splitting and severe narcissistic disturbance.

Ordinary early romantic idealisation should not be pathologised. New love necessarily involves partial knowledge. Desire and hope amplify selected qualities, and the other person is initially encountered through fantasy as well as reality. The developmental issue is what happens as knowledge increases.

Disappointment can lead in several directions. It can support mourning of an imagined perfect partner and movement toward a more realistic relationship. It can produce negotiation, in which both people revise expectations. It can also activate resentment, withdrawal or devaluation. In a more split organisation, disappointment may feel not like "this person has limits" but "I was wrong about everything; this person is worthless or dangerous". In a self-psychological formulation, disappointment may feel especially destabilising when the partner's admiration, availability or strength has become central to self-regulation.

Kernberg's account is useful here because mature idealisation is not the same as primitive idealisation. A mature form can coexist with realistic knowledge of the other and with commitment to the relationship. One can admire a partner without requiring perfection. Klein's idea of gratitude adds another dimension: the capacity to receive something good from another without destroying its value through envy or devaluation.

A practical question is therefore not "Have I stopped idealising?" but "Can I value this person while knowing more of who they actually are?" If idealisation collapses after disappointment, ask what has become difficult to tolerate: dependency, shame at having misjudged, anger, envy, grief, or a real incompatibility that idealisation had obscured.

## Dependency and autonomy

Dependency is often treated culturally as the opposite of maturity. The Project sources do not support that equation.

Winnicott places dependence inside development. The movement is from absolute dependence through relative dependence toward increasing independence, but never toward total self-sufficiency. His "capacity to be alone" develops through reliable relational presence that can later be internalised. Bowlby similarly treats attachment as a normal system that remains active in adults, especially under anxiety and stress. A secure base supports exploration rather than cancelling autonomy. He explicitly rejects the idea that later attachment behaviour is simply regrettable regression.

Kohut also resists a model in which psychological health means freedom from all selfobject need. The mature self does not become independent of responsiveness, admiration or idealisable others in an absolute sense. Such functions change form and become less archaic, but they remain part of human life. Benjamin reframes autonomy relationally: self-assertion is sustainable only alongside recognition of another subject. Mitchell likewise treats the self as formed and maintained in relational matrices rather than as a sealed unit that occasionally enters relationships.

This allows several distinctions.

**Secure connection is not fusion.** Bowlby's secure base presupposes that the attached person can explore and return. It is compatible with autonomy.

**Autonomy is not emotional exile.** Extreme self-sufficiency can sometimes function defensively when need, reliance or indebtedness feels dangerous.

**Dependence is not loss of self.** The important question is whether reliance occurs alongside separateness, agency and the ability to tolerate the other's limits.

**Receiving is a psychological capacity.** Some people can give care more easily than receive it. In Fairbairnian terms, needing another may activate a rejecting internal relation. In Kohutian terms, disappointment in responsiveness may threaten self-cohesion. In Kleinian terms, receiving good may evoke envy or indebtedness. In ordinary reality, the person may simply have learned that accepting help comes with strings attached.

**Fear of engulfment is not proof of pathology.** Requests can genuinely be intrusive or controlling. The task is to distinguish internal alarm from actual boundary violation.

The pursuit of independence can become a defence against dependency, while the pursuit of closeness can become a defence against separateness. Tavistock writers repeatedly treat healthy couple functioning as requiring both intimacy and individuation. Morgan's "creative couple" is defined by capacities that include separateness and dependency, self-reflection, the ability to exclude and to tolerate exclusion, and the ability to hold one's own point of view without feeling psychically swamped.

A useful formulation therefore asks two symmetrical questions: **Can I depend without surrendering my subjectivity? Can I separate without treating connection as weakness?**

## Recognition and being seen

Benjamin (1988) gives recognition a precise intersubjective meaning. Recognition is not merely approval. It is the response through which the self's actions, feelings, intentions, existence and independence are acknowledged by an other who is also recognised as a subject in their own right. The paradox is that recognition has psychological value only if it comes from someone whose separateness is not erased.

This differs from several neighbouring concepts:

- **mirroring** in Kohut concerns a selfobject function in which the other's responsive appreciation supports the self;
- **validation** affirms that an experience is understandable or legitimate;
- **admiration** positively values qualities or achievements;
- **agreement** means holding the same view;
- **empathy** is an attempt to understand another's subjective experience;
- **recognition** requires acknowledging the other's independent subjectivity even when agreement is absent.

Recognition is therefore distinct from agreement, validation, admiration, mirroring and empathy.

This distinction matters in common couple complaints. "You do not see me" may mean "you do not acknowledge my experience", but it can also mean "you do not agree with my account". "You should know what I need" may express a longing for attunement, but when made absolute it can erase the other's separate mind. Morgan's couple material gives a direct example: the unconscious belief that a good couple should be perfectly attuned can convert ordinary failures of mind-reading into betrayal.

Benjamin's account of domination shows what happens when the tension between self-assertion and recognition collapses. The relationship can become complementary: doer and done-to, powerful and powerless, pursuer and one who is pursued. Role reversal does not necessarily restore mutuality. If partners simply swap positions, the structure remains one of complementarity rather than subject-to-subject recognition.

Recognition therefore contains frustration. The other person will not always mirror, agree with or gratify me. Their difference is the condition that makes their recognition meaningful. The wish to be seen becomes controlling when I require the other person's subjectivity to reproduce my own account exactly.

A practical distinction is: **"Do you understand that this is how I experienced it?" is a request for recognition. "If you understood me, you would agree with me" turns recognition into control.**

## Narcissistic vulnerability in couples

"Narcissism" is theory-dependent and should not be used as a casual label. In this corpus, a moment of narcissistic vulnerability means a moment in which self-esteem, cohesion, worth, specialness or the sense of being a viable self is threatened. It does not mean that the person has a narcissistic personality disorder.

Kohut and Kernberg conceptualise such vulnerability differently. Kohut centres deficits or disruptions in selfobject experience and threats to the cohesion of the self. Failures of mirroring, idealisable support or empathic responsiveness can produce injury, fragmentation fears, shame or defensive withdrawal. Kernberg places greater emphasis on integrated versus split self and object representations, aggression, envy, pathological idealisation and devaluation, and severe forms of narcissistic organisation. The two theories should not be blended into a single explanation.

In ordinary couple life, criticism, rejection, comparison, sexual refusal, failure, dependency or public embarrassment can all threaten self-esteem. One partner may hear "you forgot to book dinner" as "you are incompetent and unlovable". Another may hear "I need more affection" as "you are failing as a partner". The psychodynamic task is not to decide that the person is narcissistic, but to ask what happened to the sense of self between the external statement and the internal meaning.

A common escalation is:

**criticism -> shame or self-esteem injury -> counterattack or withdrawal -> partner experiences evasion or contempt -> stronger criticism**

Kohut would make the injury to self-cohesion especially salient. Ego-defence theory might focus on shame being converted into anger or intellectualisation. Kernberg might ask whether devaluation protects against dependency, envy or humiliation. Benjamin would ask whether each person can still recognise the other's subjectivity. Fonagy and colleagues would ask whether certainty about motives has replaced mentalizing.

Ordinary hurt remains an important competitor. Not every intense response reflects a Kohutian narcissistic injury, and not every need for admiration is pathological. A partner may have been genuinely humiliated, repeatedly criticised or unfairly compared. Reality comes first.

## Projection and projective processes

Projection and projective identification are among the most useful and most misused ideas in couple reflection.

At the simplest level, **ordinary attribution** is a judgement about another person. It may be accurate or inaccurate without any defensive process being involved. **Defensive projection** is a hypothesis that an affect, wish or quality that is difficult to recognise in oneself is experienced as belonging to the other. **Kleinian projective identification** is more specific again. In Klein (1946), split-off parts of the self are unconsciously phantasied as put into the object, changing the way the object is experienced and the subject's relation to it.

Klein's original concept is primarily intrapsychic. Klein's original formulation should not be collapsed into the later interpersonal extension. Bion (1962) develops an important interpersonal extension. He distinguishes the omnipotent phantasy from behaviour that can create a counterpart in reality. A person may act in ways that increase the likelihood that another feels or does something compatible with the projection. Tavistock couple theory extends this logic further: projective processes can help organise reciprocal roles, so that each partner becomes both the recipient and the source of relational pressure.

Four levels should remain distinct:

1. **Intrapsychic phantasy:** a part of the self is split off and experienced as residing in the object.
2. **Altered experience of the object:** the partner is perceived through that projected material, for example as needy, critical, weak or dangerous.
3. **Interpersonal pressure or evocation:** behaviour influenced by the phantasy may invite, provoke or constrain a response.
4. **Actual reciprocal interaction:** the partner responds according to their own motives, history, limits and circumstances. They may accept, resist, reverse or ignore the relational invitation.

The fourth level can be observed. The first three are interpretive hypotheses.

Consider a person who fears being controlling. They repeatedly ask a partner whether the partner is angry, reject denials as evasive, and continue pressing until the partner becomes irritated. One formulation is a confirmation loop: disowned anger or fear of aggression contributes to interrogation; the interrogation evokes irritation; the irritation is then experienced as proof that the partner was angry all along. But several alternatives remain live. The partner may actually have been irritated before the questioning. Tone or non-verbal behaviour may have conveyed hostility. Both may have been angry. The interaction does not prove who "put" what into whom.

The fact that one partner evokes a response in the other does not prove that the first partner unconsciously "put" the feeling into them.

Tavistock writers use the concept of **projective gridlock** for a more entrenched couple pattern in which mutual projective identifications contribute to extreme lack of separateness and intense dependency. This is a specific clinical concept, not a synonym for every repetitive argument. It is most useful when the roles become highly stable, new information is excluded, and each partner seems to require the other to carry a disowned part of the couple's emotional life.

A safer applied formulation is: **"I may have behaved in a way that invited a response I already feared, and then treated the response as confirmation of my original expectation."** That formulation preserves agency, external reality and the partner's independent mind.

## Unconscious beliefs about couplehood

Mary Morgan's chapter in *Couples on the Couch* gives the Tavistock idea of **unconscious beliefs about being a couple** a particularly clear form. The central point is not that couples have opinions they have forgotten. It is that some phantasies about what a relationship is or should be can acquire the psychological status of facts. Morgan describes the important shift from "I know this is how relationships are" to "I can see that I have a belief about relationships".

These beliefs can concern what each partner owes the other, what closeness means, how separateness should work, whether conflict is dangerous, and whether love should eliminate uncertainty. Morgan's clinical examples include the beliefs that relationships are dangerous and that a good couple should be perfectly attuned. The latter can make ordinary failures of mind-reading feel catastrophic. If a partner should know what I need without my telling them, then their failure to know becomes evidence of deficient love rather than evidence that two minds are separate.

A couple can organise around less explicit beliefs such as:

- one person should need and the other should provide;
- one must be strong while the other is fragile;
- love is proved by pursuit;
- autonomy is proved by withdrawal;
- one person carries the anger so the other can remain reasonable;
- one carries dependency while the other appears self-sufficient;
- one is responsible for repair;
- being desired proves worth;
- disagreement means danger;
- asking directly ruins the value of receiving;
- closeness means loss of self;
- separateness means abandonment.

These are examples of possible organising beliefs, not decoding rules.

The Tavistock literature also uses the idea of a **couple state of mind**. In Nathans' introduction, drawing on Morgan, the phrase refers primarily to the therapist's capacity to be subjectively engaged with each person while also thinking about the relationship from a third position. The aim in couple therapy is that the couple can increasingly internalise this function and become able to observe the relationship as well as participate in it. It should not be treated as evidence that a couple literally possesses one shared mind.

Unconscious couple beliefs are best inferred when several conditions converge: the same pattern recurs across different situations; contradictory evidence is repeatedly discounted; the belief has an emotionally absolute quality; each partner's behaviour helps maintain it; and the belief explains more than an ordinary situational account. Even then, it remains a formulation.

Not every stable division of labour is unconscious collusion. One partner may handle finances because they have more expertise. One may do more practical care during the other's illness. A negotiated arrangement can be asymmetrical without being pathological. The distinction lies in flexibility, consent, reality and the psychological consequences of changing roles.

## Couple fit, complementarity and collusion

Two psychological organisations can fit together in ways that are both sustaining and restrictive. Fairbairn's object-relational logic, Kernberg's account of mutual projective identification, Benjamin's complementarity, Mitchell's relational configurations and the Tavistock idea of marital fit all offer routes into this problem.

**Complementarity** means that one person's position is organised in relation to the other's: pursuer/withdrawer, rescuer/dependent, critic/appeaser, demanding/withholding, idealiser/idealised, controller/rebel, emotionally expressive/emotionally detached. Complementarity is not automatically pathological. Many ordinary relationships contain complementary strengths and preferences.

**Collusion** is stronger. It implies that both people, often outside awareness, help maintain an arrangement that protects each from something difficult. A rescuer may avoid needing by occupying the giving position; a dependent partner may avoid agency while securing care. The arrangement can feel stable because each person's defensive solution fits the other's.

**Enactment** belongs more clearly to later relational and couple psychoanalytic language. A relational pattern is lived out in the interaction rather than merely remembered or described. It does not mean every argument is an enactment.

**Negotiated role division** is explicit, revisable and reality-based. It should not be redescribed as unconscious collusion merely because roles differ.

**Coercive control** is categorically different. If one partner restricts the other's movement, finances, relationships, bodily autonomy or safety through threats or intimidation, a language of complementary roles can obscure responsibility. Mutual influence does not convert coercion into a jointly produced psychological system for which both are equally responsible.

Benjamin's model helps explain why complementarity can become rigid. When mutual recognition fails, partners can become organised as doer and done-to, powerful and powerless. Reversing positions may preserve the structure. The critic becomes the criticised; the pursuer withdraws to make the other pursue. What changes is who occupies the role, not the underlying relational logic.

The Tavistock idea of the **creative couple** offers a counterpoint. Morgan describes a psychic capacity for different thoughts and feelings to meet without one annihilating the other. In actual couple life, this supports individuation and intimacy, separateness and dependency, the ability to hold one's own view without being swamped by the other's, and the creation of something shared that is not reducible to either person.

A useful question is therefore not only "Which role am I in?" but "What would happen to both of us if neither of us performed our familiar role?"

## Repetition in intimate relationships

Psychoanalytic theories agree that people repeat, but they do not agree about why.

Freud's account includes transference and repetition compulsion. In *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), he observes that painful relational patterns can be repeated rather than remembered and represented, sometimes in the transference. The death-drive explanation he later explores is a more speculative metapsychological layer and should not be treated as synonymous with the clinical observation of repetition.

Fairbairn offers a different account. If libido is object-seeking, painful patterns can preserve attachment to internalised objects. Familiar suffering may be maintained because it is a mode of connection. Mitchell (1988) takes this further within a relational model: people do not merely carry static internal templates; they actively organise current relationships along familiar lines in order to preserve continuity, connection and a recognisable self-other world. Bowlby offers another route: working expectations about availability and response guide perception and behaviour, especially under attachment threat. Tavistock couple theory adds the dyadic level: two people's expectations and defences can dovetail into a marital fit or unconscious couple belief that recreates itself through interaction.

| Lens | What is being repeated? | Why might it recur? | Distinctive caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freud | conflict, relational scenario, transferential expectation | the unremembered returns in action; repetition may master, express or bind conflict | do not collapse observable recurrence into death-drive speculation |
| Fairbairn | internal object relationship | painful connection can be preferable to objectlessness; old ties preserve belonging | repetition is object-seeking, not pleasure-seeking |
| Mitchell | relational configuration | current relationships are actively shaped through familiar modes of connection that preserve continuity | the present other participates and can alter the pattern |
| Bowlby | expectation of attachment availability and response | threat activates established strategies for seeking or avoiding proximity | attachment is not the same theory as internal objects or transference |
| Tavistock couple perspective | shared roles, projections, unconscious beliefs, marital fit | each person's organisation confirms and stabilises the other's | a dyadic pattern does not imply equal responsibility or identical motives |

Familiarity is psychologically powerful but should not be romanticised. A new, healthier pattern can feel wrong precisely because it is unfamiliar. Mitchell emphasises that change can threaten continuity with familial modes of connection. But a repeated pattern may also have ordinary explanations: similar circumstances, stable preferences, repeated external stressors, unequal labour, or genuine incompatibility.

A disciplined repetition formulation asks: What recurs? What does each person do? What expectation is confirmed? What familiar role is preserved? What would be psychologically lost if the pattern changed? And what evidence suggests this is more than a recurring response to the same external problem?

## Pursuit and withdrawal

Pursuit and withdrawal is a useful couple pattern precisely because it can be explained in several ways.

**perceived distance  
-> alarm  
-> pursuit  
-> experienced pressure  
-> withdrawal  
-> increased alarm  
-> intensified pursuit**

From a Bowlbian perspective, perceived distance may activate attachment protest. The pursuing partner attempts to restore proximity; withdrawal may intensify alarm. But attachment language is not sufficient. Do not reduce every pursuit/withdrawal pattern to attachment style. The withdrawing partner may also experience closeness as intrusive, shaming or overwhelming. Winnicottian or Benjaminian lenses may make loss of self or impaired recognition more salient. Defence theory may see pursuit as action against helplessness and withdrawal as avoidance of affect. Fairbairn or Mitchell may see both as familiar ways of maintaining connection: even conflict keeps the bond emotionally active.

Power also matters. Repeated withdrawal can become a way of controlling access to the relationship. Repeated pursuit can become coercive or boundary-violating. Neither should be excused as an attachment style. Anger, accumulated resentment, sensory overload, work stress, exhaustion or incompatible preferences for contact can create the same visible pattern.

The loop is self-amplifying because each person's protective move becomes the other's trigger. Pursuit intended to restore connection becomes experienced as pressure; withdrawal intended to restore safety becomes experienced as abandonment. The couple can then argue about which person "started it", although the more useful question may be where the loop can be interrupted without erasing differences in responsibility.

The key interpretive discipline is to formulate each side conditionally. "When I perceive distance, I may pursue partly to manage my alarm" is safer than "I pursue because I am anxiously attached". "When I feel pressured, I may withdraw to recover psychological space" is safer than "my partner is avoidant".

## Conflict and escalation

Ordinary disagreement becomes psychodynamic escalation when the issue begins to carry meanings about self, other or relationship that exceed the immediate problem.

A common sequence is:

**trigger -> affect -> defence -> attribution -> counter-defence -> enactment -> escalation -> reduced mentalization**

Suppose one partner says, "You did not tell me you would be late." The first-level issue is behavioural: there was or was not an agreement, a delay and a communication failure. The statement may activate shame in the late partner, who hears "you are irresponsible" and responds defensively. The first partner then experiences defensiveness as proof of not caring and intensifies criticism. The late partner experiences the intensification as humiliation and counterattacks. Within minutes the question has shifted from "What happened?" to "What does this prove about me, you, or us?"

Several forms of vulnerability can intensify the shift. Shame converts a specific error into a global defect. A self-esteem threat can make criticism feel like a global judgement of worth. Attachment alarm converts distance or anger into threat of abandonment. Projective processes can narrow what each person sees in the other. Splitting can erase contradictory evidence. Mentalization deteriorates, making certainty about hidden motives feel more compelling precisely when it is least reliable.

Escalation does not make the original issue unreal. Sometimes the criticism is accurate, the agreement was broken, or the anger is justified. Psychodynamic understanding should help the couple think about how the issue became larger, not explain away the issue itself.

## Mentalization under emotional pressure

Fonagy and colleagues (2002) use **mentalization** for the capacity to understand behaviour in relation to mental states such as beliefs, feelings, wishes, intentions and plans while retaining the distinction between inner and outer reality. It includes self and other. It is not identical with introspection, empathy or agreement.

Mentalization becomes especially difficult in emotionally charged and attachment-related contexts. Fonagy and colleagues argue that nonreflective models can dominate intimate relationships under stress and that disorganisation of attachment can signal loss of mentalizing capacity. The practical implication is decisive:

> The hotter the emotional system becomes, the less confident interpretation should become.

One form of non-mentalizing is **psychic equivalence**. A feeling or fantasy is experienced as if it were reality: "I feel abandoned, therefore you are abandoning me"; "I feel controlled, therefore you are trying to control me". The problem is not that the feeling is false. The problem is the collapse of the distinction between internal experience and external fact.

A **teleological** stance relies heavily on observable action and outcome rather than mental states. Love may be recognised only if demonstrated through a specific act: "If you cared, you would come home now". Such reasoning can be useful in ordinary life, but in complex relationships it becomes restrictive when only concrete proof counts and intentions, conflicting motives or uncertainty cannot be represented.

A **pretend** mode, in Fonagy and colleagues' developmental account, involves decoupling mental representation from external reality. This is not inherently pathological; play depends on it. Difficulty arises when inner representations become disconnected from reality testing rather than integrated with it.

In couple conflict, collapse of mentalization often appears as:

- certainty about motive;
- loss of curiosity;
- treating tone or behaviour as transparent evidence of inner intention;
- inability to imagine that two experiences can both be subjectively real;
- inability to notice changes in one's own state;
- global statements such as "you always", "you never", or "this proves";
- escalating attempts to force the other to confirm one's interpretation.

A practical mentalization reset is deliberately modest:

1. **State the observable event.** What would a camera or message log show?
2. **Name the first-person experience.** "I felt frightened/angry/ashamed" rather than "you made me feel".
3. **Separate feeling from conclusion.** "I felt unimportant" is different from "you believe I am unimportant".
4. **Identify the current guess about the other mind.** Use "I imagine", "I fear" or "I wonder".
5. **Generate at least one rival explanation.** Include an ordinary situational one.
6. **Ask rather than announce.** Give the other person room to describe their experience.
7. **Retain opacity.** Even after an answer, accept that no one has complete access to another mind.
8. **Return to behaviour and responsibility.** Understanding motive does not cancel the impact of action.

Mentalizing does not mean accepting the other person's account as objectively true. It means treating their mental state as psychologically real to them while preserving one's own perspective, external evidence and uncertainty.

## Holding, containment and emotional processing

Winnicott's **holding** and Bion's **containment** are related but not interchangeable.

Winnicott's holding originates in the parent-infant situation. It describes the total reliable environmental provision that supports an immature ego at a stage of dependence. It includes bodily and psychological continuity, protection from impingement and adaptation to need. Later writers use holding metaphorically for therapeutic and adult relational environments, but the original concept should not be reduced to reassurance or emotional caretaking.

Bion's model asks how emotional experience becomes thinkable. In *Learning from Experience* (1962), the infant's difficult emotional material can be communicated through projective processes to a receptive other. Maternal reverie, in Bion's theoretical model, is a state open to receiving such experience. Through alpha-function, raw or unprocessed experience can be transformed into something that can be reintrojected in more tolerable form and used for thought. Bion later abstracts this relation as **container/contained**.

In adult couple life, containment is best used as a cautious analogy. Partners often help one another process experience by receiving distress without immediately retaliating, concretising it or solving it. A partner can say, in effect, "I can stay with the fact that you are frightened without becoming frightened of your fear or insisting it disappear." This can help experience become more thinkable.

That is different from:

- **reassurance**, which attempts to reduce fear through a statement of safety or confidence;
- **problem-solving**, which tries to alter the external situation;
- **emotional regulation**, a broader term for how arousal is modulated within or between people;
- **holding**, in its Winnicottian sense, which belongs to a developmental theory of environmental provision.

Partners are not therapists and should not become permanent regulatory services for one another. A demand that one partner continually absorb, metabolise and return the other's distress can reproduce the very asymmetry that the concept is meant to illuminate. Healthy reciprocal regulation includes limits, timing, mutuality and the capacity for each person to recover some self-regulation.

A Bionian question for a couple is not "Who should contain whom?" but "What happens to difficult feeling between us? Can it be received, named and thought about, or must it be discharged, denied, solved immediately or handed back in intensified form?"

## Shame, guilt and repair

Shame and guilt overlap but organise experience differently. The earlier corpus established that Freud's theory is stronger on guilt, superego conflict and unconscious punishment than on a comprehensive theory of shame. McWilliams and later integrative material make shame more explicit.

A useful working distinction is:

- **shame:** "something is wrong with me; I am exposed, defective or diminished";
- **guilt:** "I have done or wished something that may have harmed or violated a value".

Neither formula is exhaustive. Shame can follow real wrongdoing; guilt can become global self-condemnation. The distinction is useful because the two affects often lead to different couple responses.

Shame tends to narrow attention toward the self and the imagined gaze of the other. It can produce hiding, counterattack, denial, perfectionism or contempt. In a criticism-defensiveness loop, the criticised partner may attack the criticism not because no mistake occurred but because acknowledging the mistake feels equivalent to accepting a defective identity.

Guilt can support responsibility when it remains specific and tolerable. Klein's depressive-position formulation links guilt to recognition that aggression and love concern the same valued object. Winnicott's capacity for concern develops from the ability to tolerate ambivalence. This opens the possibility of reparation.

**Persecutory guilt** is dominated by fear of punishment, retaliation or being irredeemably bad. **Concern** keeps the other person in view. **Compulsive repair** aims to erase guilt or restore a good self-image quickly. **Genuine repair** accepts that the other may remain hurt and that consequences cannot always be cancelled.

Repair therefore requires more than apology. It may include:

- recognising what occurred and not rewriting reality;
- acknowledging impact without requiring the injured person to minimise it;
- tolerating guilt or shame without collapsing into self-attack;
- remaining curious about the other's experience;
- taking responsibility for one's own conduct;
- changing behaviour where change is required;
- accepting that trust may return slowly or not at all.

A self-attack such as "I am a terrible partner" can look morally serious while actually ending the conversation. It may pressure the harmed person to provide reassurance. Specific responsibility is often harder: "I broke our agreement, I understand why that damages trust, and I will need to behave differently over time."

## Jealousy, rivalry and possession

Jealousy is not one thing. Bowlby (1988) treats jealousy, anxiety and anger as normal affects that can accompany threat to an attachment relationship. Kernberg (1995) treats jealousy as linked to triangularity and the feared rival and, in normal form, as an alarm concerning the integrity of the couple. Klein distinguishes jealousy from envy: jealousy is triangular and concerns losing a valued object to a rival, whereas envy attacks goodness experienced as possessed by another.

Several experiences need to be distinguished:

- **jealousy:** fear, anger or pain concerning a valued relationship threatened by a rival;
- **envy:** in the Kleinian sense, difficulty tolerating goodness located in another, with an impulse to spoil it;
- **rivalry:** competition with another for status, love or value;
- **fear of loss:** attachment threat that may exist without a rival;
- **possessiveness:** an attempt to control the other's freedom or access to others;
- **self-esteem injury:** humiliation or wounded self-worth associated with being displaced or compared;
- **realistic threat:** observable behaviour that reasonably calls the exclusivity, honesty or boundaries of the relationship into question.

Jealousy can therefore be realistic and psychodynamic at the same time. Suppose a partner has repeatedly hidden messages from a colleague, changed passwords after questions, and given inconsistent accounts of meetings. Jealousy in the other partner is not adequately explained by "insecurity". The external evidence provides a rational basis for concern. Attachment fear, earlier betrayal, rivalry or self-esteem injury may amplify the response, but they do not make the threat imaginary.

Conversely, jealousy can be intensified by internal meanings when external evidence is weak. A delayed reply can become a rival's imagined presence. A partner's independent friendship can be experienced as replacement. Kernberg's triangular formulations may illuminate the fantasy of an excluded third; Bowlby may illuminate attachment alarm; Benjamin may illuminate the temptation to convert the need for recognition into control.

The boundary is conduct. Feeling jealous does not confer a right to surveillance, threats, restriction or coercion. Psychoanalytic understanding can explain why possession feels urgent without legitimising possessive behaviour.

## Aggression in love

Freud, Klein and Kernberg all refuse the sentimental idea that love excludes aggression. Intimate relationships contain anger, rivalry, frustration, resentment, competition and destructive wishes as well as tenderness and care. Kernberg (1995) makes the integration of aggression a condition of mature sexual love rather than treating aggression as evidence that love has failed.

Ordinary aggression includes the capacity to assert, protest, compete and express anger. Clulow and Boerma (2009) explicitly include self-assertive aggression in the work of differentiation. Without some capacity to oppose, a relationship can become compliant rather than mutual.

The problem is what happens to aggression when it cannot be represented and mentalized. It can be projected, turned inward, displaced, disguised as contempt, expressed through withdrawal, or recruited into control. Klein highlights the fear that one's aggression will damage the loved object. Benjamin highlights how failed recognition can become domination and complementarity. Kernberg highlights the interaction of love, aggression and superego processes in couple life.

Four categories must remain separate:

1. **ordinary aggression:** assertion, irritation, anger, rivalry and protest that remain bounded by recognition of the other's personhood;
2. **conflict:** disagreement that may be intense but remains compatible with agency, consent and safety;
3. **coercion:** pressure backed by threat, intimidation, restriction or exploitation of dependency;
4. **abuse:** conduct that violates safety, bodily integrity, sexual consent or basic freedom.

Psychoanalytic understanding must never be used to normalise abuse or make the harmed partner jointly responsible for another person's violence or coercion.

A relational loop can explain escalation without assigning equal moral or causal responsibility. A person's fear of abandonment may contribute to pursuit, but it does not make a partner responsible for threats used to stop them leaving. A partner's criticism may be provocative, but it does not make them responsible for being assaulted. Psychological causation and responsibility for conduct are different questions.

## Sexual desire and erotic intimacy

Sexuality is not an appendix to couple life in the Project source base. Freud made sexuality foundational. Kernberg's *Love Relations* places sexual excitement, erotic desire, tenderness, aggression, idealisation and commitment at the centre of mature love. *Sex, Attachment, and Couple Psychotherapy* devotes sustained attention to desire, loss of desire, sexual dread, attachment, power, fear of intimacy and sexuality in later life. Mitchell (1988) treats sexuality as a powerful medium of relational search, surrender, escape and mutual responsiveness.

Kernberg proposes that mature sexual love integrates sexual excitement transformed into erotic desire, tenderness, identification with the other, mature idealisation, commitment and passion. The formulation preserves something important: erotic desire is not identical with attachment, companionship or moral commitment. It has bodily, fantasied, aggressive and relational dimensions.

### Desire and difference

Erotic desire requires an other who is not fully under one's control. Mitchell emphasises the vulnerability of sustaining desire for a particular other whose responsiveness cannot be guaranteed. Benjamin's account of recognition adds that desire can occur in the space between separate subjects rather than through reduction of one person to the other's object.

This makes difference psychologically consequential. Complete sameness or perfect attunement can be comforting, but erotic life often depends on enough separateness for curiosity and discovery. Tavistock writers similarly connect mature couple functioning with the capacity to tolerate difference and triangularity.

Difference does not mean contrived distance. It means that the partner remains a person with a body, mind and desire that cannot be possessed as an extension of the self.

### Familiarity and novelty

Long-term relationships produce knowledge, routine and internalisation. These can deepen trust and make sexual play safer, but they can also reduce novelty. The Project sources do not justify a simple law that familiarity kills desire. Clulow's volume contains competing positions: Fonagy's foreword proposes a possible tension between attachment and sexual excitement, while later chapters emphasise how security can also create room for desire and play.

Clulow's attachment-informed discussion in *Couples on the Couch* suggests that security may allow delays, disruptions and separations in erotic encounters to be tolerated without being experienced as rejection. That confidence can support play. The key source-grounded point is therefore not "security versus desire" but a tension: attachment can make erotic vulnerability safer, while over-fusion, rigid caregiving roles or loss of separateness may constrain erotic difference.

### Attachment and sexuality

Bowlby (1988) explicitly keeps attachment and sexual behaviour conceptually distinct while allowing that behavioural systems interact. This is an important boundary. Sexual desire should not be reduced to attachment strategy.

In Clulow's couple literature, attachment insecurity is used as one lens on how people regulate closeness and arousal. Anxious states can blur sexual desire with the need for reassurance; avoidant or distancing strategies can separate sexual activity from dependency or affection. These are tendencies discussed in the supplied sources, not diagnostic rules.

Sex can serve multiple functions at once: pleasure, connection, reassurance, assertion, play, curiosity, regulation of self-esteem, reconciliation, competition, escape or confirmation of desirability. The same sexual act can have different meanings to each partner.

### Dependency, shame and aggression

Sex places bodies and responsiveness into direct relation. Performance, arousal, orgasm, desire and refusal are only partly under voluntary control. That makes sexuality fertile ground for shame, humiliation and self-esteem injury. A partner's lack of desire can be experienced as a verdict on attractiveness or worth even when it is not intended that way.

Aggression also belongs to erotic life in the broad psychoanalytic sense. Kernberg treats aggressive components as integrated into mature erotic desire rather than necessarily pathological. Clulow and Boerma write of love and hate, merger and differentiation, as tensions within desire. This should not be confused with coercion. Consensual erotic intensity and aggression are categorically different from non-consensual sexual behaviour.

### Fantasy and separateness

Sexual fantasy can preserve private psychic space within a relationship. Kernberg describes secret fantasies and triangulations as common elements of sexual life, while Mitchell emphasises that sexuality carries relational meanings beyond simple tension reduction. A fantasy does not have the same ethical status as an action. Internal scenarios can be aggressive, rivalrous or transgressive without constituting betrayal.

At the same time, the existence of private fantasy can challenge a wish for complete psychic transparency. Mature intimacy does not require total access to the partner's mind. Benjamin's recognition principle is relevant: the other remains a separate subject even in intense union.

### Ageing and long-term relationship

Balfour's chapter in Clulow (ed., 2009), and his later chapter in *Couples on the Couch*, treat sexuality and ageing as both bodily and psychological. Ageing brings real physical changes, health conditions, changes in potency, hormonal shifts, illness and medication, but it can also activate mourning, shame, dependency, envy of youth, fear of loss and changed images of self and partner.

A partner's ageing body can confront each person with time passing. Withdrawal from sexual intimacy may protect against grief as much as against desire. But the source material explicitly keeps bodily reality in view. A psychoanalytic account of ageing that ignored physiology would be inadequate.

### Sexual difficulties require plural explanations

Reduced desire, erectile difficulty, pain, orgasmic change or discrepant libido should never be assigned a single psychodynamic cause. Relevant possibilities include:

- medical illness;
- hormonal change;
- medication effects;
- fatigue and sleep loss;
- pain;
- stress and workload;
- ageing;
- sexual orientation;
- preferences and practices;
- differing baseline libido;
- attraction;
- relationship satisfaction;
- conflict, resentment or fear;
- shame or performance anxiety;
- attachment and dependency concerns;
- narcissistic vulnerability;
- fantasy and meaning.

The correct sequence is external and bodily reality first, psychological meaning alongside it, and motive inferred only with evidence.

## Desire versus attachment

The Project sources support a tension between security and erotic difference, but not a universal opposition.

Attachment seeks a reliable bond and, under threat, proximity or confidence in availability. Sexual desire includes bodily arousal, fantasy and responsiveness to a distinct other. Bowlby insists that attachment and sexual behaviour are distinct systems. Clulow's volume explores how they interact. Mitchell understands sexuality as one of the most powerful media through which relatedness is sought, lost and restored. Kernberg treats mature love as integration rather than victory of one system over another.

Security, familiarity and dependency can support desire when they make vulnerability tolerable. A person who trusts that the relationship will survive difference can risk play, refusal, delay and experimentation. But if security becomes fusion, if the partner becomes mainly a caretaker, or if dependency is felt to eliminate separateness, erotic desire may become more difficult.

Conversely, novelty and distance can intensify desire without necessarily producing intimacy. The unavailable other can remain idealised because ordinary disappointment never fully tests the fantasy. Kernberg and Mitchell both provide routes for understanding why desire may attach to unavailable or forbidden objects.

The most defensible synthesis is therefore a tension among **security, dependency, separateness and difference**, not a rule that attachment extinguishes sexuality. Where the source base offers stronger claims, such as Fonagy's foreword suggestion that attachment may inherently constrain sexual excitement, this should be treated as one contribution within the volume rather than as a settled corpus conclusion.

## Sexual withdrawal

Sexual withdrawal is behaviour, not a motive. Reduced initiation, refusal, avoidance of touch, or decline in sexual activity does not by itself establish hostility, loss of love, attachment avoidance or unconscious punishment.

Psychodynamic hypotheses can still be useful when grounded in a pattern. Clulow and Boerma discuss desire as containing tensions between merger and differentiation. Loss of desire may sometimes protect a fragile sense of self from feared fusion. Other possible formulations include resentment, fear of dependency, fear of engulfment, shame, performance anxiety, narcissistic vulnerability, aggression, loss of idealisation, a need for separateness or unresolved conflict.

But each has ordinary competitors:

- fatigue;
- illness;
- medication;
- hormonal change;
- pain;
- stress;
- pregnancy or caregiving demands;
- differing libido;
- changed attraction;
- relationship dissatisfaction;
- lack of privacy;
- practical circumstances;
- a preference not to have sex.

A couple can easily form a secondary loop around withdrawal:

**lower desire -> partner experiences rejection -> reassurance-seeking or pressure -> sex becomes more evaluative and less spontaneous -> more anxiety or resentment -> further withdrawal**

The loop does not tell us why desire fell initially. It only shows how the response to the change can become a new maintaining factor.

A useful inquiry starts with the least interpretive questions. What has changed in body, health, medication, sleep, workload and relationship circumstances? Is desire absent generally or only in this relationship? Is affection also reduced? Is there pain or fear? How does each person interpret the change? What happens when sex is discussed? Only then do psychodynamic hypotheses become proportionate.

Never infer motive from reduced desire alone.

## Infidelity, secrecy and betrayal

The supplied sources support only a bounded psychoanalytic treatment of infidelity. Kernberg (1995) discusses triangulation, jealousy, deception and extramarital relationships within his theory of love, aggression, narcissism and superego functioning. *Couples on the Couch* also situates infidelity within the broader Tavistock field, although the specific Nathans paper on infidelity cited there is not reproduced as a full chapter in the supplied volume. Strong claims should therefore be avoided.

Possible psychodynamic questions include whether an affair is connected with erotic desire, recognition, triangulation, narcissistic regulation, avoidance of dependency, aggression, escape from a particular couple role, repetition or enactment. A third person may function psychologically as a rival, idealised rescuer, witness of a disowned self, or route out of a deadened relational configuration. These are hypotheses, not excuses.

Kernberg's triangulation model is especially important to handle cautiously. He proposes that fantasies of excluded third parties are common in sexual love and that actual triangular relationships may sometimes enact feared and desired scenarios. But a fantasy of triangulation is not the same as an affair, and a systemic formulation of an affair does not establish that both partners caused it.

Secrecy also has more than one meaning. Privacy is part of separateness. Deception is different. Kernberg notes that deceptiveness may be defensive or aggressive and can intensify suspicion even when intended to protect the relationship. The ethical and relational issue remains the actual agreement between partners.

Interpretation does not remove responsibility for deception or breach of agreed boundaries. The betrayed partner did not cause another person's decision to deceive. Relationship dissatisfaction may be part of the context while responsibility for the deceptive act remains with the person who enacted it.

## Rupture and repair

A rupture is not simply an argument. It is a disruption in the relationship's capacity to provide trust, recognition, safety, sexual connection, mutuality or a workable sense of both minds. Some ruptures are minor and routine. Others alter the relationship's structure because what one person believed about the bond can no longer be assumed.

Psychoanalytic theories illuminate different aspects. Klein and Winnicott emphasise concern, guilt and reparation. Benjamin emphasises the restoration of recognition between subjects after complementarity. Bowlby highlights re-establishing confidence in availability and safety. Fonagy and colleagues highlight recovery of mentalization. Tavistock theory asks whether the couple can regain a third position from which the relationship itself can be thought about. Kernberg adds the capacity to tolerate loss of idealisation and normal ambivalence.

Repair may require several distinct tasks:

1. **Name the event.** Repair cannot proceed through denial or vague language.
2. **Restore reality.** Where facts are disputed, establish what can be established before interpreting motives.
3. **Listen to impact.** The injured person's experience must be allowed to exist without immediate correction.
4. **Take responsibility.** Explanation can add context but should not dissolve agency.
5. **Tolerate guilt and shame.** The person who caused harm must remain psychologically present without forcing reassurance or collapsing into global self-condemnation.
6. **Mentalize both minds.** Each person's experience can be explored without pretending equivalence of responsibility.
7. **Change behaviour.** Reparation that exists only in words remains fragile.
8. **Build repeated reliability.** Trust often changes through accumulated evidence, not a single conversation.
9. **Accept limits.** Repair may be partial, and reconciliation may not be possible or desirable.

Repair is not the same as immediate reconciliation. It is not reassurance that "we are fine". It is not avoidance of conflict. It is not forced forgiveness. Kernberg's discussion of forgiveness treats authentic forgiveness as related to acceptance of ambivalence and the possibility of recovering trust, but forgiveness cannot be demanded as proof of maturity.

A repaired relationship is not one in which the rupture disappears. It is one in which the event can become part of a more reality-based shared history without continually dictating the whole relationship.

## What a psychoanalytic couple formulation looks like

A couple formulation is a structured set of hypotheses. It is not a diagnosis, a verdict on who is right, or a licence to infer hidden motives. Its value lies in making several levels of the relationship thinkable at the same time while preserving alternatives.

| Element | Formulation question |
|---|---|
| 1. Presenting pattern | What repeatedly happens between the partners? |
| 2. Trigger | What observable event tends to start the sequence? |
| 3. Each person's subjective experience | How does each describe what the event feels like from inside? |
| 4. Affect | What emotions are most visible, and what emotions may be less accessible? |
| 5. Defences | What protective operations may become active, stated as hypotheses rather than facts? |
| 6. Attachment threat | Does the event raise fear of distance, loss, unavailability or abandonment? |
| 7. Narcissistic/self threat | Does it threaten self-esteem, cohesion, dignity, competence or worth? |
| 8. Internal object/relational expectation | What familiar self-other expectation may be activated? |
| 9. Role each person moves into | Pursuer, critic, caretaker, withdrawer, appeaser, controller, rebel, or another role? |
| 10. Role each invites or pressures the other to occupy | What response does each person's behaviour make more likely without determining it? |
| 11. Actual reciprocal behaviour | What does the other person in fact do next? |
| 12. Unconscious couple belief, if evidence supports one | What relationship "fact" may actually be a belief, such as "love means perfect attunement"? |
| 13. Sexual/intimacy dimension | How do desire, touch, bodily vulnerability, exclusivity or sexual meanings enter the pattern? |
| 14. Mentalization capacity under stress | Can both minds remain uncertain and thinkable, or does feeling become fact? |
| 15. Repair capacity | Can impact be recognised, responsibility tolerated and behaviour changed? |
| 16. External reality and practical stressors | What role is played by work, money, health, sleep, caregiving, discrimination, geography, illness or actual misconduct? |
| 17. Alternative explanations | What competing psychodynamic and ordinary explanations fit the same facts? |
| 18. What remains unknown | Which motives, histories, meanings or facts cannot currently be established? |
| 19. Questions for reflection | What question would increase curiosity rather than confirm the preferred theory? |

A formulation should become less confident when evidence is thin. It should also change when new behaviour contradicts it. The best formulation is not the deepest-sounding one. It is the one that organises the available facts without exceeding them and that remains capable of being disproved.

**Attachment loop**

distance  
-> alarm  
-> pursuit  
-> pressure  
-> withdrawal  
-> more alarm

**Recognition loop**

feeling unseen  
-> demand for recognition  
-> partner feels controlled  
-> resistance  
-> intensified sense of being unseen

**Reassurance loop**

uncertainty  
-> request for reassurance  
-> brief relief  
-> renewed uncertainty  
-> stronger reassurance demand  
-> partner feels unable to provide enough  
-> distancing  
-> more uncertainty

**Sexual rejection loop**

reduced desire  
-> partner experiences rejection  
-> pressure or testing  
-> sex feels evaluative  
-> anxiety or resentment  
-> further reduced desire

**Caretaking loop**

partner distress  
-> rapid caretaking  
-> less room for distressed partner's agency  
-> more dependence or passivity  
-> caretaker feels burdened  
-> resentment  
-> guilt  
-> renewed caretaking

The loop maps interaction, not moral equivalence. One person can contribute more, and some acts fall outside the category of reciprocal loop entirely because they involve coercion, deception or harm for which responsibility is not symmetrical.

## Worked couple patterns

The examples below are invented. They are formulation exercises, not diagnoses. Each deliberately includes more than one theory and at least one non-psychodynamic explanation.

### 1. Criticism <-> defensiveness

**Observable behaviour.** Alex says, "You never follow through. I have to organise everything." Sam immediately lists tasks completed that week, corrects details in Alex's account and says Alex is impossible to please. Alex raises the volume and adds examples from previous months. Sam leaves the room.

**Likely subjective experiences.** Alex may feel alone with responsibility and interpret correction as evasion. Sam may feel accused, shamed or globally judged and interpret Alex's complaint as evidence of being seen as inadequate.

**Lens one: defence and narcissistic vulnerability.** A specific complaint may be converted into a threat to self-esteem. Shame can be defended against through counter-criticism, factual argument or withdrawal. Alex then experiences the defence as further proof of irresponsibility and escalates.

**Lens two: recognition and mentalization.** Each person wants an experience recognised but begins requiring the other to accept a total account. Benjamin's recognition gives way to complementarity: accuser and accused. As arousal rises, each becomes more certain about motive and less curious.

**Ordinary situational explanation.** There may actually be an unequal division of labour. Sam may repeatedly fail to complete agreed tasks. If so, the practical problem requires correction and should not be dissolved into mutual psychology.

**What cannot be known.** Whether Sam's defensiveness is driven by shame, anger at unfairness, prior experience or simple disagreement; whether Alex's criticism is defensive or proportionate to the workload.

**Reciprocal loop.** complaint -> experienced global criticism -> defence -> experienced evasion -> stronger criticism -> withdrawal.

**Reflective questions.** What was the exact request or agreement? When did the conversation move from behaviour to character? Could Alex make the complaint without a global verdict? Could Sam acknowledge the concrete issue before contesting interpretation?

### 2. Pursuit <-> withdrawal

**Observable behaviour.** Priya notices Lee is quiet after dinner and repeatedly asks what is wrong. Lee says, "Nothing, I just need some time." Priya follows Lee into another room and asks whether the relationship is okay. Lee becomes curt, puts on headphones and stops responding. Priya sends several messages from the next room.

**Likely subjective experiences.** Priya may experience silence as distance, rejection or impending abandonment. Lee may experience repeated questioning as pressure, intrusion or loss of psychological space.

**Lens one: attachment.** Perceived inaccessibility may activate attachment behaviour and protest in Priya. Lee's withdrawal then increases the signal of inaccessibility. This does not establish a fixed attachment style in either person.

**Lens two: Winnicott/Benjamin.** Lee may be trying to preserve a sense of self against experienced impingement. Priya may experience separateness as loss of recognition. The problem becomes not need versus autonomy but inability to hold both need and separateness at once.

**Ordinary situational explanation.** Lee may simply be exhausted after a demanding day and need quiet before talking. Priya may know from recent events that Lee has previously withdrawn before raising major relationship concerns. Both facts could make their responses understandable without a deep developmental formulation.

**What cannot be known.** Whether Priya's alarm is based mainly on past history, current evidence or both; whether Lee is regulating or punishing.

**Reciprocal loop.** perceived distance -> alarm -> pursuit -> pressure -> withdrawal -> increased alarm -> intensified pursuit.

**Reflective questions.** What observable evidence signalled relationship danger? Can a time-limited request for space be made and honoured? What would make temporary distance feel bounded rather than indefinite?

### 3. Idealisation <-> disappointment/devaluation

**Observable behaviour.** During the first year, Maya describes Rowan as "the only person who has ever really understood me" and minimises differences in money, social life and long-term plans. After Rowan disagrees strongly about moving cities, Maya begins describing Rowan as selfish and says the early relationship must have been fake.

**Likely subjective experiences.** Maya may feel not only disappointed but humiliated by the collapse of certainty. Rowan may feel that ordinary disagreement has transformed them from cherished partner into enemy.

**Lens one: Klein/Kernberg.** The shift may indicate difficulty integrating positive and frustrating aspects of the same person. Idealisation can protect the good object; devaluation can protect against dependency or disappointment. Kernberg's distinction between mature and splitting-based idealisation is relevant.

**Lens two: Kohut/Benjamin.** Rowan may have functioned as an idealised or mirroring selfobject supporting Maya's sense of being understood. Disagreement can then feel destabilising. Benjamin would ask whether recognition of Rowan as a separate subject has been replaced by a demand that understanding require sameness.

**Ordinary situational explanation.** The disagreement may reveal a major real incompatibility that early romance obscured. Devaluation language may still be unfair, but the relationship may genuinely no longer fit both people's plans.

**What cannot be known.** Whether the earlier idealisation was defensive, developmentally ordinary, or both; whether the current incompatibility is negotiable.

**Reciprocal loop.** idealisation -> difference emerges -> disappointment -> devaluation -> Rowan becomes defensive/distant -> Maya treats distance as proof that the idealised relationship never existed.

**Reflective questions.** Which good qualities remain true despite disagreement? Which differences were previously minimised? Is mourning an idealised future more appropriate than deciding that one person was fraudulent?

### 4. Jealousy <-> reassurance-seeking or control

**Observable behaviour.** Noah notices that Ellis has been exchanging frequent late-night messages with a former partner. Ellis initially says the messages are "nothing", deletes part of the thread and later gives a fuller account. Noah then asks to inspect the phone regularly and becomes distressed when Ellis goes out without sharing live location.

**Likely subjective experiences.** Noah may feel fear of loss, humiliation and uncertainty. Ellis may feel guilty, scrutinised or controlled and may also fear that any private space will now be interpreted as deception.

**Lens one: reality plus attachment.** Noah's jealousy has a realistic basis because secrecy and inconsistent accounts occurred. Attachment threat can intensify the response, but "insecurity" would be an inadequate explanation of the initial concern.

**Lens two: Kernberg/mentalization.** A triangular threat can activate rivalry and self-esteem injury. Once trust is damaged, psychic equivalence can grow: feeling unsafe becomes proof that every unobserved action is dangerous. Concrete surveillance can become a teleological demand for proof.

**Ordinary situational explanation.** Trust has been materially damaged. Repeated checking may be an understandable short-term attempt to reduce uncertainty, even if it later becomes ineffective or controlling.

**What cannot be known.** The full meaning of Ellis's contact without Ellis's account and evidence; whether ongoing secrecy exists; whether monitoring reflects temporary repair conditions or coercive control.

**Reciprocal loop.** secrecy -> justified alarm -> checking -> Ellis experiences scrutiny -> defensiveness or further privacy -> increased alarm -> more checking.

**Reflective questions.** What facts need clarification? What boundaries were agreed? What transparency is voluntarily part of repair, and what would cross into coercive monitoring? Can trust be rebuilt through behaviour rather than endless proof?

### 5. Sexual withdrawal <-> perceived rejection

**Observable behaviour.** After several months of less frequent sex, Jordan stops initiating because repeated refusals feel humiliating. Casey then reports feeling pressured whenever Jordan does initiate, because each encounter seems to carry the question "Do you still want me?". Affection outside sex also declines.

**Likely subjective experiences.** Jordan may experience reduced sex as rejection and loss of desirability. Casey may experience initiation as a test of love rather than invitation, making desire more evaluative and less spontaneous.

**Lens one: Mitchell/Clulow.** Sexuality can become a medium for reassurance, dependency and relational vulnerability. Once sex carries the burden of proving the relationship, erotic play can narrow.

**Lens two: shame and self-regulation.** Jordan's shame may lead to withdrawal; Casey may anticipate responsibility for Jordan's self-esteem and feel trapped, which produces further withdrawal.

**Ordinary situational explanation, and in this example the best first explanation.** Casey recently started medication associated with reduced libido, is sleeping poorly and reports pelvic pain. These facts provide a stronger initial explanation than unconscious hostility or fear of intimacy. Psychodynamic meanings may become relevant to the secondary loop, but should not replace medical and bodily reality.

**What cannot be known.** How desire would change if pain, sleep and medication issues improved; whether relationship resentment also contributes.

**Reciprocal loop.** reduced desire from bodily/practical factors -> Jordan experiences rejection -> initiation becomes loaded with reassurance need -> Casey feels pressure -> more avoidance -> Jordan withdraws affection.

**Reflective questions.** What changed physically and practically before desire changed? Can affection be restored without making it a contract for sex? Can desire differences be discussed without turning them into judgments of worth?

### 6. Caretaking <-> dependency/resentment

**Observable behaviour.** Sofia routinely anticipates Ben's practical needs, rearranges her schedule when he is stressed and solves problems before he asks. Ben increasingly relies on her planning. Sofia later complains that Ben is incapable and that "everything falls on me". When Ben takes independent action, Sofia often corrects how he does it.

**Likely subjective experiences.** Sofia may feel indispensable, burdened and unrecognised. Ben may feel cared for but also incompetent, criticised or discouraged from agency.

**Lens one: Fairbairn/defence.** Caretaking may be a way to secure connection while keeping Sofia in the less vulnerable position of giver rather than receiver. Ben's dependence may fit this organisation and then become a target of resentment.

**Lens two: Benjamin/Tavistock.** A complementary doer/done-to structure may develop. A couple belief such as "one of us must be competent and the other must be looked after" can stabilise roles. The pattern becomes self-confirming if Sofia intervenes whenever Ben acts and Ben withdraws agency in response.

**Ordinary situational explanation.** Ben may be going through a temporary period of severe work stress or illness in which more support is rational. Sofia may also have more skill in specific tasks. Asymmetry alone is not pathology.

**What cannot be known.** Whether Sofia's caretaking primarily expresses generosity, anxiety, identity, control or some mixture; whether Ben's passivity precedes or follows the caretaking.

**Reciprocal loop.** Ben struggles -> Sofia rapidly takes over -> Ben has less opportunity to act -> Ben appears more dependent -> Sofia feels burdened -> criticism -> Ben loses confidence -> Sofia takes over again.

**Reflective questions.** What help was requested? What happens if Sofia waits before intervening? What tasks can Ben own without correction? Can Sofia express need directly rather than earning connection through indispensability?

## Self-reflection before partner interpretation

Couple theory is safest when it begins with one's own experience and behaviour rather than with claims about the absent partner's unconscious mind.

Before asking:

> What is my partner unconsciously doing?

ask:

- What happened?
- What did I feel?
- What did I fear?
- What did I want?
- What did I do next?
- What did my behaviour invite?
- What story did I tell myself about the other?
- What evidence supports that story?
- What would the other person say happened?
- What alternative explanation fits?
- What remains unknown?

The phrase **"what did my behaviour invite?"** requires care. It asks about interpersonal pressure and reciprocal uptake, not culpability for whatever the other person did. An invitation can be refused. The other person remains an agent. If my anxious questioning increased the probability of irritation, that is useful to see. It does not make me responsible if the other responds with intimidation or violence.

This sequence also protects against retrospective certainty. The theory should follow the observed pattern, not be used to manufacture one. If the external explanation is sufficient, there may be no need for a deeper account. If a deeper hypothesis is useful, it should remain one hypothesis among several.

The final applied file will develop a fuller protocol for self-analysis and relationship reflection. Here the rule is narrower: interpret oneself before interpreting the partner, and treat every account of the partner's inner world as provisional.

## Common mistakes and misuses

### "My partner is projecting."

Perhaps, but projection is an inferred defence, not a synonym for being wrong. First separate the partner's statement, the available evidence, your reaction and the possibility that the statement contains some truth. If projection is considered, ask what makes that hypothesis better than ordinary misperception, disagreement or justified criticism.

### "This proves attachment trauma."

It does not. Attachment theory describes a behavioural system and developmental expectations. An adult pattern can be attachment-related without proving trauma, and similar behaviour can arise from current stress, temperament, learning, practical circumstances or another psychodynamic process.

### "They are a narcissist."

This collapses ordinary narcissistic vulnerability, theoretical accounts of narcissism, personality style and diagnosable pathology into a label. The more disciplined question is what happens to self-esteem, self-cohesion, idealisation, shame, admiration or recognition in this specific moment. Kohut and Kernberg would not give the same explanation.

### "Our conflict is just transference."

Transference is primarily a clinical concept. Its extension to intimate relationships can illuminate how past relational meanings shape the present, but the current partner is not merely a screen. The conflict may also concern a real present-day problem.

### "They made me feel this, so it must be projective identification."

No. Your feeling may arise from your history, the partner's actual behaviour, the situation, interpersonal pressure or some combination. Bion and later couple theory allow an interpersonal counterpart to projective processes, but the actual response never proves the underlying phantasy.

### "If I understand the unconscious cause, the behaviour is acceptable."

Explanation and justification are separate. A fear of abandonment can help explain controlling behaviour without making control acceptable. Shame can help explain verbal cruelty without cancelling responsibility for it.

### "Both people create the system, so both are equally responsible."

A reciprocal pattern can be jointly maintained while responsibility remains unequal. Coercion, deception, violence and boundary violations are actions for which the actor remains responsible.

### "Sexual withdrawal always means hostility."

No. Reduced desire can reflect health, hormones, medication, pain, fatigue, ageing, stress, preference, differing libido, attraction or practical circumstances. Psychodynamic hypotheses become appropriate only after these possibilities remain visible.

### "Jealousy always reflects insecurity."

Jealousy can involve attachment alarm, self-esteem injury or triangular fantasy, but it can also respond to real secrecy, betrayal or boundary ambiguity. External evidence must be assessed before jealousy is psychologised.

### "Repair means forgiving."

Repair concerns reality, impact, responsibility, changed behaviour and trust. Forgiveness may or may not follow. It cannot be required from the injured person as proof of psychological health.

### "Mentalizing means accepting the other person's version."

Mentalization means treating behaviour as related to mental states while retaining uncertainty. One can understand that the other genuinely felt attacked and still conclude that their factual account is inaccurate.

### "Secure attachment means no conflict."

Bowlby's secure-base model does not predict conflict-free relationships. Attachment security concerns confidence in availability and support, not permanent harmony. Anger, jealousy, separations and disagreements remain possible.

### "Good couples meet each other's needs automatically."

Morgan's Tavistock material directly challenges the unconscious belief that good couples should be perfectly attuned. Separate minds require communication. Recognition is not mind-reading, and dependency does not entitle one person to unlimited provision from the other.

## When interpretation should stop

Psychodynamic reflection should not be used to explain away violence, coercive control, threats, stalking, non-consensual sexual behaviour, serious deception, acute psychiatric crisis or immediate safety concerns.

In such situations, reality, safety, consent and appropriate professional support take precedence over interpretive curiosity. The question is not which unconscious couple pattern produced the danger. The first question is what is happening and what is required for safety and clear boundaries.

## Limits and unresolved questions

Psychoanalytic couple theory is rich in formulation but uneven in evidentiary status. Many concepts arise from clinical observation and theoretical reconstruction rather than controlled causal research. Their practical value does not establish their literal truth.

Several limits are especially important.

First, **developmental reconstruction is uncertain**. An adult pattern may resemble an early relational configuration without allowing us to infer exactly what happened in childhood. Klein's earliest developmental hypotheses were themselves explicitly inferential. Winnicott, Fairbairn and Kohut reconstruct different developmental processes from different theoretical premises.

Second, **theories often explain the same behaviour differently**. Withdrawal can be defence against affect, preservation of an internal object relation, protection from impingement, regulation of attachment threat, protection of self-cohesion, a relational invitation, failure of recognition or collapse of mentalization. The multiplicity is not solved by calling all explanations "psychodynamic".

Third, **the couple literature contains semantic extensions of earlier concepts**. Projective identification moves from Klein's intrapsychic phantasy toward Bionian communication and Tavistock interpersonal process. Holding and containment are often used more broadly in clinical language than in Winnicott's and Bion's original formulations. Such extensions can be useful if their lineage is made explicit.

Fourth, **sexuality remains irreducibly plural**. Freud, Kernberg, Mitchell, attachment-informed couple theory and the Tavistock tradition do not offer one settled explanation of desire. The supplied literature supports a tension between security and separateness, but not a universal law that secure attachment suppresses desire or that distance increases it.

Fifth, **shame is less systematically theorised in the foundational Freud material than guilt**. The corpus can formulate shame through later integrative and narcissism-related sources, but should not attribute a comprehensive shame theory to Freud or Anna Freud.

Sixth, **one partner's account cannot disclose the other's unconscious**. Couple reflection conducted by one person is structurally limited. It can generate better questions about reciprocity and self-contribution, but it cannot substitute for the other person's subjectivity.

Finally, **psychoanalysis does not decide whether a relationship should continue**. A pattern can be understandable and still unacceptable. Two people can mentalize each other well and remain incompatible. Repair can be possible without reconciliation. Insight does not create desire, trust or shared values by itself.

## Questions this framework helps you ask

These questions are retrieval prompts, not a protocol and not a demand to interpret every difficulty.

### Reality and experience

- What actually happened, stripped of motive attribution?
- What did each person say or do?
- What practical, bodily or external factors matter?
- What did I experience emotionally and physically?
- What part of my account is observation and what part is meaning?

### Desire, dependency and self

- What do I want from this person in this moment: closeness, sex, reassurance, admiration, help, space, recognition, repair?
- What is difficult about needing that?
- What is difficult about receiving it?
- Does closeness feel like safety, engulfment, obligation or all three?
- Does separateness feel like freedom, abandonment, rejection or ordinary difference?
- What happens to my self-esteem when the partner disagrees, refuses, succeeds or turns attention elsewhere?

### Internal relationship and repetition

- Does this interaction feel strikingly familiar?
- What role do I reliably occupy?
- What role does my behaviour invite the other to occupy?
- What changes if neither of us performs the familiar role?
- Am I repeating a conflict, preserving an internal object tie, following an attachment expectation, or re-creating a relational configuration? Which explanation has actual evidence?

### Recognition and mutuality

- Am I asking to be understood or requiring agreement?
- Can I recognise the other person's experience without surrendering my own?
- Am I treating the other as a separate centre of desire and agency?
- Have we become organised as doer and done-to, critic and accused, pursuer and withdrawer?
- If the roles reverse, does the underlying structure actually change?

### Projection and couple belief

- What quality am I especially certain belongs to the other?
- Is there evidence that it also exists in me, or is that merely a tempting interpretation?
- What response does my behaviour make more likely?
- Am I mistaking a belief about relationships for a fact about relationships?
- Do I believe love should mean perfect attunement, constant availability, automatic sexual desire, no conflict or no private mental space?

### Attachment and mentalization

- What sign of distance or unavailability activated me?
- Is my response aimed at restoring proximity, recovering autonomy or both?
- Am I treating my fear as proof of the other's intention?
- How certain am I about what the other is thinking?
- Would I make the same interpretation if I were calmer?
- What rival explanation can I still entertain?

### Sexual intimacy

- What changed in health, medication, hormones, pain, sleep, workload or practical circumstances?
- What meanings has sex acquired in the relationship: pleasure, reassurance, proof of desirability, obligation, repair, power, play, escape?
- Can desire exist without becoming a test of love?
- Can refusal exist without becoming a verdict on worth?
- Is there enough separateness for curiosity and enough security for vulnerability?
- What remains private fantasy, and what belongs to an agreed relational boundary?

### Rupture and repair

- What exactly was ruptured: trust, recognition, safety, sexual connection, an agreement, an idealised image?
- Has reality been named clearly?
- Can the person who caused harm hear impact without demanding immediate reassurance?
- Is apology accompanied by changed behaviour?
- Is the injured person being pressured to forgive before trust has been rebuilt?
- What would repeated reliability look like in observable terms?
- Is repair possible without reconciliation?

### Epistemic discipline

- What is my strongest current hypothesis?
- What would disconfirm it?
- What is the least psychodynamic explanation that fits the facts?
- Am I using theory to increase curiosity or to increase certainty?
- What do I genuinely not know?
