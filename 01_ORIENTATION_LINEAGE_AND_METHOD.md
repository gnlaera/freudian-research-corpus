# Orientation, Lineage and Method

## Purpose of this file

This file is the map for the Freudian and Post-Freudian Thought Research Corpus. Its purpose is not to decide which psychoanalytic school is correct. It is to make the differences between the major traditions visible enough that later files can be read without flattening them into a single generic "psychodynamic" language.

Psychoanalytic thought began with Freud, but it did not remain conceptually stationary. Freud himself repeatedly revised his model. Later analysts then disagreed about what should count as the basic unit of explanation: instinctual drive, conflict, ego function, defence, internal objects, environmental provision, self-cohesion, attachment, the relational field, recognition, or the capacity to understand minds in terms of mental states. These are not merely different labels for the same thing. They arrange psychological experience differently and therefore generate different questions.

Stephen A. Mitchell and Margaret J. Black (1995/2016) are useful guides to this plurality because they present post-Freudian psychoanalysis as a set of branching traditions rather than a fixed doctrine. Mitchell (1988) goes further. He argues that simply retaining every theory in an eclectic collection creates conceptual confusion, because theories may rest on incompatible assumptions. His proposed alternative is selective integration: identify what can be combined, what requires reframing, and what must remain in tension.

That is the governing method here. Freud is presented first on his own terms. Later theorists are then presented as revisions, breaks, extensions or competing models where the Project sources support those descriptions. The practical aim is equally disciplined. Psychoanalytic ideas can help generate questions about one's own experience and relationships, but an interpretation is not direct access to hidden truth. In particular, another person's motives, fantasies and developmental history remain partly unknown.

This file therefore has two jobs:

1. to provide a conceptual lineage map for the rest of the corpus; and
2. to establish a method for using psychoanalytic ideas in self-reflection and couple reflection without turning theory into pseudo-diagnosis.

## How to use this corpus

The corpus is best read as a set of competing lenses rather than a ladder in which each later theory replaces the one before it. Freud's language of conflict and repression may illuminate one aspect of an event. Fairbairn's focus on preserving relationships may illuminate another. Winnicott may direct attention toward dependence and environmental provision. Kohut may foreground threats to self-cohesion. Kernberg may focus on aggression, splitting and idealisation or devaluation. Bowlby may ask about attachment security and availability. Mitchell may ask what relational pattern is being recreated between people. Benjamin may ask whether each person can experience the other as a separate subject. Fonagy and colleagues may ask whether either person can keep mental states in mind without treating an inference as fact.

The point is not to ask every question every time. The point is to know what kind of question a theory licenses and what assumptions come with it.

Three reading rules are especially important.

**First, distinguish observation from formulation.** "My partner looked at the phone while I was speaking" is an observed event. "I felt dismissed" is a subjective experience. "My needs do not matter" is a meaning attributed to the event. "I am repeating a familiar expectation of not being recognised" is a psychodynamic hypothesis. These are different levels of statement.

**Second, hold explanations comparatively.** A sharp reaction can have several plausible explanations at once: justified irritation, fatigue, attachment alarm, shame, a defensive manoeuvre, a self-esteem injury, a familiar internal object relation, or a reciprocal interaction that has become self-reinforcing. The presence of a psychologically interesting hypothesis does not make the ordinary explanation false.

**Third, use concepts functionally rather than morally.** A defence is an operation that protects the person from something harder to bear. It may be adaptive in one context and costly in another. A relational pattern may preserve connection while also producing suffering. A formulation asks, "What does this accomplish, and at what cost?" It does not ask, "What is wrong with this person?"

McWilliams (2004, 2011) is useful here because her contemporary psychoanalytic formulation is inferential, contextual and dimensional. She treats psychoanalytic understanding as a way of organising complex subjective experience, not as a licence to assign a fixed label from a distance. That stance is consistent with the method used throughout this corpus.

## What psychoanalytic thought is trying to explain

Psychoanalytic traditions differ sharply, but they repeatedly return to a common family of puzzles:

- Why do people act against their stated intentions?
- How can experience remain psychologically active without being consciously represented?
- Why do painful patterns repeat?
- How are anxiety, conflict and difficult affect managed?
- How do relationships become internal psychological structures?
- How do dependence and autonomy develop?
- How is a coherent sense of self sustained or disrupted?
- How does another person become experienced as a separate subject rather than only through one's needs, fears or fantasies?
- How do two people create reciprocal patterns that neither fully intends?

The schools answer these questions differently. Freud begins with the dynamic unconscious, conflict, repression, drive and compromise. Anna Freud shifts attention toward the ego's defensive operations. Klein foregrounds primitive anxiety, phantasy, splitting and internal objects. Fairbairn makes object-seeking and the preservation of relationships primary. Winnicott centres dependence and environmental provision. Bion asks how emotional experience becomes thinkable. Kohut makes self-cohesion and selfobject experience central, while Kernberg retains conflict and aggression within an object-relations framework. Bowlby recasts attachment as a primary behavioural system. Mitchell locates psychological life in a relational matrix, Benjamin focuses on recognition between subjects, and Fonagy and colleagues emphasise the capacity to represent minds under conditions of uncertainty.

The shared subject matter therefore does not imply a shared theory. The central discipline of this corpus is to keep the recurring human problem separate from the particular theory used to explain it.

## Freud: the starting architecture

Freud is the starting point because later traditions develop, revise or reject parts of his architecture. Freud's own model also changes over time, so this section should be read as a map rather than a single timeless system. File `02_FREUD_THE_DYNAMIC_UNCONSCIOUS.md` is the substantive home for the full account.

### The dynamic unconscious

For Freud, the unconscious is not simply information outside current attention. The decisive idea is dynamic: some mental contents are kept from consciousness by active forces. Repression and resistance therefore imply conflict, not just absence of awareness. *The Ego and the Id* (1923) complicates the early conscious/unconscious opposition further because parts of the ego and its defensive work are themselves unconscious.

The practical implication is modest but important. The useful question is not "What secret truth is hidden?" but "What processes may be shaping this experience without being fully represented in awareness?" Psychoanalytic inference starts from effects, not direct observation of an unconscious mechanism.

### Conflict, repression and compromise

Freud's model is fundamentally conflictual. Wishes, prohibitions, fears, identifications and demands of reality may oppose one another. Repression is one response, but what appears in consciousness or behaviour is rarely a pure expression of one side. Symptoms and other formations can function as **compromises**, simultaneously expressing and defending against conflicting tendencies.

This is one reason the corpus avoids one-to-one decoding. A behaviour can serve several functions at once, and an ordinary situational explanation may still be relevant.

### Drives, pleasure and reality

Freud gives bodily rooted drives a primary motivational role. The pleasure principle concerns the management of unpleasure and satisfaction; the reality principle modifies immediate satisfaction in relation to external conditions. Mitchell (1988) stresses that drive theory is not an optional metaphor attached to otherwise neutral observations. It organises Freud's explanatory hierarchy.

That matters for later comparison. In Freud's classical framework, objects and relationships are understood within a drive-based theory of motivation. Fairbairn, Bowlby, Kohut and relational theorists alter what counts as primary rather than merely adding new vocabulary. A conflict understood as drive versus prohibition is therefore not conceptually identical to a conflict among relational configurations or attachment expectations.

### Repetition

*Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920) begins from phenomena that strain a simple pleasure-seeking account: traumatic dreams, repeated painful patterns, transferential re-enactment and repeated play. Freud asks whether a compulsion to repeat may be more fundamental than the pleasure principle.

The corpus preserves a crucial distinction. **Painful repetition is an observation and clinical problem; the death instinct is a speculative metapsychological explanation.** Freud marks the latter development as conjectural. Later schools retain the problem of repetition while explaining it through different theories of conflict, object relations, attachment, familiarity or relational organisation.

### Transference

Freud's technical writings make transference central to analytic work. Earlier wishes, affects and expectations become organised around the analyst, but the result is not a photographic replay of the past. Transference can be intensified by resistance and shaped by the immediate analytic situation.

Later traditions widen the concept. Object-relations theorists attend to internal object relations as they emerge in treatment; relational theorists emphasise the analyst's participation. These are later developments, not concepts that should be silently back-read into Freud. In ordinary relationship reflection, transference should remain a hypothesis about added historical meaning, not a way to dismiss present reality.

### Narcissism and identification

Freud's "On Narcissism" (1914) opens major questions about investment in the self, self-regard and the ego ideal. In *The Ego and the Id*, identification becomes central to the formation of ego and superego, so important relationships can leave enduring structural residues within personality.

This provides a bridge toward later object-relations and self theories but does not make Freud a mature relational theorist. Mitchell's historical argument is that Freud's clinical material becomes increasingly saturated with relationships and identifications while drive regulation remains the dominant metapsychological frame.

### Ego, id, superego, anxiety and guilt

The structural model distinguishes id, ego and superego as organisations or agencies, not cartoon personalities. The ego mediates among instinctual demands, external reality and moral pressures while also using unconscious defences. The superego carries ideals and prohibitions and may become harsh or punitive; guilt can therefore arise even without conscious wrongdoing.

Freud's theory of anxiety also changes. In the later model, anxiety becomes a signal of danger that can mobilise defence. Klein, Winnicott and later theorists give different accounts of guilt, concern and developmental anxiety. Those should remain later revisions rather than being folded back into Freud.

### What endures and what remains contested

The dynamic unconscious, repression, resistance, conflict, ambivalence, identification, transference and repetition remain organising problems across many psychoanalytic traditions. The biological and economic details of drive theory, especially the death instinct as an explanation of repetition, are much more contested. The rest of the corpus preserves this difference in status rather than treating all elements of Freud's metapsychology as equally established.

## From ego and defence to post-Freudian pluralism

One major post-Freudian development was a shift of attention from the repressed content to the operations of the ego itself. Anna Freud's *The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence* (1936) makes this shift explicit. She describes the ego as warding off unpleasure and anxiety and regulating impulses and affects. She also treats defensive operations as appropriate objects of psychoanalytic observation in their own right.

This changes the question. Classical interpretation may ask, "What forbidden wish has been repressed?" Ego psychology also asks, "How does the person keep this wish, fear or affect out of awareness, and what does that defensive organisation permit or prevent?"

Anna Freud examines defences against instinctual impulses, affects and external danger, as well as their developmental timing, motives, successes and costs. Her examples also show that a defence can protect functioning and impair it. Restriction can reduce anxiety, for example, but if it becomes rigid it can narrow interests and impoverish development. Defence is therefore not synonymous with pathology.

McWilliams (2011) preserves this functional approach in contemporary language. She describes personality organisation dimensionally and distinguishes characteristic defensive styles without treating them as moral defects. Her account is particularly useful for non-clinical reflection because it invites the question "What function does this operation serve?" before "What category does this person belong to?"

Post-Freudian pluralism expands from this point in several directions. Some ego psychologists increasingly study adaptation, developmental capacities and the construction of psychic structure. British object-relations theorists relocate motivation toward relationships and internal objects. Winnicott moves environmental provision and dependence closer to the centre. Kohut makes self-cohesion and selfobject experience fundamental. Interpersonal and relational analysts place actual transactions and reciprocal influence inside the theory rather than outside it. Attachment theory reconstructs motivation around protection, proximity and secure-base functioning. Mentalization theory links attachment, affect regulation and the capacity to represent minds.

Mitchell and Black (1995/2016) describe this diversification as a central fact of modern psychoanalysis. Mitchell (1988) warns against pretending that all these developments are simply additions to one unchanged Freudian core. Some are extensions. Some change the unit of explanation. Some are incompatible at the level of metapsychology.

For the rest of this corpus, ego and defence should therefore be treated as one important branch, not as the final common language into which every later school can be translated.

## Object relations: a family of theories, not one doctrine

"Object" in psychoanalytic language usually means a psychologically significant other - or, in some theories, a part or representation of that other - rather than an inanimate thing. British object-relations traditions make relations with others increasingly central, but **object relations is a family of theories, not one doctrine**. Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott and Bion differ over motivation, aggression, phantasy, environmental provision and what development requires. File `04_OBJECT_RELATIONS_AND_THE_INTERNAL_WORLD.md` develops those differences in full.

### Klein: phantasy, anxiety and the internal object world

Klein gives primitive anxiety, aggression and unconscious phantasy a major organising role. In her model, good and bad experiences of the object are initially difficult to hold together. **Splitting** protects the good from destructive feelings but also fragments experience; projection and introjection continually organise relations between internal and external worlds.

**Projective identification** begins in Klein as a specific phantasy in which parts of the self are split off and projected into the object, affecting how the object is experienced and related to. Later Bionian, relational and couple uses give more weight to communication, interpersonal pressure and enacted response. The original and later meanings should not be collapsed.

Klein's paranoid-schizoid and depressive **positions** are recurring modes of organisation, not diagnoses or fixed stages left permanently behind. Greater depressive-position functioning involves more capacity to hold love and hate toward the same whole object, tolerate ambivalence and experience guilt or concern. **Reparation** concerns preserving or restoring the valued object in the face of awareness of one's own destructive feelings. Klein's accounts of very early infancy remain psychoanalytic reconstructions rather than direct observational reports of infant subjectivity.

### Fairbairn: object-seeking and preserved ties

Fairbairn changes the motivational premise. Libido is fundamentally **object-seeking**, not primarily pleasure-seeking. When essential relationships are frustrating or rejecting, the person may internalise those relationships rather than relinquish them. Splits in the ego become organised around exciting and rejecting objects and the hopes, anger and dependence attached to them.

The important applied paradox is that a painful internal relationship can be preserved because it remains a relationship. This provides one account of repetition that differs from both Freud's drive theory and Klein's emphasis on aggression and phantasy. It does not mean a person wants suffering.

### Winnicott: dependence and environmental provision

Winnicott shifts much more explanatory weight toward actual dependence and the quality of environmental provision. **Holding** refers to the reliable physical and psychological environment that supports continuity of being and ego integration. Repeated impingement can organise reactive compliance rather than spontaneous experience.

His **true/false self** distinction describes a defensive organisation in which compliance may protect a less accessible source of spontaneity. It is not a moral contrast between authenticity and fakery. The **capacity to be alone** is another paradox: it develops through being alone in the presence of a sufficiently reliable other, so autonomy emerges through dependence rather than by abolishing it.

Winnicott's transitional phenomena and **potential space** describe an intermediate area that is neither simply internal fantasy nor external fact. Play, creativity and cultural experience develop in this area. His later idea of the **use of an object** also gives weight to recognising an object as existing outside omnipotent control.

### Bion: thinking and containment

Bion asks how emotional experience becomes thinkable. **Alpha function** transforms otherwise unprocessed elements of experience into forms that can enter dreaming, memory and thought. When this transformation fails, experience remains closer to what he calls beta-elements and may be evacuated or acted upon rather than represented.

Frustration is central because thinking develops partly through tolerating the absence of immediate satisfaction. **Reverie** describes a receptive relation to emotional communication, and **container-contained** models how unprocessed experience can be received, transformed and returned in a more thinkable form.

Bionian **containment** is therefore not a synonym for Winnicottian **holding**. Holding concerns environmental provision, dependence and continuity; containment concerns the transformation of emotional experience into something that can be mentally processed. The concepts may overlap in clinical situations while doing different theoretical work.

### Why the differences matter

Klein asks how love, aggression and anxiety are organised through phantasy and splitting. Fairbairn asks what relationship is being preserved. Winnicott asks what environmental conditions support spontaneous development. Bion asks whether emotional experience can become thought. Using "object relations" as a generic synonym for "childhood relationships create patterns" would erase the distinctions the corpus is designed to preserve.

## Self psychology and competing theories of narcissism

"Narcissism" is one of the most misleading terms in ordinary psychological language. In this corpus it is theory-dependent, not a generic synonym for vanity, selfishness or difficult relationship behaviour. The clearest orientation is to keep Kohut and Kernberg separate. File `05_SELF_NARCISSISM_RECOGNITION_AND_RELATIONALITY.md` provides the fuller comparison.

### Kohut: self-cohesion and selfobject experience

Kohut's *The Restoration of the Self* (1977) makes the cohesion, vitality and continuity of the self a primary explanatory problem. A **selfobject** is not simply another person who is supportive or admired. It refers to the experienced function of another in maintaining or developing self-organisation.

Kohut gives particular weight to **mirroring** and **idealising** selfobject experiences. Mirroring concerns responsive recognition that supports vitality and worth; idealising experience involves connection to a calm, valued or powerful other whose functions can gradually be internalised. Development is therefore not permanent dependence on perfect responsiveness but increasing internal structure and capacity to survive ordinary frustration.

When this organisation is fragile, criticism, disappointment or failed responsiveness can threaten self-esteem or self-cohesion with unusual force. Kohut's framework asks whether a seemingly small event became a broader threat to the continuity or worth of the self. That is different from merely saying someone was hurt or wanted praise.

### Kernberg: conflict, aggression and integration

Kernberg also theorises narcissistic pathology, but within a conflict and object-relations framework that gives much greater weight to aggression, splitting, envy and the integration of self and object representations. Mature functioning requires enough integration for love and anger, idealisation and disappointment, dependence and separateness to coexist without the person or partner becoming entirely good or bad.

In less integrated organisation, idealisation and devaluation may alternate sharply. Dependency can be especially difficult because needing another person's goodness also exposes the self to frustration, envy and aggression. Kernberg's account of love therefore keeps sexuality, aggression and ambivalence centrally in view.

### Why Kohut and Kernberg must not be blended

Both theorists address narcissistic suffering, but their explanatory assumptions differ. Kohut foregrounds self-cohesion, selfobject provision and developmental vulnerability. Kernberg foregrounds conflict, aggression, splitting and integration of internalised self-object relations.

A formulation should therefore avoid combining Kohutian mirroring failure or narcissistic injury with Kernbergian primitive aggression and devaluation as if they formed one seamless theory. Better practice is to generate rival hypotheses: one asks whether the event threatened self-cohesion or a needed selfobject relation; another asks whether dependency, aggression and splitting are organising the response. The disagreement is analytically useful rather than something to be smoothed over.

## The relational and intersubjective turn

Relational psychoanalysis is a later reconstruction, not a hidden version of Freud. Mitchell and Black (1995/2016) map the growing plurality of psychoanalytic traditions, while Mitchell (1988) attempts a selective integration around the **relational matrix**. File `05_SELF_NARCISSISM_RECOGNITION_AND_RELATIONALITY.md` develops this shift in full.

The relational matrix includes actual interactions, internalised relational configurations, fantasy and expectation, ways of experiencing the self with particular others, and reciprocal influence in the present. It is therefore neither a synonym for "relationships matter" nor a reduction of psychology to observable behaviour. Mitchell resists the either/or split between intrapsychic and interpersonal life: internal worlds shape transactions, and transactions can reinforce or transform internal organisation.

This changes the account of repetition. A person may participate in recreating a familiar relational configuration because it preserves continuity, identity or attachment, even when the pattern is constraining. The person is neither a passive prisoner of childhood nor fully free of history. Present partners also respond from their own histories, motives and circumstances, so the pattern is genuinely interactive rather than simply projected onto an inert other.

### Enactment and reciprocal influence

**Enactment** is later relational language for a pattern becoming lived between people rather than only described or consciously remembered. It should not be treated as a classical synonym for transference. Nor does enactment mean that one person literally inserts a mental state into another.

A more disciplined formulation tracks sequence. One person's withdrawal may intensify another's pursuit; pursuit may increase the pressure to withdraw; the resulting withdrawal may then be experienced as proof of unavailability. The interaction can become self-confirming even though each participant retains an independent mind and neither consciously intended the result.

This makes causal humility essential. Two-person psychology does not mean that both people are equally responsible. Power, coercion, deception and harm may be asymmetrical. Reciprocal influence is a psychological description, not an ethical equaliser. The applied couple consequences belong mainly in `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md`.

### Benjamin: recognition and the other as subject

Jessica Benjamin's *The Bonds of Love* (1988) adds an explicitly intersubjective question: how can I depend on another person, need their recognition and still experience them as a separate subject whose mind and desire are not under my control?

**Recognition** is more than approval, agreement or validation. It involves acknowledging another centre of subjectivity. Mutual recognition therefore requires a tension between self-assertion and responsiveness, independence and dependence, influence and separateness.

Benjamin describes **complementarity** as a breakdown of that tension into paired positions such as doer/done-to or controller/controlled. The positions can reverse without creating mutuality. For couple reflection, the key questions are whether I can seek to be understood while remaining curious about the other's separate experience, and whether disagreement turns difference into domination or defect in my mind.

Benjamin's concepts remain later intersubjective developments. They should not be attributed retrospectively to Freud, Klein or Winnicott simply because those theorists also wrote about relationships.

## Attachment: continuity and revision

Bowlby's attachment theory emerged from psychoanalytic clinical concerns but constitutes a substantial theoretical revision. It should neither be separated entirely from psychoanalysis nor absorbed into it as "modern Freud". In *A Secure Base* (1988), attachment is a distinct biologically rooted behavioural/motivational system organised around protection through the accessibility of an attachment figure.

Attachment behaviour becomes especially salient under pain, fatigue, fear and perceived inaccessibility. The **secure base** captures the central paradox: security does not require constant proximity; sufficiently reliable availability supports exploration, separation and return. This complicates any simple equation of dependence with immaturity.

Bowlby also describes **working models** of self and attachment figures. Repeated experience organises expectations about whether others are available and responsive and about the self in relation to care. These enduring expectations create an obvious bridge to psychoanalytic ideas about internal relationships, but the theories are not identical. A Bowlbian working model is not simply another name for a Kleinian internal object or a Fairbairnian endopsychic structure, and attachment need is not the same as Kohutian selfobject need.

For adult reflection, attachment questions are concrete: what happens when an important person becomes unavailable, or seems unavailable? Do I intensify bids for contact, detach from the need, or oscillate? Can I explore and disagree while retaining confidence that the relationship remains available? These remain context-sensitive hypotheses. The other person's actual reliability, ordinary stress and situational explanations must remain in view.

## Mentalization: development, reflective function and epistemic humility

Fonagy, Gergely, Jurist and Target's *Affect Regulation, Mentalization, and the Development of the Self* (2002) provides an important bridge among psychoanalytic thinking, attachment research and developmental psychology. It also supplies one of the strongest safeguards against interpretive overconfidence in this corpus.

**Mentalization** is the capacity to understand behaviour in terms of mental states in oneself and others while recognising that those states are not directly visible. **Reflective function** is their operationalised term for the psychological processes supporting this capacity. Mentalization therefore involves distinctions between inner and outer reality, one's own mind and another person's mind, and representation and fact.

The authors connect mentalization to **affect regulation**. A feeling can be represented as a mental state, placed in context and linked with intentions or beliefs rather than treated automatically as proof about the world. Under emotional pressure this capacity can narrow: certainty can increase precisely when curiosity and alternative perspectives would be most useful.

A central developmental point is the relative **opacity of mental states**. Another person's intention must be inferred, and one's own motives are not transparently available either. This gives the applied corpus a governing rule:

> A plausible account of another person's mental state remains a hypothesis.

The same restraint applies to self-analysis. Articulate introspection can still be wrong, defensive or incomplete. Conversely, uncertainty is not failure. The capacity to say "this is my current hypothesis, but I may be missing something" can represent greater reflective functioning than an elegant but closed explanation.

Mentalization therefore does not replace psychoanalytic interpretation. It constrains it. Interpretation asks what a behaviour might mean in a wider dynamic pattern; mentalization asks whether that meaning is being held as a representation rather than confused with the mind itself. The operational rule for later files is to formulate clearly enough to generate useful questions, but lightly enough that new evidence, the other person's account or an ordinary situational explanation can revise the formulation.

## The major theoretical fault lines

The traditions in this corpus overlap enough to tempt synthesis but differ enough that careless synthesis becomes misleading. The following fault lines should remain visible whenever later files are used for self-reflection or relationship reflection.

## Historical and conceptual lineage map

The history is branching, not linear. Later theorists often preserve some Freudian concepts while rejecting others. Some contemporaries diverge sharply from each other. Attachment and mentalization are bridges into developmental and empirical traditions, not simply later stations on one psychoanalytic track.

| Fairbairnian object relations | Fairbairn | Internalised self-object relationships | Libido is object-seeking | What relationship is being preserved even through pain? | Object-seeking, internal bad objects, exciting/rejecting objects, ego splitting | Offers a relational explanation of painful attachment and repetition | Risks being blurred into other object-relations theories despite distinct motivation | `04`, `06` |
| Winnicottian development | Winnicott | Developing self-in-environment | Maturation depends on sufficiently facilitating provision under conditions of dependence | Was there enough holding and room for spontaneous being? | Holding, facilitating environment, dependence, true/false self, capacity to be alone, transitional phenomena, play, concern, use of object | Makes actual provision, dependence, creativity and potential space central | Can be misused as a simple "good parent/bad parent" theory or merged with Bion | `04`, `05`, `07` |
| Bionian development of Klein | Bion | Transformation of emotional experience into thinkable form | Thinking develops through tolerating frustration and transforming emotional experience | Can this experience be contained and thought rather than evacuated? | Alpha-function, beta-elements, reverie, container-contained, frustration, K | Explains the development and breakdown of thinking about emotion | Vocabulary is abstract and easily turned into vague metaphor | `04`, `07` |
| Self psychology | Kohut | Cohesion and organisation of the self | Selfobject experience supports a cohesive, vital self | What threat to self-cohesion or self-esteem is being experienced? | Selfobject, mirroring, idealisation, self-cohesion, fragmentation, narcissistic vulnerability | Reframes narcissistic suffering around development of the self rather than drive conflict alone | Can be incorrectly fused with Kernberg or turned into a theory that all frustration is empathic failure | `05`, `07` |
| Kernbergian object relations | Kernberg | Integrated or split self-object representations within conflict | Love and aggression must be integrated within object relations | How are aggression, dependency, idealisation and devaluation organised? | Splitting, idealisation/devaluation, envy, aggression, object-relational organisation, superego | Links conflict, aggression and object relations; useful for love and couple dynamics | Not interchangeable with Kohut's deficit/self-cohesion model | `05`, `06` |
| Interpersonal and relational | Sullivan; Mitchell as organising source | Relational patterns between self and others, internal and actual | Relatedness is constitutive rather than secondary | What relational configuration is being recreated here and now? | Interpersonal field, relational matrix, reciprocal influence, enactment | Places present interaction and co-created patterns inside the theory | Can be diluted into vague "everything is relational" language | `05`, `06`, `07` |
| Intersubjective recognition | Benjamin | Relation between two subjects | Human beings seek recognition while remaining dependent on an other outside their control | Can I experience the other as a separate subject, not only as a role in my drama? | Recognition, mutuality, complementarity, domination, doer/done-to | Clarifies power, agency and the breakdown and renewal of mutual recognition | Must not be retrofitted into classical Freud or Klein as if already present | `05`, `06`, `07` |
| Attachment | Bowlby | Attachment system and working models of self/other | Protection and proximity to attachment figures are primary | Is the attachment figure available enough for security and exploration? | Secure base, attachment behaviour, separation/loss, working models | Provides a distinct motivational and developmental system linked to observation and research | Not identical to object relations; style labels can become reductive | `05`, `06`, `07` |
| Mentalization | Fonagy, Gergely, Jurist, Target | Capacity to represent behaviour in terms of mental states | Self-organisation and affect regulation depend partly on developing reflective function in relationships | Can I represent my own and the other's mind while remembering that minds are opaque? | Mentalization, reflective function, psychic equivalence, affect regulation | Adds an epistemic safeguard and developmental bridge | Can be mistaken for mere introspection or certainty about motives | `05`, `07` |

## Terminology and translation guide

Psychoanalytic terms often migrate across schools and into ordinary speech. The same word can therefore conceal different theories. This guide is intentionally compact. Its purpose is to flag where translation is dangerous.

| Term | Working meaning in this corpus | School-specific shifts and common traps |
|---|---|---|
| **Unconscious** | Mental processes active outside awareness. | In Freud, the important sense is dynamic: repression and resistance keep material out of consciousness, while parts of the ego are also unconscious. Later schools broaden what is unconscious to include relational configurations, unmentalized affect and implicit expectations. "Subconscious" is not used as a precise synonym. |
| **Drive** | A motivational pressure rooted in Freud's metapsychology, classically linked to bodily sources, aims and objects. | Do not translate drive as simply "need" or "emotion". Fairbairn rejects pleasure-seeking as primary; Bowlby separates attachment from drive theory; Mitchell relocates motivation within a relational matrix. |
| **Object** | A person, part-person or representation toward which feeling and motivation are directed. | "Object" does not mean that the person is treated as a thing. The concept changes from Freud's object of a drive to the internal-object worlds of Klein and Fairbairn and later object-relational models. |
| **Internal object** | A psychologically organised representation or relational configuration involving an important other. | It is not a photographic memory of the actual person. Klein's internal objects, Fairbairn's exciting/rejecting objects and Kernberg's internalised object relations are related but not identical concepts. |
| **Defence** | A mental operation that manages anxiety, affect, conflict or perceived danger. | Anna Freud systematises ego defences. McWilliams treats them dimensionally and functionally. A defence is not automatically pathological, deliberate or dishonest. |
| **Repression** | A dynamic process that keeps certain mental contents or tendencies from conscious access. | Repression is one defence, not a generic word for all avoidance. Klein's splitting and projection, for example, are not simply forms of repression. |
| **Projection** | Attributing or locating an aspect of one's own experience in an external object. | The term can be used loosely in ordinary speech. In psychoanalytic formulation it should specify what is hypothesised to be projected and why. It is not established merely because an accusation seems inaccurate. |
| **Projective identification** | In Klein, projection of split-off aspects of the self into an object in a way that reorganises the subject's relation to that object. | Later Kleinian and couple traditions give it more explicitly interpersonal meanings. Do not automatically equate Klein's 1946 concept with a later two-person enactment or claim that one person literally causes another to feel a projected state. |
| **Paranoid-schizoid position** | Kleinian organisation marked by splitting, persecutory anxiety, partial objects, idealisation and projection. | It is a "position", not a diagnosis and not simply a chronological infant stage. Ordinary temporary splitting under stress does not establish severe pathology. |
| **Depressive position** | Kleinian organisation in which loved and hated aspects of the object become more integrated, bringing ambivalence, guilt, mourning and concern for damage to the loved object. | "Depressive" here does not mean clinical depression. It names a developmental and recurring psychic achievement. |
| **Reparation** | In Klein, impulses and efforts to restore or preserve the loved object after recognising one's destructive feelings toward it. | It is not simply apologising. Winnicott also discusses opportunities for reparation in the development of concern, but within his different environment-centred framework. |
| **Holding** | Winnicott's term for reliable environmental provision supporting continuity of being under conditions of dependence. | Do not use as a synonym for emotional comfort in general, and do not equate it with Bionian containment. |
| **Containment** | In Bion, the reception and transformation of emotional experience so that it can become more thinkable. | The popular phrase "hold space" can obscure Bion's specific model of alpha-function, reverie and transformation. |
| **True/false self** | Winnicott's distinction between spontaneous continuity of being and compliant organisation around environmental demands. | False self is not simply social politeness or "inauthenticity". Winnicott includes normal social forms and sees false-self organisation as potentially protective. |
| **Selfobject** | Kohut's term for another person as experienced in a function that supports self-cohesion and development. | It does not mean "a person treated as an object". Mirroring and idealising selfobject functions are central examples. |
| **Narcissism** | A family of psychoanalytic problems concerning self-investment, self-esteem, self-cohesion and relations between self and others. | Freud, Kohut and Kernberg organise narcissism differently. In this corpus it is never shorthand for "selfish person" and should not be used as a lay diagnosis. |
| **Attachment** | Bowlby's primary behavioural and motivational system organised around protection, proximity and availability of attachment figures. | Attachment is not a renamed object relation, dependency drive or love in general. Adult behaviour varies with context and activation; fixed style labels can oversimplify. |
| **Mentalization / reflective function** | Capacity to represent behaviour in terms of mental states while distinguishing representation from reality. | Fonagy et al. distinguish reflective function from mere introspection. The concept should increase uncertainty where evidence is limited, not provide a sophisticated way to claim knowledge of another mind. |
| **Recognition** | Benjamin's intersubjective concept of acknowledging the other as a separate centre of subjectivity while sustaining one's own agency. | It is more than praise or validation. Mutual recognition includes tension, difference and the possibility of breakdown and renewal. |
| **Enactment** | Later psychoanalytic term for a relational pattern being lived between participants rather than only remembered or described. | It belongs especially to contemporary relational and couple formulations. Do not back-attribute it to Freud or equate it automatically with Klein's projective identification. |
| **Transference** | Organisation of current experience around expectations, feelings and relational prototypes shaped by earlier relationships and conflicts. | Freud made transference central but retained a more one-person technical frame. Later object-relational and relational traditions widen the concept to include internal object relations and mutual participation. |
| **Countertransference** | The analyst's emotional and psychological responses in relation to the patient. | Historically it moved from being treated mainly as interference to being used by later analysts as potential information about the relational field. It remains data requiring reflection, not automatic evidence about the patient. |

A useful rule is to keep the theorist attached to the term until its meaning is clear: **Kleinian projective identification**, **Winnicottian holding**, **Bionian containment**, **Kohutian selfobject**, **Bowlbian attachment**, **Benjaminian recognition**. Removing the attribution too early makes unlike concepts sound interchangeable.

## A disciplined method for moving from theory to reflection

The corpus uses psychoanalytic theories as hypothesis-generating tools. The practical sequence is:

**Observed event  
→ subjective experience  
→ meaning/fantasy  
→ psychodynamic hypothesis  
→ relational outcome**

Each transition adds inference.

### 1. Observed event

Describe what could in principle be captured by a neutral recording or agreed factual account. Keep it narrow.

- "I asked a question and my partner did not answer for about a minute."
- "After I criticised how the booking was handled, my partner left the room."
- "We had sex once this month after previously having sex more often."

Even observation is selective, but it is still useful to separate it from what the event meant.

### 2. Subjective experience

Name what happened in you: affect, body state, urge, image or shift in attention.

- "I felt suddenly unimportant and became angry."
- "I felt ashamed and wanted to disappear."
- "I became anxious and started scanning for evidence that something was wrong."

This is first-person data. It is not yet an explanation of the other person.

### 3. Meaning or fantasy

Ask what the event came to mean in the moment.

- "If I mattered, they would have anticipated this."
- "Criticism means I have failed and will be rejected."
- "If desire has fallen, I am no longer attractive or wanted."

Here "fantasy" is used broadly for an organising scenario or expectation unless a school-specific term such as Kleinian phantasy is explicitly intended.

### 4. Psychodynamic hypothesis

Only now introduce theory. Several hypotheses should normally compete.

A Freudian hypothesis might ask whether a conflict, wish or guilt is being defended against. An ego-psychology hypothesis might identify the function of withdrawal or intellectualisation. A Fairbairnian hypothesis might ask what familiar relationship is being preserved. A Winnicottian hypothesis might ask whether closeness feels like intrusion or whether compliance has displaced spontaneity. A Kohutian hypothesis might ask whether self-cohesion has been threatened. An attachment hypothesis might ask whether availability is in doubt. A relational hypothesis might ask how each person's action is shaping the other's. A mentalization hypothesis might ask where inference has hardened into certainty.

A hypothesis should make the event more thinkable, not merely more dramatic.

### 5. Relational outcome

Finally ask what the formulation predicts about the pattern that follows. A useful psychodynamic idea should help explain a sequence, not merely attach a label to an individual.

For couple reflection, the fuller loop is:

**Event  
→ perception  
→ affect  
→ impulse  
→ defence  
→ fantasy/expectation  
→ behaviour  
→ partner response  
→ my interpretation of that response  
→ reinforcement or revision of the original expectation**

Suppose a partner arrives home distracted. I perceive distance, feel anxiety, experience an impulse to obtain reassurance, defend against vulnerability by criticising, expect that a caring partner should immediately recognise my need, and speak sharply. The partner experiences criticism and withdraws. I interpret withdrawal as proof that I do not matter. The original expectation is strengthened.

This loop does not establish what the partner unconsciously intended. It identifies how one person's internal process can participate in creating the evidence that seems to confirm it.

Several safeguards follow.

- **Observation is not interpretation.** "They went quiet" is different from "They withdrew to punish me."
- **Interpretation is not proof.** A coherent account may still be wrong.
- **Developmental reconstruction is not memory recovery.** A present pattern may suggest a developmental question without demonstrating that a particular childhood event occurred.
- **A useful theory may generate a productive question without being literally true.** "What if I experience disagreement as abandonment?" can be useful even if no single attachment explanation accounts for the event.
- **Situational explanations remain live competitors.** Fatigue, time pressure, illness, justified anger, incompatible preferences, work stress, sexual timing, distraction and simple misunderstanding can be sufficient explanations.
- **Self-analysis should precede analysis of the absent partner.** One has more direct access to one's own affect, impulses and meanings than to another person's internal world.
- **Multiple rival hypotheses are usually preferable to one totalising explanation.** Competing formulations reduce confirmation bias and reveal what evidence would actually matter.

The method is therefore disciplined curiosity rather than interpretive certainty.

## Worked comparative examples

The examples below are invented. They are deliberately ordinary and non-diagnostic. Their purpose is to show how different theories change the question.

### Example 1: criticism followed by withdrawal

**Observed event**

One partner says, "You always leave the planning to me. I am tired of carrying everything." The other says, "Fine," becomes quiet and leaves the room for twenty minutes.

**Subjective meaning for the first partner**

"I am alone in this relationship. I have to force things into the open before anything happens."

**Subjective meaning for the second partner**

"Nothing I do is good enough. If I stay here I will be humiliated or say something worse."

Neither meaning is contained in the observed event. Both are possible first-person interpretations.

**Competing formulations**

1. **Freud/classical conflict:** Withdrawal may function as a compromise between anger and prohibition against expressing anger. Leaving reduces the danger of open aggression while still communicating displeasure. The critic's attack may likewise express both a wish for help and hostility toward dependence.
2. **Ego/defence:** The second partner may use withdrawal or isolation to reduce affect intensity. The first may use criticism to defend against a more vulnerable request. The question is what each defence protects and what it costs.
3. **Klein:** The exchange may temporarily organise each partner into a bad-object position. The critic experiences the other as neglectful; the withdrawing partner experiences the critic as persecutory. Splitting may narrow access to the loved aspects of the same person. A more integrated depressive-position response would allow irritation and continued concern to coexist.
4. **Fairbairn:** The pair may be preserving a familiar bond organised around demanding and rejecting roles. Painful as it is, the pattern may provide a recognisable form of connection.
5. **Winnicott:** Withdrawal may protect a private core from perceived impingement. Alternatively, chronic compliance before the argument may have hidden resentment until spontaneous response becomes possible only through retreat.
6. **Bion:** Affect may have exceeded the couple's capacity to think together. Criticism is returned as silence, silence as further accusation, and emotional experience is circulated without being transformed.
7. **Kohut:** Criticism may be experienced by the withdrawing partner as a global narcissistic injury rather than a specific complaint about planning. The first partner may experience non-response as a failure of recognition that destabilises self-worth.
8. **Kernberg:** The exchange may involve a rapid shift toward devaluation and complementary roles, with aggression making it difficult to hold a more integrated image of the partner.
9. **Relational/Mitchell:** The high-value question is the loop. Pursuit through criticism makes withdrawal more likely; withdrawal makes stronger pursuit more likely. Each person's protective move recruits the response they fear.
10. **Benjamin:** Both may move from mutual recognition into complementarity: one becomes doer/accuser, the other done-to/withdrawer. Reversing the roles later would not by itself restore mutuality.
11. **Attachment:** Criticism may be an intensified proximity bid under perceived unavailability; withdrawal may be a deactivating response under relational threat. Whether that formulation fits depends partly on what happens under stress across situations.
12. **Mentalization:** Both partners may temporarily lose reflective distance. "You do not care" and "I can never do anything right" become experienced as facts rather than state-dependent interpretations.

**Ordinary situational alternatives**

The second partner may simply be exhausted and know that twenty minutes of cooling down prevents escalation. The first partner may in fact be carrying an unfair share of planning. The complaint may be substantially accurate. A psychodynamic formulation does not cancel the workload problem.

**What remains unknown**

We do not know whether the withdrawal is punitive, regulatory, fearful, contemptuous or merely practical. We do not know the history of similar exchanges, the distribution of planning work, or what either person intended.

**Reflective questions**

- What did I feel immediately before my criticism or withdrawal?
- What did I imagine the other person's action said about me?
- What was I trying to make happen, and what response did my method actually evoke?
- Could I state the practical issue without treating my interpretation of motive as fact?
- Which alternative formulation best explains the repeated sequence, not just this episode?

### Example 2: disappointment when a need was not anticipated

**Observed event**

A person has had a difficult day but does not explicitly ask for comfort. Their partner makes dinner, talks about their own work and does not ask what is wrong. The first person later says, "You should have known I needed you."

**Subjective meaning**

"If I have to ask, the care is not real. Being loved means being known without explaining myself."

**Competing formulations**

1. **Freud/classical:** The wish to be cared for may coexist with conflict over dependence. Requiring anticipation allows dependency to be gratified without openly acknowledging the wish.
2. **Defence:** Indirectness may defend against the shame or risk of asking and being refused. Anger then protects against the more vulnerable feeling of wanting.
3. **Klein:** Failure to anticipate the need may rapidly transform a good object into a disappointing or bad object. The reflective task is to recover the whole person who both cared in some ways and failed in another.
4. **Fairbairn:** Longing for an unavailable or inconsistently responsive other may itself be a familiar mode of connection. Explicitly requesting care could threaten the established relationship pattern by testing whether a different form of connection is possible.
5. **Winnicott:** The expectation may contain a wish for an environment that adapts before the need has to be fully formulated. In adult intimacy, however, mature dependence also requires recognition of separateness; the partner is no longer an adapting maternal environment.
6. **Kohut:** The failure of attunement may be experienced as a selfobject failure, producing a disproportionate collapse in feeling recognised or held together. That is different from saying the partner objectively should have known.
7. **Benjamin:** The demand to be known without communicating may inadvertently deny the partner's separateness as another subject with a different mind. The challenge is to seek recognition without requiring mind-reading.
8. **Attachment:** A difficult day may activate a legitimate wish for proximity and care. If asking directly feels dangerous, protest may appear after the need goes unmet.
9. **Relational:** The unspoken test creates a structure in which the partner can easily fail, and the failure then confirms the expectation of not being known. The other partner's own habits, attention and responsiveness also matter.
10. **Mentalization:** The statement "you should have known" collapses the distinction between my internally obvious state and what was externally available to another mind.

**Ordinary situational alternatives**

The partner may have been preoccupied, tired, inexperienced at reading subtle cues, or reasonably assuming that dinner itself was supportive. The first person may also have given no visible sign of distress. None of these possibilities disproves the emotional importance of the missed bid.

**What remains unknown**

We do not know how clear the cues were, whether the partner usually notices distress, whether direct requests are ordinarily welcomed, or whether there is a history of failed responsiveness.

**Reflective questions**

- What would it mean to me to ask directly for comfort?
- What do I fear would happen if I asked and the answer were no?
- Am I testing whether I am loved by setting a condition the other person does not know exists?
- Can I distinguish the legitimacy of my need from the claim that the other should automatically know it?
- What evidence would show that this was a one-off miss rather than a stable pattern of unavailability?

## Limits, cautions and common misuses

Psychoanalytic ideas are unusually generative. That makes them useful, but it also creates predictable errors.

### 1. Turning a theory into a personality label

Terms such as "narcissistic", "projective", "avoidant", "false self" or "paranoid-schizoid" can become shorthand insults. That destroys their explanatory value. In this corpus, the preferred form is functional and situational: "I may be devaluing to protect myself from dependency" rather than "I am a narcissist"; "splitting may be narrowing how I see this person under stress" rather than "I am in a paranoid-schizoid personality state".

### 2. Analysing the absent partner

Psychoanalytic language can create the illusion of privileged access to another person's motives. The more sophisticated the vocabulary, the stronger the illusion can become. The discipline is to begin with one's own experience and the observable interaction. A partner's possible unconscious motive can be considered only as one hypothesis among alternatives.

### 3. Confusing developmental reconstruction with recovered fact

A present expectation of rejection may be consistent with a history of unreliable care. It does not demonstrate a specific childhood event, a particular parental intention or a recovered memory. Klein's own early-development hypotheses were explicitly inferential. Kohut's, Winnicott's and attachment-based developmental formulations also require caution when applied retrospectively to a specific adult.

### 4. Explaining away reality

A person may feel criticised because someone is actually being critical. Attachment alarm can occur because an attachment figure is actually unreliable. Sexual withdrawal may reflect exhaustion, medication, illness, resentment, changing desire, practical overload or relationship conflict. Psychoanalytic depth should not become a way of discounting the surface when the surface is causally sufficient.

### 5. Treating all schools as one model

Object relations is not one doctrine. Holding is not containment. Attachment is not object relations. Recognition is not classical transference. Kohut and Kernberg do not offer interchangeable theories of narcissism. Mitchell's relational model is a later reconstruction, not simply Freud with more emphasis on relationships. Theoretical precision matters because each model implies different causal claims.

### 6. Treating later empirical bridges as proof of earlier metapsychology

Attachment research and mentalization theory can support developmental claims about relationships, regulation and representations. They do not empirically prove Freud's drive theory, Klein's death-instinct assumptions or any other classical metapsychology. Continuity of subject matter is not equivalence of evidence.

### 7. Assuming the deepest explanation is always the best explanation

Sometimes the best explanation is that someone was tired, the request was unclear, the workload was unequal or the couple has incompatible preferences. A psychodynamic explanation is useful when it adds explanatory power, especially across repeated patterns. Depth for its own sake is not rigour.

### 8. Mistaking certainty for insight

Fonagy et al.'s mentalization framework is a direct corrective. Minds are relatively opaque. Good reflection preserves the ability to revise. "I know why you did that" is epistemically stronger than most relationship situations permit.

### 9. Using reciprocity to erase responsibility

Relational models study loops, but a loop does not make all acts equivalent. Abuse, coercion, deception and intimidation cannot be neutralised by saying that both people "co-created" the situation. Reciprocal psychological influence and responsibility for conduct are different questions.

### 10. Converting psychoanalysis into generic self-help

The theories in this corpus are most useful when they make contradictions more thinkable: desire and prohibition, love and hate, dependence and autonomy, recognition and control, repetition and change. They lose value when reduced to slogans such as "communicate better", "set boundaries" or "heal your inner child" without a source-grounded conceptual argument.

## How the remaining corpus files fit together

This file is deliberately broad. The later files should be used for depth rather than repeated orientation.

- **file `02`** develops Freud's changing model of the unconscious, drive, conflict, repression, repetition, narcissism, transference and structural theory. Use it when a formulation depends specifically on Freud rather than on later "psychodynamic" ideas.
- **file `03`** develops ego functions, anxiety, defence, adaptation, superego and guilt. Use it when the key question is how experience is managed rather than what latent content is present.
- **file `04`** separates Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott and Bion in detail. Use it for internal objects, splitting, projective identification, painful object ties, holding, concern, containment and thinking.
- **file `05`** develops Kohut, Kernberg, Mitchell, Benjamin, Bowlby and mentalization where relevant. Use it when the central issues concern self-cohesion, narcissism, recognition, attachment, relational patterns and the other as subject.
- **file `06`** applies the theoretical lineage to adult love, sexuality, dependency, idealisation, aggression, couple unconscious processes and reciprocal patterns. It should not be used as a licence to diagnose a partner.
- **file `07`** supplies the full reflective method, prompts, safeguards and ways to compare hypotheses. It is the operational file for turning theory into disciplined first-person inquiry.

A sensible sequence for a concrete personal question is: begin with `07` for method, follow its router to the relevant substantive file, then return to the observed event and compare at least two plausible formulations. If the question concerns another person's mental state, add the mentalization safeguard from `05` before drawing conclusions.

## Questions this framework helps you ask

The value of the framework lies less in answers than in higher-quality questions. Among the most useful are:

1. What happened that I could describe without interpreting motive?
2. What did I feel, want and fear before I acted?
3. What meaning did the event acquire for me?
4. What defence or protective manoeuvre may have reduced a harder feeling?
5. What did that protection cost me or the relationship?
6. What familiar relational position did I move into: pursuer, withdrawer, critic, appeaser, rescuer, disappointed child, self-sufficient one, admired one, rejected one?
7. What relationship might this pattern preserve even while making me unhappy?
8. Am I splitting a complex person into an all-good or all-bad version because ambivalence is difficult in this moment?
9. Am I trying to repair, control, punish, secure reassurance, obtain recognition or avoid dependence?
10. Did the event threaten a specific wish, or did it threaten my broader sense of cohesion or worth?
11. Is attachment alarm active because the bond feels unavailable, and is that perception supported by current evidence?
12. Am I asking another person to perform a selfobject, holding or containing function, and am I using those terms precisely rather than metaphorically?
13. Can I tolerate the other person's separateness without interpreting difference as rejection or domination?
14. What am I doing that makes the response I fear more likely?
15. What is the other person actually doing that should not be explained away as my projection?
16. Which part of my explanation is observation, which is first-person experience, and which is inference?
17. What ordinary situational explanation could account for the same event?
18. What alternative psychodynamic theory would tell a different but plausible story?
19. What evidence would make me revise my preferred explanation?
20. Can I remain curious about the other person's mind while accepting that I cannot know it directly?

The mature use of psychoanalytic theory is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to make uncertainty more structured, emotionally tolerable and informative.
