# 00_SOURCE_MANIFEST_EVIDENCE_HIERARCHY_AND_ROUTER

## Purpose

This file is the control document for the **Freudian and Post-Freudian Thought Research Corpus**.

The corpus is designed for four linked purposes:

1. structured self-learning in Freudian and post-Freudian psychoanalytic thought;
2. disciplined self-analysis and self-reflection;
3. reflection on recurring patterns in everyday interaction with an intimate partner; and
4. reflection on sexuality, intimacy, dependency, conflict, recognition, repair and mutual influence within a couple.

This is not a diagnostic manual and is not a substitute for psychotherapy, psychiatric assessment or couple therapy. Its practical use is to generate **plausible psychodynamic hypotheses**, not hidden certainties about the self or another person.

The source library contains 28 raw files. Some are alternate editions or duplicate copies of the same work. This router therefore distinguishes **canonical sources**, **alternate/retrieval copies**, **secondary interpretive sources**, **applied sources**, and **reference-only sources**.

# 2. Evidence hierarchy

The corpus must preserve a strict distinction between what a thinker actually wrote, what later interpreters say that thinker meant, and what the corpus itself synthesises.

## Level P1: Direct primary theoretical text

Highest authority for claims about a thinker's own theory.

Examples: Freud on repetition or the structural model; Klein on schizoid mechanisms; Fairbairn on object relations; Winnicott on holding and false self; Bion on learning from experience; Kohut on the self; Mitchell on the relational matrix.

**Rule:** When attributing a concept to an author, use that author's own text where available.

## Level P2: Primary text in an anthology or collected edition

Authoritative for the selected primary text, but editorial framing must not be confused with the author's own claims.

Examples: Freud in *The Freud Reader*; Klein in *Envy and Gratitude and Other Works*; Freud in Standard Edition Volume XIX.

**Rule:** Separate editorial introduction, footnotes and retrospective commentary from the primary text.

## Level S1: Scholarly secondary synthesis or intellectual history

Used to explain lineages, compare schools, clarify terminology and identify disagreements.

Examples: Mitchell and Black's *Freud and Beyond*; Kristeva's *Melanie Klein*; McWilliams's integrative discussions.

**Rule:** A secondary author's interpretation may illuminate Freud, Klein or another theorist, but must not be back-attributed to the original theorist.

## Level A1: Applied clinical or couple-psychotherapy source

Used to translate theory into clinical, relational and couple-level patterns.

Examples: McWilliams, Clulow, Nathans and Schaefer, Kernberg.

**Rule:** Clinical formulations are models for generating hypotheses. They are not observational proof that a particular unconscious mechanism is operating in the user's life or partner.

## Level B1: Bridge source with psychoanalytic and empirical/developmental components

Used when psychoanalysis is explicitly connected with attachment, affect regulation, developmental research or mentalization.

Examples: Bowlby; Fonagy, Gergely, Jurist and Target.

**Rule:** Preserve the distinction between psychoanalytic propositions, developmental models and empirical claims. Do not describe all three as if they carried the same evidentiary status.

## Level R1: Reference, bibliography or source-discovery material

Useful for names, dates, publication history and finding further literature. Not a substantive evidentiary basis for theoretical claims.

Example: Karnac's *Melanie Klein and Beyond: A Bibliography of Primary and Secondary Sources*.

# 3. Mandatory epistemic labels for corpus generation

When a substantive file makes a consequential claim, the drafting process should internally distinguish the following categories even if the final prose is natural rather than mechanically tagged:

- **Primary claim:** directly attributable to the theorist's own text.
- **Secondary interpretation:** a later author's reading or reconstruction.
- **Corpus synthesis:** an integration across sources that no single author explicitly states.
- **Applied hypothesis:** a possible use of the concept in self-reflection or couple reflection.
- **Empirical/developmental bridge:** a proposition partly grounded in attachment, developmental or mentalization research rather than classical psychoanalytic inference alone.
- **Unresolved disagreement:** a point on which the schools materially differ and should not be harmonised.

If the source base does not support a claim, omit it or state that the corpus does not establish it.

# 6. Core terminology governance

## Unconscious

Use broadly only after specifying the model. Freud's dynamic unconscious, Klein's unconscious phantasy, Fairbairn's internal object relations, Bion's transformations and relational accounts of implicit patterns are not interchangeable.

## Drive

For Freud, drive is a foundational metapsychological concept. Later authors alter, reduce, supplement or reject its explanatory priority. Do not use "drive" as a generic synonym for motivation.

## Object

In psychoanalytic language, "object" normally means the target or relational other of a drive, need, affect or relationship, not an inanimate thing. Specify when an author means part-object, whole object, internal object or external person.

## Internal object

An internalised or fantasied relational presence or configuration. It is not simply a conscious memory of another person.

## Defence

A psychological operation serving to manage conflict, anxiety, affect, impulse, self-esteem, relational danger or other intolerable experience. Use Australian/British spelling **defence** in corpus prose except when preserving a source title or direct quotation.

## Repression

Do not use as a catch-all for anything unconscious. In Freud it is a specific defensive process concerning exclusion from consciousness.

## Projection

Distinguish ordinary attribution, defensive projection and Kleinian projective processes. Do not infer projection merely because a perception of another person may be inaccurate.

## Projective identification

Treat first as a Kleinian concept with a specific theoretical history. Later interpersonal and couple uses may place greater emphasis on induced or enacted interpersonal responses. Mark the shift in meaning rather than pretending there is one timeless definition.

## Splitting

Do not equate normal ambivalence, inconsistency or conflict with pathological splitting. In Kleinian theory, splitting has a specific role in primitive organisation of good/bad experience and anxiety.

## Paranoid-schizoid and depressive positions

Use as Kleinian **positions or modes of organisation**, not as diagnostic labels and not as one-off developmental stages permanently left behind.

## Reparation

In Klein and Winnicott, link reparation to ambivalence, guilt/concern and the wish or capacity to restore a valued object or relationship. Do not reduce it to apology or conflict-resolution technique.

## Holding

Use primarily in Winnicott's developmental and clinical sense. In ordinary relationship reflection it may be used metaphorically only when identified as an application rather than a literal claim about infancy.

## Containment / container-contained

Use primarily in Bion's theoretical sense. Do not use "containment" as a vague synonym for being supportive without explaining the transformation of unprocessed emotional experience that the concept is meant to capture.

## True self / false self

Use in Winnicott's sense. Do not turn "false self" into a moral judgement, accusation of fakery, or a fixed identity category.

## Selfobject

Preserve Kohut's specialised term. A selfobject is not simply a supportive person or an object of narcissistic use in ordinary language.

## Narcissism

Always specify the theoretical frame. Freud, Kohut, Kernberg and later relational theorists conceptualise narcissism differently. Avoid using "narcissist" as a casual label for a partner.

## Attachment

Use Bowlby's framework distinctly from object relations and drive theory. Attachment and object relations can illuminate each other but are not synonyms.

## Mentalization / reflective function

Use for the capacity to understand behaviour in terms of mental states while retaining uncertainty about those states. It is a core safeguard against interpretive certainty.

## Recognition

Use Benjamin's relational/intersubjective meaning where relevant: the other must be experienced as a subject with independent agency, not merely as an object of one's needs or projections.

## Enactment

Use for relational patterns becoming lived in interaction rather than merely thought about. Avoid treating every argument or reciprocal reaction as evidence of unconscious enactment.

## Transference

Use first in the psychoanalytic sense of patterns, expectations, wishes and affects becoming organised in relation to the analyst. Applications to intimate relationships should be explicitly marked as extensions or analogies, not identical clinical situations.

## Countertransference

Reserve primarily for the analyst's responses within treatment. In couple reflection, prefer terms such as reciprocal response, induced feeling, relational pull or feedback loop unless a specifically clinical source is being discussed.

# 7. Major theoretical fault lines that must remain visible

The corpus must not produce a smooth story in which every later theory simply adds detail to Freud. Several disagreements concern the basic unit of explanation.

## 7.1 Drive versus relationship

**Freud:** psychic life is fundamentally organised around drives, their satisfactions, transformations, conflicts and defences.

**Fairbairn:** libido is fundamentally object-seeking rather than pleasure-seeking; painful patterns may persist because they preserve ties to important objects.

**Mitchell:** the relational model treats the matrix of self, other and interaction as primary rather than deriving relatedness from endogenous drives.

**Corpus rule:** present this as a genuine metapsychological disagreement, not merely different vocabulary.

## 7.2 Endogenous phantasy/aggression versus environmental provision

**Klein:** gives very great explanatory weight to primitive phantasy, aggression, splitting and projective processes.

**Winnicott:** shifts more explanatory weight toward dependence, environmental provision, holding, impingement and the facilitating environment.

**Corpus rule:** preserve both perspectives. Do not rewrite Klein as if she were Winnicott, or Winnicott as a simple extension of Klein.

## 7.3 Internal object relations versus actual interpersonal field

**Klein/Fairbairn:** emphasise internal objects and internalised relational configurations, though in different ways.

**Sullivan/relational traditions as represented through Mitchell:** emphasise actual transactions and the interactional field.

**Mitchell:** attempts to integrate intrapsychic and interpersonal dimensions within a relational matrix.

**Corpus rule:** when analysing a couple interaction, distinguish what was actually said/done from the internal meanings and relational expectations brought to it.

## 7.4 Conflict model versus developmental-deficit/arrest model

**Classical Freud/Kernberg:** retain central emphasis on conflict, aggression, desire, defence and internal opposition.

**Kohut:** places greater weight on deficits or disruptions in self-cohesion and selfobject experience.

**Mitchell:** critiques simple model-mixing and develops a relational-conflict alternative.

**Corpus rule:** do not assume disproportionate affect is necessarily either hidden conflict or developmental deficit. Generate rival hypotheses.

## 7.5 Kohut versus Kernberg on narcissism

**Kohut:** narcissistic vulnerability is centrally about self-cohesion, selfobject needs, mirroring and developmental failure.

**Kernberg:** gives greater weight to aggression, conflict, splitting, idealisation/devaluation and object-relational organisation.

**Corpus rule:** a self-esteem injury can be formulated through either lens, but the explanatory assumptions are different and should remain visible.

## 7.6 Classical psychoanalysis versus attachment theory

**Freud:** attachment to objects is initially embedded within drive theory.

**Bowlby:** attachment is a primary behavioural/motivational system with its own evolutionary function.

**Corpus rule:** do not call attachment theory simply "modern Freud". It is partly a revision of psychoanalytic motivational assumptions.

## 7.7 One-person versus two-person psychology

**Classical models:** often privilege the individual's intrapsychic conflict and fantasy.

**Relational/intersubjective models:** give greater explanatory weight to mutual influence, co-created meaning and the interactional field.

**Corpus rule:** self-analysis must preserve both. Ask both "What is happening in me?" and "What happens between us that changes what happens in each of us?"

## 7.8 Interpretation versus mentalization

**Strong interpretive psychoanalytic style:** can tempt the observer toward claims about latent meaning.

**Mentalization:** requires recognition that one's own and another person's mental states remain partly opaque.

**Corpus rule:** the practical corpus should prefer a good hypothesis held lightly over an elegant interpretation held with certainty.

# 10. Rules for self-analysis and relationship reflection

The applied corpus must use psychoanalysis as a disciplined way to formulate questions, not as a method for declaring hidden truth.

## 10.1 Separate five layers of an event

For any episode, distinguish:

1. **Observed event:** what was actually said or done.
2. **Subjective experience:** what I felt, perceived and noticed in my body.
3. **Meaning/fantasy:** what I imagined the event meant about me, the other person or the relationship.
4. **Psychodynamic hypothesis:** possible conflict, defence, object relation, attachment expectation, narcissistic injury, projective process or enactment.
5. **Relational outcome:** what I then did, how the other person responded, and how that response fed back into my original expectation.

Never collapse these five layers into one.

## 10.2 Analyse the self before analysing the partner

The corpus may ask:

- What did I experience?
- What did I fear, want or expect?
- What feeling might I have been defending against?
- What role was I implicitly inviting the other person to occupy?
- What did my behaviour make more likely in the other person's response?

It should be much more cautious when asking:

- What unconscious motive did my partner have?
- What childhood event caused my partner to behave this way?
- What personality disorder does my partner have?

The latter questions cannot be answered reliably from one person's account.

## 10.3 Prefer multiple competing hypotheses

For a strong affective reaction, generate at least two or three plausible explanations where the evidence allows it. For example:

- ordinary justified irritation;
- shame or narcissistic injury;
- attachment alarm;
- projection or misattribution;
- repetition of a familiar relational expectation;
- conflict between dependency and autonomy;
- fatigue, stress or situational factors.

A psychoanalytic explanation should not automatically outrank a simpler situational explanation.

## 10.4 Distinguish function from moral judgement

A defence has a function. Withdrawal, intellectualisation, idealisation, minimisation, projection or anger may protect the person from something harder to bear. The analytical question is "What does this operation accomplish?" rather than "What is wrong with me or the other person?"

## 10.5 Use developmental explanations cautiously

The corpus may say that a current pattern is **consistent with**, **reminiscent of**, or **could plausibly repeat** an earlier relational template. It should not state that an adult reaction proves a particular event occurred in childhood.

## 10.6 Preserve mental opacity

Even when an interpretation is compelling, use formulations such as:

- one possible reading is;
- this could function as;
- this pattern is consistent with;
- another explanation would be;
- the available account cannot establish;
- the partner's actual intention remains unknown.

## 10.7 Analyse reciprocal loops

For couple material, use the sequence:

**Event -> perception -> affect -> impulse -> defence -> fantasy/expectation -> behaviour -> partner response -> my interpretation of that response -> reinforcement or revision of the original expectation.**

This helps avoid the simplistic question "Who caused the problem?" and instead examines how a dyadic system reproduces itself.

## 10.8 Do not confuse interpretation with intervention

A plausible psychodynamic explanation does not automatically tell the user what to say or do. When moving from interpretation to action, consider timing, proportionality, consent, the other person's perspective, and whether direct communication is more useful than further interpretation.

# 11. Required structure for substantive corpus files

Every substantive master file should, where relevant, contain the following layers:

### A. Core theory
What the theorist or school actually claims.

### B. Historical development
What problem in earlier theory the new formulation was trying to solve.

### C. Key concepts and mechanisms
Definitions with distinctions between adjacent terms.

### D. Internal tensions and criticisms
Contradictions, ambiguities and major later critiques.

### E. Cross-school comparison
How the same phenomenon would be understood differently by Freud, Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, Kohut, Kernberg, relational theory, attachment theory or mentalization where relevant.

### F. Application to self-reflection
Questions that help examine one's own experience without claiming diagnostic certainty.

### G. Application to couple dynamics
How the framework may illuminate reciprocal patterns, intimacy, sexuality, dependence, conflict and repair.

### H. Limits and misuse
What the framework cannot establish and common ways the concept becomes overextended.

### I. Questions this framework helps ask
A compact list of genuinely psychoanalytic questions, not generic journalling prompts.

# 12. Attribution rules

1. **Never write "psychoanalysis says" when the schools materially disagree.** Name the thinker or tradition.
2. **Never back-read later concepts into Freud without marking the move as retrospective.**
3. **Never attribute attachment theory to Freud.** Bowlby's break with drive derivation matters.
4. **Never equate Klein's projective identification automatically with later couple-therapy enactment.** Explain the development in usage.
5. **Never equate Winnicott's holding automatically with Bion's containment.** They overlap functionally but arise in different theoretical systems.
6. **Never merge Kohut and Kernberg into one theory of narcissism.**
7. **Never describe mentalization as a classical Freudian concept.** It is a later integrative development.
8. **Never present Benjamin's recognition theory as if it were present in classical object relations.**
9. **Never treat McWilliams's personality formulations as primary historical definitions by Freud, Klein or others.**
10. **Never count alternate editions as independent evidence.**

# 14. Corpus-level exclusions and compression rules

To keep the Notebook useful and digestible:

- Do not reproduce long biographical histories unless needed to explain a theoretical break.
- Do not give each theorist a standalone mini-biography.
- Compress detailed psychosexual stage theory unless required for a concept being explained.
- Compress extended case histories unless a brief case illustrates a mechanism better than abstract prose.
- Treat the Oedipus complex as historically and conceptually important, but do not allow it to dominate the applied relationship corpus.
- Avoid long surveys of minor schools not represented strongly in the source library.
- Avoid diagnostic catalogues in `07`; use formulations and mechanisms instead.
- Avoid generic wellness or communication advice that is not anchored in the psychoanalytic/attachment/mentalization source base.
- Do not duplicate the same definition across multiple files. Cross-reference conceptually instead.

# 15. Final corpus quality-control tests

Before the eight-file corpus is frozen, run the following audit.

### Provenance test
Can every major theoretical claim be traced to a supplied source or clearly labelled as corpus synthesis?

### Attribution test
Would the named theorist recognise the concept as their own, or has a later interpretation been silently back-read into them?

### Difference test
Have genuine disagreements between schools been preserved rather than harmonised away?

### Application test
Does the practical section turn theory into useful questions without pretending to know hidden motives?

### Couple-system test
Does the analysis examine reciprocal interaction rather than diagnosing the partner?

### Mentalization test
Does the corpus preserve uncertainty about both people's mental states?

### Ordinary-explanation test
Does it allow fatigue, stress, incompatible preferences, poor communication or justified anger to compete with deeper psychodynamic explanations?

### Duplication test
Are duplicate editions being treated as one intellectual source?

### Retrieval test
Can a Gemini Notebook query find the relevant concept quickly from headings and terminology?

### Digestibility test
Is each file coherent enough to be read as a chapter rather than functioning as an unstructured quotation dump?

# 16. Locked production principles

1. **Primary text before secondary interpretation.**
2. **Differences between schools are data, not defects to be smoothed over.**
3. **Concepts are explanatory tools, not personality labels.**
4. **Self-analysis has priority over analysing the absent partner.**
5. **A relational account examines the loop, not merely the individual.**
6. **Mental states are inferred, never directly observed.**
7. **Multiple hypotheses are preferable to one totalising interpretation.**
8. **The practical value of a theory does not prove its historical or empirical truth.**
9. **Developmental reconstruction must remain tentative.**
10. **The final aim is greater capacity to notice, think, mentalize, tolerate ambivalence and choose a response rather than merely repeat a familiar pattern.**
