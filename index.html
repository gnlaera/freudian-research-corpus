Freudian and Post-Freudian Thought

A structured research corpus tracing psychoanalytic thought from Freud through ego psychology, object relations, self psychology, relational theory, attachment, mentalization, sexuality and couple dynamics — with a disciplined framework for applied reflection.

Browse the corpus View repository

What this corpus is for

The corpus is organised as a compact set of canonical Markdown files rather than a collection of isolated summaries. The files build on one another while preserving important disagreements between psychoanalytic schools.

Learn the traditions

Follow the development of key psychoanalytic ideas while keeping Freud, Klein, Winnicott, Bion, Kohut, Kernberg, Mitchell, Benjamin, Bowlby and Fonagy conceptually distinct.

Compare frameworks

Examine how different traditions explain conflict, defence, internal objects, selfhood, attachment, recognition, desire, repetition and relational patterns.

Reflect without diagnosing

Use psychoanalytic concepts as hypotheses for disciplined first-person reflection, while preserving external reality, uncertainty and the opacity of another person's mind.

Method and guardrails

Psychoanalytic ideas are used here as disciplined interpretive frameworks, not as privileged access to hidden motives.

01
Hypotheses, not diagnoses

Concepts generate questions and competing formulations rather than labels or verdicts.

02
External reality remains primary

Actual behaviour, context, consent, incompatibility, care and harm must not be psychologised away.

03
Theoretical differences matter

Holding is not containment; mirroring is not recognition; object relations are not attachment theory.

04
Other minds remain partially opaque

Reflection distinguishes observation, first-person experience, inference, hypothesis and what remains unknown.

05
Reciprocity is not equal responsibility

Systemic interaction does not erase asymmetry in responsibility for deception, coercion, violence or boundary violations.

06
Interpretation remains revisable

Important formulations should compete with ordinary explanations and be tested against disconfirming evidence.
