# Self, Narcissism, Recognition and Relationality

## Purpose of this file

This file asks how psychoanalytic and adjacent developmental traditions explain four linked problems: how a person comes to experience a coherent self; why self-esteem can become unusually vulnerable; why recognition from other people matters; and how people participate in recurring relational patterns without reducing those patterns to conscious intention or equal responsibility.

The argument is deliberately plural. Freud, Kohut, Kernberg, Mitchell, Benjamin, Bowlby, and Fonagy and colleagues do not describe one theory in different vocabularies. They often identify different primary problems, different motives, and different mechanisms of change. The value of placing them together is comparative. A self-psychological question about cohesion may illuminate one aspect of an experience while an object-relations question about aggression and splitting, a relational question about reciprocal patterning, an attachment question about availability, or a mentalizing question about uncertainty illuminates another. None should be made to swallow the others.

The file uses the evidence discipline established for the corpus. A **primary claim** is attributed to the author who makes it. A **secondary interpretation** is identified as such. A **corpus synthesis** connects frameworks without pretending the connection was made by the original author. An **applied hypothesis** is a tentative use of a concept in reflection, not a diagnosis. An **empirical/developmental bridge** is used where Bowlby or Fonagy and colleagues connect psychoanalytic questions to developmental research. An **unresolved disagreement** remains unresolved when the source traditions genuinely conflict.

For self-reflection, two safeguards are central. First, explanation is not excuse. Understanding why a pattern may occur does not erase agency, responsibility, boundaries, or the effects of harm. Second, reciprocal influence does not imply equal responsibility. Coercion, abuse, intimidation, deception, and other harmful conduct must not be redescribed as merely a jointly created relational dance.

This file therefore supports questions such as: What threat to my sense of self is being activated here? What am I expecting the other person to do for my psychological equilibrium? What role am I moving into, and what role am I pressuring the other person to occupy? Am I recognising the other as a separate subject? What is actually known, and what is interpretation? These are questions for inquiry, not labels for oneself or a partner.

## Freud and the problem of narcissism

Freud's 1914 paper *On Narcissism* provides the starting problem, not the final vocabulary. Freud broadened narcissism from a description of a sexual perversion into a normal dimension of psychic life. Libido could be invested in the ego as well as in objects. This led him to distinguish, in economic terms, between ego-libido and object-libido and to ask how libidinal investment moves between self and other. The model remained a drive model: narcissism was formulated through the distribution and transformation of libido, not through later ideas of self-cohesion, recognition, or attachment security.

Freud also linked narcissism to self-regard. In *On Narcissism*, loving, being loved, and the fate of object investment affect self-regard. He argued that being loved and having love returned can raise self-regard, while longing and deprivation can lower it. The point is not that self-esteem is simply a social popularity score. Rather, Freud was trying to explain how object love and self-love are economically connected within his theory of libido.

The same paper introduced the ego ideal as a major problem. The child cannot indefinitely preserve an image of narcissistic perfection. Freud proposed that the lost perfection is displaced onto an ideal against which the actual ego can be measured. He distinguished **idealisation**, which exalts an object in the mind, from **sublimation**, which concerns the redirection of an instinctual aim. Later, in *The Ego and the Id* (1923), identification became still more important. Abandoned or altered object ties can leave identifications within the ego, and these identifications contribute to character and to the formation of the ego ideal or superego.

Freud therefore gives later theorists several problems to inherit: how self-regard is regulated; how ideals organise the self; how object choice and identification interact; and how love of self and love of others are related. But later theories substantially transform the explanatory model. Kohut moves toward the cohesion and vitality of the self. Kernberg reconstructs narcissism through internalised object relations, aggression, splitting, and identity integration. Mitchell places self-organisation inside a relational matrix. Benjamin asks how one subject recognises another. Bowlby gives attachment its own motivational status. Fonagy and colleagues focus on the development of an agentic self able to represent mental states.

For Freud's fuller model of unconscious conflict, repression, structural theory, and drive, this corpus treats the present section as a bridge rather than a substitute for the dedicated classical-theory file.

## What does "self" mean?

"Self" is one of the easiest words in this corpus to use too loosely. Different authors mean different things, and some do not treat the self as a single technical entity at all. The following distinctions are therefore working safeguards rather than claims that every theorist accepts the same definitions.

| Term | Working meaning in this corpus | Important caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Ego** | In Freud, a psychic organisation with perceptual, defensive, mediating, and reality-oriented functions, much of which can be unconscious. | Do not simply replace "ego" with "self". A structural agency is not identical to lived identity. |
| **Self** | The person as experienced or organised as a centre of initiative, continuity, value, and identity. In Kohut it becomes a central theoretical focus. | The term changes across authors and can be descriptive, phenomenological, developmental, or structural. |
| **Self-representation** | A mental representation of oneself, often considered in relation to representations of others. | A representation of self is not the whole person or the full lived experience of being a self. |
| **Self-experience** | The immediate or recurring subjective sense of oneself, such as feeling coherent, ashamed, powerful, empty, real, or fragmented. | Subjective experience is evidence about experience, not by itself proof of its historical cause. |
| **Self-cohesion** | Kohut's concern with experiencing oneself as a relatively integrated, continuous whole rather than as psychologically fragmenting. | Ordinary self-doubt or changing moods are not equivalent to a disturbance of self-cohesion. |
| **Identity** | A relatively integrated and continuous organisation of who one takes oneself and significant others to be. Kernberg gives integration of self and object representations particular weight. | Identity is not merely a list of traits or social roles. |
| **Subjectivity** | Lived first-person experience, including the fact that another person also has a distinct first-person centre of experience. | Intersubjectivity does not mean that two people share the same mind or reality. |

A useful synthesis is to treat "self" as a family of questions. What holds experience together? What gives a person a sense of continuity? How is value or esteem regulated? How are representations of self and others integrated? How does a person experience agency? How does the self remain itself while being dependent on other people? Different traditions answer different members of that family.

## Kohut and self psychology

Kohut's *The Restoration of the Self* (1977) makes a decisive shift in emphasis. Classical psychoanalysis had organised many clinical problems around conflict among drive, defence, prohibition, and reality. Kohut argues that some forms of suffering are better understood by asking whether the self has developed sufficient cohesion and reliable structure. The central problem may not be that a strong wish has been repressed. It may be that a vulnerable self has difficulty maintaining continuity, vitality, self-esteem, direction, or a stable sense of being a centre of initiative.

### Self-cohesion and fragmentation

Kohut describes the healthy self as a cohesive and enduring psychic configuration. His "nuclear self" is associated with the experience of being a centre of initiative and perception, with continuity across time, bodily and mental unity, and organising ambitions and ideals. This is not the Freudian ego under a new name. Kohut is changing the central explanatory problem from regulation of instinctual conflict toward the organisation and maintenance of self-experience.

The corresponding danger is **fragmentation**. In the material Kohut uses, disruptions in a needed selfobject relationship can be followed by a loss of the felt unity of the self. The person may experience diffuse anxiety, deadness, loss of vitality, or a sense that parts of experience no longer belong together. Kohut explicitly treats some such states as different in kind from a symptom that symbolically expresses a specific repressed sexual or aggressive wish.

This concept should be used narrowly in self-reflection. Feeling rattled after an argument, embarrassed after a mistake, or uncertain during change does not establish fragmentation in Kohut's technical sense. At most, it may prompt the applied question: did this event merely upset me, or did it seem to threaten my basic sense of coherence, value, or continuity?

### What is a selfobject?

A **selfobject** is not simply a kind or supportive person. The term refers to the function or experience of another person as participating in the maintenance, development, or restoration of the self. Kohut is interested in relationships in which functions that later can be carried more securely within the self are initially experienced through another. The emphasis is therefore on the role the other occupies in the person's self-experience, not on whether the other is objectively "good", warm, or helpful.

This explains why selfobject needs can persist in adult life without being reducible to childish dependency. Adults continue to rely on other people for responsiveness, inspiration, shared meaning, and a sense of belonging. The developmental question is not whether a mature person becomes psychologically self-sufficient. It is whether the person can use relationships without requiring a particular other to perform rigidly as an external psychological structure that cannot be lost, contradicted, or differentiated from.

Kohut's account includes several selfobject configurations. **Mirroring** concerns the self's need to have its aliveness, initiative, and value responsively registered. **Idealising** selfobject experience concerns the ability to connect with calmness, strength, value, or ideals experienced in another and gradually make those functions more one's own. *The Restoration of the Self* also refers to **twinship** or alter-ego experience, a sense of essential likeness or human belonging that can support development. These are not interchangeable forms of praise.

### The bipolar self, ambitions, ideals, and skills

Kohut describes a bipolar organisation of the self. One pole is associated with ambitions and the development of the grandiose-exhibitionistic self. The other is associated with ideals and the idealised parental imago. Between them lie talents and skills that enable ambitions and ideals to be realised. His model asks whether these sectors have developed and become integrated well enough to organise a vital life.

"Grandiose" here should not be imported directly from everyday moral language. In developmental usage it refers to early forms of self-expansiveness, assertiveness, and a wish to have one's emerging capacities responsively received. Those possibilities can develop into realistic ambition. Likewise, idealisation is not inherently pathological. The ability to admire, value, and draw strength from another person can become part of the development of stable ideals.

### Transmuting internalisation and optimal frustration

Kohut uses **transmuting internalisation** for the gradual process by which functions initially sustained through a selfobject relationship become more securely organised within the self. He links this to manageable, "optimal" frustrations. The other person does not respond perfectly. The mismatch is neither so severe that it overwhelms development nor absent altogether. Across repeated experiences, functions once carried by the relationship are transformed into internal psychological structure.

The adult implication is subtle. Growth is not achieved by arranging perfect responsiveness from others. Nor, in Kohut's framework, is it achieved by deliberately withholding responsiveness in the name of toughness. The relevant process is an experience of sufficiently attuned connection combined with tolerable failures and repair, through which a person becomes less dependent on the concrete presence or perfect performance of a particular selfobject.

### Narcissistic vulnerability and injury

Kohut gives special attention to injuries that strike at self-cohesion or core self-esteem. A **narcissistic injury** is therefore not a technical synonym for "someone criticised me". The injury matters because of what the event means to the organisation of self. A mild correction may be ordinary disappointment for one person but may be experienced by another, in a particular state, as humiliation, collapse of an idealised self-image, or evidence that a sustaining relationship has vanished.

This does not mean the external event is irrelevant. Kohut is explicit that failures of empathy can occur in the present and that a person's reaction need not be treated as proof of a hidden viciousness. Yet the fact that an event is experienced as narcissistically injurious does not by itself prove that the other person acted wrongly. Experience, event, and interpretation must remain distinguishable.

## Mirroring, idealisation and narcissistic vulnerability

Kohutian language is easily diluted into ordinary relationship advice. Several distinctions help keep it precise.

| Concept | What it is closer to in Kohut | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| **Mirroring** | Responsive participation in the emergence and maintenance of a valued, alive, coherent self. | Generic praise, compliments, flattery, or agreeing that the person is impressive. |
| **Idealising selfobject experience** | Drawing organisation, calm, value, or direction from connection with an idealised other, with later internalisation of some functions. | Blind idealisation, worship, surrender of judgement, or insisting the other is flawless. |
| **Twinship / alter-ego experience** | A sustaining experience of likeness, belonging, or human kinship. | Requiring another person to be identical, to share every preference, or to erase difference. |
| **Narcissistic vulnerability** | Unusual sensitivity where self-esteem, cohesion, or continuity depends on precarious forms of external regulation. | Any wish to be appreciated or any dislike of criticism. |
| **Narcissistic injury** | An event experienced as striking the organisation or value of the self, often with shame, rage, withdrawal, or collapse. | Every disagreement, disappointment, boundary, or corrective comment. |

An adult example makes the distinction clearer. Imagine that a person gives a presentation and their partner says, "You seemed nervous in the middle." An ordinary response may be mild embarrassment followed by curiosity. A different response might be intense humiliation, a conviction that the partner has exposed them as fraudulent, and a wish to retaliate or disappear. A Kohutian applied hypothesis would not be "this person is narcissistic." It would be: perhaps the comment acquired disproportionate force because competence in that moment was carrying too much of the person's self-cohesion or self-esteem.

A second example: one partner may admire the other's steadiness during a crisis. That admiration can be healthy. It becomes theoretically closer to idealising selfobject functioning when the admired person's strength is being used to organise one's own sense of safety, values, or direction. The developmental question is whether that function can gradually be internalised, not whether admiration should disappear.

Likewise, mirroring is not a demand that one's partner continually endorse one's preferred self-description. A person may need to feel seen in effort, initiative, pain, or accomplishment while still being contradicted about facts or held accountable for conduct. The most useful distinction is between **recognising subjective experience** and **certifying every interpretation of reality**.

Kohut's theory also prevents the opposite error: pathologising ordinary interdependence. Self-esteem fluctuates with relationships in almost everyone. McWilliams (2011), writing from a later integrative perspective, stresses that ordinary people are affected by approval and disapproval. A clinically significant narcissistic organisation concerns the disproportionate centrality and rigidity of self-esteem regulation, not the existence of such regulation at all.

## Empathy in Kohut

Kohut gives empathy a technical status. He treats empathic or vicarious introspection as a mode of access to subjective psychological experience. To understand a person's inner world, the observer attempts to grasp what the situation feels like from within that person's organisation of experience. Empathy, in this sense, is epistemic before it is comforting. It is a way of gathering psychological data.

This is why empathy should not be collapsed into reassurance or approval. One can understand how a criticism was experienced as annihilating without agreeing that the critic intended annihilation. One can understand rage without endorsing aggression. One can understand a wish for admiration without providing limitless admiration. Empathy does not require emotional fusion, and it does not abolish the observer's independent judgement.

Kohut also does not make perfect empathy a realistic goal. He treats empathic failures as unavoidable. In his developmental logic, manageable failures can become part of structure formation when they can be experienced, understood, and worked through. This is different from deliberately causing injury. The point is that no relationship can provide perfect attunement, and psychological growth requires some capacity to survive and metabolise misattunement.

A useful corpus synthesis is therefore:

1. **Empathy asks:** What does this experience mean from the person's point of view?
2. **Reality testing asks:** What else happened, including facts the person may not see?
3. **Ethical judgement asks:** What conduct was acceptable or harmful?
4. **Mentalization asks:** How certain are we about any person's state of mind?

These questions can coexist. Empathy becomes hazardous when it is turned into mind-reading or moral exoneration. It becomes thin when it is reduced to soothing language that never tests assumptions, conflict, aggression, or the reality of the other person.

## Kernberg and a different theory of narcissism

Kernberg's *Love Relations: Normality and Pathology* (1995) approaches narcissism from a different architecture. The central language is not primarily selfobject failure and cohesion. It is internalised object relations, self and object representations, affect, aggression, splitting, idealisation and devaluation, identity integration, and conflict.

### Self and object representations

In Kernberg's object-relations model, psychological life is organised through affectively charged relations between representations of self and representations of others. Development involves increasing integration of loving and hating aspects of self and object. Where integration succeeds sufficiently, a person can experience both self and other as complex wholes with mixed qualities. Where it is weak, sharply idealised and devalued configurations can remain separated.

Kernberg gives this integration major importance for identity. A stable relationship requires more than strong feeling. It requires enough integration that ambivalence can be borne without the loved person repeatedly becoming all-good when gratifying and all-bad when frustrating. Mature idealisation therefore differs from defensive idealisation. It can value the other without denying limitation, aggression, conflict, or difference.

### Aggression and conflict

Aggression has a more central role in Kernberg than in Kohut's account. *Love Relations* repeatedly treats love and aggression as intertwined in intimate object relations. Intimacy activates dependency, longing, envy, rivalry, sexual excitement, frustration, and ambivalence. Growth does not mean eliminating aggression. It means a greater capacity to integrate it, contain it within concern for the other, and prevent it from destroying the relationship or the representation of the other as a whole person.

Kernberg therefore interprets some idealisation/devaluation cycles as defensive organisations around conflict and aggression. In pathological narcissistic organisation, a grandiose self may be maintained partly by projecting devalued aspects of self into others, extracting admiration, or defending against envy and dependency. This is theoretically different from saying that grandiosity is mainly compensation for a structurally deprived or insufficiently cohesive self.

### Dependency, envy, and the other person

Kernberg's treatment of dependency is especially relevant to narcissistic love relations. If a needed other is also experienced as the possessor of what one lacks, dependence can activate envy. In severe narcissistic organisation, the goodness of what the other can give may itself become difficult to tolerate. Admiration can then be preferred to dependence on love because admiration supports the grandiose self while avoiding gratitude and indebtedness.

This is not a claim about everyone who dislikes dependence. It is a theoretical account of one possible organisation. The applied question is narrower: when I devalue someone immediately after needing them, is the devaluation protecting me from the vulnerability of dependence, envy, gratitude, or disappointment? Other explanations may fit better.

### Mature love

Kernberg's account is not simply a pathology theory. He describes mature sexual love as involving stable identity, the integration of loving and aggressive currents, the capacity for concern, realistic idealisation, sexual passion, and an ability to cross psychological boundaries without losing the distinction between self and other. He emphasises curiosity about the other's internal reality. In his account, the world of internalised and actual object relations keeps sexuality psychologically alive rather than reducing it to repetitive discharge.

Mature love also involves accepting ambivalence. A partner can gratify and frustrate, be admired and limited, inspire desire and anger, and remain the same complex person. This capacity to sustain mixed representations is one of Kernberg's clearest contrasts with splitting and devaluation.

## Kohut versus Kernberg

Kohut and Kernberg are often grouped under "narcissism", but their disagreement is structural, not cosmetic. McWilliams (2011) captures the contrast as a difference between a developmental-deficit model and a conflict/organisation model. That is a secondary summary, not a declaration that every case must fit one camp.

| Dimension | Kohut | Kernberg |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic problem** | Vulnerability or defect in the cohesion and structure of the self. | Pathological organisation of internalised object relations, self/object representations, affects, and identity. |
| **Role of conflict** | Can be secondary to self-defect in the patients for whom self psychology is most relevant. | Central. Conflict, ambivalence, aggression, envy, and defence are constitutive. |
| **Role of aggression** | Rage may arise importantly in response to narcissistic injury, fragmentation threat, or empathic failure. | Aggression is a major organising force that must be integrated with love; it can infiltrate grandiosity and object relations. |
| **Developmental emphasis** | Failures in selfobject responsiveness can leave structural deficits or unfinished developmental tasks. | Pathology involves defensive organisation, splitting, distorted internalised relations, and failures of integration. |
| **Narcissistic vulnerability** | Threat to self-esteem, cohesion, continuity, or vitality, with possible fragmentation. | Threat to grandiose self-organisation, identity, idealisation, or defence against dependency, envy, and aggression. |
| **Idealisation** | A normal selfobject line that can support development and become internalised. | Can be mature and realistic, but primitive idealisation can defend splitting and deny aggression or ambivalence. |
| **Empathy** | Fundamental mode of psychological observation and central clinical stance. | Important, but not sufficient; understanding must also include conflict, defence, aggression, and distorted relations. |
| **Interpretation** | Should emerge from empathic understanding of the self's experience and developmental needs. | More emphasis on tactful confrontation and interpretation of grandiosity, splitting, envy, aggression, and defensive patterns. |
| **Self-esteem regulation** | Central problem of the vulnerable self and selfobject matrix. | Linked to grandiose self-organisation, superego/ego ideal, object relations, and defence against dependency and envy. |
| **View of relationships** | Others can function as selfobjects whose responsiveness participates in maintaining and developing self-cohesion. | Relationships activate internalised self/object configurations, love and aggression, idealisation/devaluation, projection, and dependency. |
| **Clinical implication** | Foster sufficiently empathic understanding, work through inevitable failures, support transmuting internalisation and stronger self-structure. | Bring split or disavowed configurations into relation, interpret defensive operations, and increase integration and capacity for ambivalence. |
| **Application caution** | Do not call ordinary reassurance-seeking or hurt "selfobject pathology". | Do not infer primitive aggression, envy, or grandiosity merely because a relationship is conflictual. |

The disagreement produces different readings of the same event. Suppose a person reacts to their partner's independent success with withdrawal and contempt. A Kohutian hypothesis might ask whether the success disrupted a needed selfobject equilibrium or exposed fragile self-esteem. A Kernbergian hypothesis might ask whether admiration and envy became difficult to integrate, leading to devaluation as a defence. A relational formulation might ask how both people then entered a familiar pursue-withdraw or superiority-inferiority configuration. An ordinary explanation might simply be fatigue, disappointment about a missed shared plan, or a separate unresolved grievance.

The correct discipline is not to choose the most dramatic interpretation. It is to keep competing hypotheses open and look for repeated evidence.

## Interpersonal psychoanalysis and Sullivan

Harry Stack Sullivan is represented in this Project through later historical and integrative accounts, especially Mitchell and Black (1995), not through a supplied primary Sullivan text. The claims in this section should therefore be read as **secondary interpretation** of Sullivan's place in the lineage.

Sullivan's broad move was to change the unit of observation. Rather than treating personality as something that can be adequately understood inside an isolated individual, interpersonal psychoanalysis treats the person as inseparable from an interpersonal field. Mitchell and Black describe Sullivan's personality as an enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations. The point is not that there is no inner life. It is that inner life develops, appears, and is maintained in relation to other people.

This approach puts anxiety in relationships at the centre. The developing child encounters not only satisfaction and frustration but also the anxiety of important others. Experiences that fit what significant others can tolerate become organised into familiar versions of self. Experiences that repeatedly provoke intense anxiety may be excluded from ordinary self-recognition. In the secondary account, Sullivan's **self-system** is the organisation that steers experience toward what has historically reduced anxiety and away from what threatens to increase it.

Sullivan called these rapid anti-anxiety manoeuvres **security operations**. Their significance is relational and procedural. A person may enter an interaction wanting closeness but, at the point where closeness creates vulnerability, use criticism, superiority, withdrawal, joking, compliance, or some other familiar move that restores security. The resulting pattern can feel as though it simply "happened", because the relevant sequence may occur too quickly or too automatically to be noticed.

Sullivan's clinical method, as reconstructed by Mitchell and Black, follows from this model. The analyst asks what actually happened between people: who said what, when the atmosphere changed, what the person noticed, and what they did next. This differs from assuming that the most important data will emerge only as disguised derivatives of repressed instinctual wishes. The interpersonal analyst is looking for "unattended" interactions that organise relationships outside focal awareness.

This provides a bridge to Mitchell. Sullivan's model makes actual interaction psychologically consequential and treats the analyst as a participant-observer rather than a detached decoder. Mitchell retains this interpersonal emphasis but brings it into explicit dialogue with British object relations, self psychology, fantasy, internalisation, and self-organisation.

## Mitchell and the relational matrix

Mitchell's *Relational Concepts in Psychoanalysis* (1988) is best understood as an attempt at selective integration rather than as a claim that all previous theories were secretly saying the same thing. He draws especially on interpersonal psychoanalysis, British object relations, and psychologies of the self, while also preserving much of Freud's clinical attention to unconscious processes, identification, conflict, sexuality, and fantasy.

### The relational matrix

Mitchell uses **relational matrix** to resist a set of misleading either/or choices: interpersonal versus intrapsychic, inner versus outer, self versus other, fantasy versus actuality, conflict versus developmental failure. He argues that psychological reality includes self-organisation, attachments to others, internalised relational configurations, actual interpersonal transactions, fantasy, and the person's active participation in repeatedly organising a subjective world.

The relational matrix is therefore not a synonym for "relationships matter". Nor is it a theory that behaviour between two people is more real than internal fantasy. Mitchell explicitly rejects both reductions. Internal and interpersonal worlds interpenetrate. Expectations formed in earlier relationships shape perception and action in current relationships; current interactions can reinforce, modify, or challenge internal configurations; fantasy alters what is perceived; and actual behaviour from another person can confirm or disconfirm expectation.

A simple formulation is:

**I bring an organised way of being with others -> I perceive and select cues through that organisation -> I act in ways shaped by it -> the other person responds from their own organisation and from the actual situation -> that response affects me -> the pattern is reinforced, modified, or disrupted.**

This formulation does not imply that every response is caused by the first person's expectations. The other has an independent mind, history, interests, freedom, and capacity for harmful conduct. The matrix is interactive, not solipsistic.

### Self-organisation and relatedness

Mitchell treats self-continuity and connection as tightly linked. A familiar psychological organisation can persist because it is the way a person recognises themselves as themselves and the way they know how to remain connected to significant others. This makes apparently self-defeating patterns more intelligible without making them desirable.

In his account, early relationships narrow a much wider range of possible ways of being. A child develops a particular shape in relation to the contours of significant others. Later, moving outside that familiar organisation can threaten not only comfort but identity, loyalty, and connection. A new relationship pattern may therefore feel attractive consciously and dangerous implicitly.

This differs from a simple developmental-deficit theory. Mitchell resists making all persistent difficulty the result of something missing that must now be supplied. Familiar relational organisations may themselves be actively maintained because they organise the self and preserve ties, even when they are painful.

## From drive conflict to relational conflict

One risk in relational theory is to sound as though conflict disappeared when drive theory was revised. Mitchell explicitly argues against that conclusion. He treats conflict as inherent in relatedness, although the unit of conflict changes.

| Classical conflict emphasis | Relational conflict emphasis |
|---|---|
| Drive or wish versus defence, prohibition, conscience, or reality. | One relational configuration versus another. |
| Conflict between instinctual pressure and the conditions under which satisfaction is possible. | Conflict between different loyalties, identifications, expectations, or ways of being with people. |
| Oedipal wishes and prohibitions provide a major organising template. | Dyadic and triangular patterns remain important, but not because all relational life is derived from drive economics. |
| Symptom may be compromise formation between opposed forces. | A repetitive pattern may be a compromise among attachment, self-definition, safety, desire, guilt, and relational familiarity. |

A person may want closeness and fear the loss of self that closeness seems to require. They may want autonomy but experience autonomy as betrayal of a family bond. They may want to be cared for but believe that needing care means inferiority. They may want sexual spontaneity but associate it with loss of control or danger to attachment. These are conflicts, not simply deficiencies.

Mitchell also does not eliminate sexuality or aggression. He relocates them inside relational configurations. Sexual experience may organise identity, attachment, power, fantasy, recognition, and difference. Aggression may express conflict about dependence, autonomy, humiliation, rivalry, or intrusion. Unconscious fantasy remains important, but it is no longer treated as psychologically independent of the relational matrix in which meanings are created and sustained.

## Repetition in relational theory

Why would a person repeat a painful pattern? A crude answer is that they secretly want the suffering. Mitchell provides a more useful account. Familiar patterns can solve several psychological problems at once even while producing distress.

From the standpoint of **self-organisation**, repetition can preserve continuity: "This is who I am, this is what people are like, and this is how relationships work." From the standpoint of **object ties**, repetition can preserve loyalty and connection to important early relationships. From the standpoint of Sullivanian **security operations**, familiar interactions can reduce anxiety because their sequence and ending are known. From the standpoint of fantasy, repetition can keep alive a hope that this time the familiar drama will end differently.

Mitchell describes maladaptive patterns as woven rather than merely imposed. A person enters new situations with prestructured ways of connecting and may implicitly assign others a narrow range of roles. This does not mean the person consciously chooses the outcome. Nor does it mean the other person lacks agency. It means participation in the pattern is a legitimate object of inquiry.

Four distinctions are essential:

1. **Historical repetition** concerns continuity with earlier relational organisations.
2. **Relational familiarity** concerns the safety or identity value of a known pattern even when its history is uncertain.
3. **Active participation** means one's behaviour can help evoke, stabilise, or respond to a pattern without conscious intent.
4. **Actual mistreatment** refers to what another person genuinely does. A relational formulation must never use one's participation to erase coercion, abuse, betrayal, intimidation, or unequal power.

An applied question is therefore not "How did I cause this person to treat me badly?" It is "Given what actually happened, what did I expect, what role did I move into, what options did I perceive, and what kept the pattern familiar or difficult to leave?" Responsibility for harmful action remains with the actor.

## Enactment

"Enactment" is used differently across psychoanalytic literatures, so it should be handled cautiously. In the relational context relevant here, it can be understood as an interaction in which relational meanings are expressed and organised through participation before, or instead of, being fully represented in words.

For example, one person expects that expressing need will make the other withdraw. They hint rather than ask directly. The other experiences the hints as pressure and becomes distant. The first person experiences the distance as proof that direct need is dangerous and escalates the hints. Neither person needs to have consciously designed the sequence for the interaction to enact a familiar expectation.

The concept has several parts:

- an expectation or relational configuration is carried into an interaction;
- participants respond to each other, not just to internal fantasy;
- each person's response can draw out a familiar position in the other;
- the pattern conveys meaning through action, timing, tone, silence, pursuit, withdrawal, control, compliance, or other behaviour;
- understanding often comes after participation has already occurred.

Enactment is not the same as **manipulation**. Manipulation implies a more deliberate attempt to influence another for a purpose. Enactment may be largely unplanned. It is not identical to **projective identification**, a concept with a specific Kleinian and post-Kleinian history. It is not identical to **transference**, because transference can be conceptualised without reciprocal behavioural participation. It is not ordinary disagreement, because disagreement does not necessarily carry a recurrent, partly unrepresented relational configuration.

Mitchell's relational matrix supports this broader way of thinking even where his terminology is not identical to later relational usage. His clinical model treats the analyst as discovering themselves inside repetitive configurations rather than standing wholly outside them. The analyst's or partner's actual response matters. This is one reason relational work places such weight on observing the pattern while participating in it.

The responsibility safeguard is decisive. A person can be drawn into a role without becoming equally responsible for what the other does from that role. Reciprocal influence is a descriptive claim about interaction, not a moral equation.

## Jessica Benjamin: recognition and mutuality

Benjamin's *The Bonds of Love* (1988) adds a problem that cannot be reduced to self-esteem regulation or attachment security: **recognition between subjects**. Her central question is how a person can need another person's recognition while also recognising that the other is an independent centre of experience, not an extension, possession, audience, or instrument of the self.

### Recognition

Recognition is more than receiving a positive response. Benjamin's argument is paradoxical: recognition matters only if it comes from someone whose independent subjectivity is also acknowledged. If the other is treated merely as a mirror under one's control, their recognition loses the very independence that gives it value.

Recognition therefore involves two directions at once. I want my action, feeling, intention, and existence to be registered by you. But I must also encounter you as someone with intentions, feelings, and purposes that are not reducible to mine. Subjectivity is confirmed in a subject-subject relation, not by turning one party into the permanent subject and the other into an object.

This makes recognition different from generic validation. Validation can mean acknowledging that an experience is understandable. Recognition, in Benjamin's stronger sense, includes the reality of two centres of agency. I may recognise that you are disappointed while disagreeing with your judgement of me. I may assert my own desire while recognising that your desire differs. Mutual recognition does not require shared preferences or shared interpretations.

### Mutuality without perfect symmetry

Benjamin's use of **mutuality** should not be read as a claim that relationships must be symmetrical at every moment. Parent and infant are unequal in capacity and responsibility, yet she argues that a form of mutual recognition can still exist because the infant is an active participant and the caregiver responds to the infant as a centre of initiative.

Adult mutuality likewise allows temporary asymmetry. One person may need more care during illness, grief, or crisis. The issue is whether asymmetry becomes a fixed structure in which only one person's subjectivity counts. Mutuality is the capacity for influence to run both ways and for both people, over time, to exist as subjects rather than one being reduced to the function of serving the other's self.

### Dependence and independence

Benjamin's developmental problem can be stated simply:

> How can I depend on another person while also recognising that they are not an extension of me?

The mature solution is not complete independence. If recognition must come from an independent other, dependence and independence are intertwined. The other matters precisely because the other is outside my control. This creates a permanent tension between connection and separateness rather than a developmental journey that ends in self-sufficiency.

## Recognition versus mirroring

Mirroring and recognition overlap in ordinary language because both involve being "seen". The theories are nevertheless distinct.

| Concept | Core question | What the other is doing | What it does **not** require |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kohutian mirroring** | Is my emerging or vulnerable self responsively registered so that cohesion, vitality, and self-esteem can develop? | Participates in a selfobject function by responding to initiative, affect, achievement, or aliveness. | Agreement with every claim, praise on demand, or denial of limits. |
| **Benjaminian recognition** | Can I be recognised by you while also recognising you as a separate subject with your own centre of experience? | Meets the person as another subject and accepts mutual influence and difference. | Approval, sameness, or permanent symmetry. |
| **Validation** | Can the experience be acknowledged as understandable or real for this person? | Communicates that the person's experience has been heard and can be made sense of. | Agreement about facts or motives. |
| **Agreement** | Do we reach the same judgement, preference, or conclusion? | Shares a proposition or position. | Empathy, recognition, or admiration. |
| **Admiration** | Do I value or look up to this person or quality? | Attributes positive value, competence, beauty, strength, or status. | Selfobject functioning or mutual recognition. |
| **Empathy** | Can I understand the other's subjective experience from within their frame of reference? | Attempts vicarious understanding of experience. | Approval, reassurance, fusion, or certainty. |

A partner saying "I can see why that landed as humiliating" may be empathic and validating. It becomes closer to Benjaminian recognition when the partner also remains a subject: "I can see why it hurt, and I also meant what I said about the decision." It becomes closer to Kohutian mirroring when the response participates in restoring or consolidating a threatened sense of value or aliveness. These can coexist, but they are not the same operation.

## Complementarity and domination

Benjamin uses domination to analyse what happens when the tension of mutual recognition collapses. The relation can harden into **complementarity**, a structure in which the parties occupy opposed positions such as active/passive, controller/controlled, pursuer/withdrawer, knower/known, or doer/done-to. The positions can reverse while the structure remains intact.

In such a structure, the other is no longer fully encountered as a separate centre of desire. The other is recruited into a role required by the relationship's organisation. A person may feel subject only by making the other an object, or may secure connection only through submission to the other's subjectivity. Benjamin links domination to this failure of recognition rather than treating it simply as an excess of individual aggression.

This is particularly useful in adult reflection because many conflicts become organised around binary positions. One partner feels they are "always the responsible one" and the other "always the irresponsible one". One becomes the critic and the other the criticised. One pursues explanation while the other withholds. Each move makes the complementary move more likely.

But two cautions are essential. First, not every polarisation is domination. People divide tasks, differ in temperament, and temporarily occupy unequal roles for ordinary reasons. Second, where coercion or abuse exists, the language of complementarity must not distribute responsibility evenly. A person adapting to threat is not thereby equally responsible for the threat.

Benjamin's alternative is not a relationship without conflict. It is the restoration of a subject-subject tension in which one's own agency and the other's otherness can coexist. The other must be able to make a difference without becoming either omnipotent or negligible.

## Attachment as continuity and revision

Bowlby's *A Secure Base* (1988) is historically connected to psychoanalysis but theoretically revisionary. Bowlby rejected the idea that attachment to a caregiver is secondary to feeding or a derivative of a more general libido. Attachment behaviour has its own dynamics and biological function, especially protection through proximity or access to a familiar figure.

### Attachment as a primary system

Attachment behaviour becomes especially visible when a person is frightened, tired, ill, separated, or uncertain. The goal is not permanent physical closeness but sufficient proximity or accessibility to a figure expected to be responsive. Bowlby emphasises that attachment behaviour persists across the life cycle. The wish for comfort or support under adversity is not, in his model, evidence of immaturity.

This makes "dependency" too crude a synonym. Dependency can imply a global weakness or one-way reliance. Attachment refers to an organised bond with particular figures and to a system that is activated under particular conditions. Caregiving and exploration are related but distinguishable systems.

### Secure base and exploration

A secure attachment figure provides a **secure base** from which exploration is possible. When the figure is expected to be accessible and responsive if needed, the person can move away, explore, work, play, and return. Security therefore supports autonomy rather than opposing it.

The developmental implication is important for adult relationships. A need for reassurance at a moment of threat does not contradict autonomy. The relevant questions are whether the relationship permits both proximity and exploration, and whether reliance can flex with circumstances. Secure attachment is not never needing anyone. It is closer to confidence that need can be expressed without making separation or exploration impossible.

### Working models

Bowlby describes representational or **working models** of attachment figures and of self-in-relation-to-them. These models organise expectations about whether help will be available, whether signals will be responded to, and what happens when closeness is sought. They are central to personality functioning across life in Bowlby's framework.

There is conceptual overlap here with object relations, but not identity. Internal objects in object-relations theory carry a different theoretical history involving fantasy, introjection, projection, splitting, and unconscious object relations. Bowlby's working models arise within an ethological, control-systems, and observational framework. Similarly, a selfobject need is not simply an attachment need: Kohut asks how another participates in self-cohesion; Bowlby asks how an attachment system regulates proximity, availability, and security.

Attachment is also not equivalent to relational psychoanalysis. Mitchell's relational matrix is a broader theory of how self-organisation, fantasy, object ties, and transactions mutually shape experience. Mitchell explicitly warns against reducing the richness of the relational field to any single superordinate motive, including attachment.

## Mentalization and reflective function

Fonagy, Gergely, Jurist, and Target's *Affect Regulation, Mentalization, and the Development of the Self* (2002) provides a later developmental bridge. Their work draws on psychoanalysis and attachment while adding a more explicit account of how a person develops the capacity to understand behaviour in terms of mental states.

### Mentalization

**Mentalization** refers to the capacity to perceive and interpret one's own and others' behaviour as related to thoughts, feelings, wishes, beliefs, intentions, and other mental states. **Reflective function** is the operational expression of this capacity. It has both self-reflective and interpersonal components.

Mentalization is not simply "thinking about feelings". A person can produce sophisticated psychological explanations while being rigidly certain, emotionally disconnected, or wrong about the other person's mind. Mentalization includes representation, flexibility, perspective taking, and the ability to distinguish inner experience from external reality.

Fonagy and colleagues connect this capacity to affect regulation, impulse control, self-monitoring, self-organisation, and agency. If I can experience anger as a mental state rather than as an unquestionable fact about the world, I have more room to reflect on what it means and what to do with it. If I can imagine that another person's behaviour may arise from several possible mental states, I have more room than if I treat my first interpretation as direct perception.

### Psychic equivalence, pretend mode, and integration

Their developmental account distinguishes modes that later become integrated. In **psychic equivalence**, inner experience and external reality are treated as if they correspond directly. If I feel rejected, then I have been rejected; if I fear you despise me, then your contempt feels like a fact. In **pretend mode**, inner representations can become disconnected from external reality, so thoughts can be manipulated without being felt as anchored in consequences or actuality.

Mentalization develops when internal and external reality can be linked without being collapsed. A thought is meaningful but remains a representation. A feeling is real as a feeling but does not prove its own explanation. This is a powerful epistemic discipline for psychoanalytic reflection.

### Marked affect mirroring

Fonagy and colleagues, drawing particularly on Gergely and Watson, describe **marked affect mirroring** as one developmental mechanism by which the child comes to represent affect. A caregiver responds contingently to the child's state but marks the response as an "as if" representation rather than simply displaying the caregiver's own overwhelming emotion. The infant can then begin to experience the reflected affect as referring to the infant's state and as something that can be represented and regulated.

This should not be confused with Kohutian mirroring. Both involve responsive recognition of affect or self-experience, but the theoretical work is different. Kohutian mirroring concerns selfobject participation in self-cohesion and self-esteem. Marked affect mirroring is part of an account of affect representation, regulation, and the development of mentalizing capacities.

### Play, psychic reality, and agency

Fonagy and colleagues also give play an important role. In safe pretend play, a child can experience mental states as meaningful without treating them as literal reality. The caregiver can hold an alternative perspective without annihilating the child's experience. This helps integrate psychic equivalence and pretend modes into a more flexible representational stance.

The resulting self is increasingly agentic because mental states can be experienced as states of mind that influence behaviour rather than as raw commands or incontrovertible facts. Agency here does not mean independence from other minds. The capacity develops interpersonally and remains supported by relationships.

## Mentalization versus interpretation

Psychoanalytic interpretation and mentalization can complement each other, but they ask different questions and carry different epistemic risks.

| Approach | Characteristic question | Strength | Main risk if overused |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Psychoanalytic interpretation** | What unconscious meaning, conflict, defence, fantasy, identification, or relational configuration may be organising this pattern? | Can connect present experience to latent organisation and recurring structure. | Becoming overconfident, totalising, or unfalsifiable; treating inference as fact. |
| **Mentalizing inquiry** | What might each person be thinking, feeling, expecting, or fearing, and how certain can we be? | Preserves multiple perspectives, representational flexibility, and uncertainty. | Remaining descriptive or tentative when deeper recurring organisation also needs to be examined. |

The key constraint is:

> The mind of another person remains partially opaque.

That principle does not prohibit interpretation. It regulates it. "Perhaps you withdrew because needing me felt humiliating" is an interpretation offered as a hypothesis. "You withdrew because you cannot tolerate dependence" claims privileged access to another mind and developmental history. The first can be tested against experience, alternatives, and the person's own account. The second is more likely to become psychoanalytic mind-reading.

Mentalization also protects against intellectualisation only if it remains connected to lived affect. An elegant explanation of everyone's motives can function as a way of avoiding fear, grief, anger, desire, or uncertainty. Reflective function is not measured by psychological vocabulary. It is shown in the capacity to represent mental states while remaining open to revision.

A productive sequence is therefore: describe the observable event; identify one's own affect and interpretation; imagine several plausible mental states; check what evidence exists; consider a psychodynamic formulation; and return to uncertainty. Interpretation can deepen mentalization, while mentalization can keep interpretation epistemically disciplined.

The following table is a retrieval aid, not an attempt to reconcile the theories. Each row simplifies a more complex framework so that the differences remain visible.

Several overlaps are real. Kohut and Bowlby both take relational provision seriously, but selfobject function and attachment function are not identical. Benjamin and Fonagy both require the other mind to be real, but recognition is a theory of subject-subject relation while mentalization is a theory of representing mental states. Kernberg and Mitchell both analyse patterns involving self and other, but Kernberg gives aggression, internalised object relations, and identity integration a different theoretical weight. These are connections, not collapses.

## Dependency and autonomy

Psychoanalytic writing has often inherited a cultural tendency to contrast dependence with maturity. The frameworks in this file complicate that contrast.

For **Kohut**, development does not eliminate selfobject needs. Human beings remain responsive to recognition, ideals, belonging, and sustaining relationships. The developmental achievement is greater self-structure and flexibility, not an absence of relational need. A person who can internalise functions and survive ordinary misattunement is less compelled to make one particular other responsible for psychological cohesion.

For **Bowlby**, attachment security actually supports exploration. A secure base makes separation more possible because accessibility can be trusted. The opposite of dependency is therefore not simply distance. A person who never seeks comfort may be highly constrained by expectations about care rather than especially autonomous.

For **Benjamin**, autonomy becomes psychologically meaningful only in relation to another independent subject. If I can feel free only when the other has no claim on me, my autonomy is brittle. If I can maintain connection only by surrendering my subjectivity, my dependence is likewise constrained. Mutual recognition requires the paradox that the other matters and cannot be controlled.

For **Mitchell**, both dependence and autonomy can become organised inside familiar relational configurations. One person may preserve attachment by becoming helpless; another may preserve attachment by becoming indispensable; another may maintain connection by repeatedly threatening separation. Conversely, apparent autonomy can be a familiar role that keeps dangerous need out of awareness. The important question is what the position does within the relational matrix.

**Winnicott (1965)** provides a limited developmental bridge. He makes actual early dependence central to the emergence of the self and describes development as movement from dependence toward independence within a facilitating environment. His false-self formulation also shows how apparent adaptation can become organised through compliance when spontaneous experience cannot safely find a place in the relationship. Yet even Winnicott's mature person does not become a sealed unit. The capacity to be alone depends on having internalised a sufficiently reliable relational environment.

Winnicott (1971) adds another useful bridge in his account of moving from a subjectively created object toward the capacity to use an object experienced as existing outside omnipotent control. In the present corpus, that idea should not be collapsed into Benjamin's later theory of recognition, but it helps clarify why mature relatedness involves tolerating an other who is not simply an extension of one's own need or fantasy.

Two practical corrections follow:

- **Dependency is not automatically immaturity.** Illness, grief, fear, love, parenting, sex, work, and ordinary life involve legitimate reliance on others.
- **Autonomy is not self-sufficiency.** It includes the capacity to choose, to differ, to remain agentic while connected, and to tolerate the other's agency in return.

A useful applied question is therefore not "Am I too dependent?" but "How flexible is my dependence? Can I ask, receive, refuse, separate, return, and tolerate that the other can do the same?"

## Shame, self-esteem and narcissistic injury

Shame is important to later psychoanalytic accounts of narcissistic vulnerability, but the Project sources do not justify reducing all narcissistic phenomena to shame. The safest approach is to distinguish several levels.

**Ordinary disappointment** occurs when an outcome or response is worse than hoped. It may hurt without becoming a judgement of the whole self. **Shame** involves an experience of the self as exposed, deficient, contemptible, or falling short in the eyes of self or other. **Narcissistic injury**, in Kohutian usage, concerns an injury that strikes self-esteem or cohesion with unusual force because of the self's organisation and selfobject context. **Narcissistic pathology** refers to a more pervasive and rigid organisation, not to an isolated episode of shame, pride, embarrassment, or sensitivity.

McWilliams (2011) is useful as a contemporary secondary source here. She stresses that everyone regulates self-esteem partly through responses from significant others. Narcissistic organisation becomes clinically distinctive when self-esteem regulation is disproportionately central and externally precarious. She also highlights shame and envy, and the defensive use of idealisation and devaluation, without suggesting that these affects belong only to people with narcissistic pathology.

The difference between **criticism** and **humiliation** also matters. A criticism concerns an action, decision, product, or trait. Humiliation is an experience of being lowered, exposed, defeated, or rendered contemptible. The same sentence can function differently depending on tone, audience, relationship history, and the listener's state. It is therefore unsafe to infer narcissistic injury solely from the content of a remark.

Kohut helps ask whether the event threatened self-cohesion or a needed idealised/mirroring relation. Kernberg helps ask whether injury activated devaluation, envy, aggression, or a split between ideal and devalued self/other. Benjamin helps ask whether the person felt recognised as a subject or reduced to an object of judgement. Bowlby helps ask whether criticism also signalled threatened availability or abandonment. Fonagy and colleagues help ask whether shame collapsed the capacity to represent mental states, making the felt meaning seem unquestionably true.

A disciplined self-reflection might therefore be:

- What exactly happened?
- What did I immediately conclude it meant about me?
- Did I feel disappointed, ashamed, humiliated, abandoned, enraged, empty, or some combination?
- Did I move to restore self-esteem through withdrawal, attack, explanation, achievement, pleasing, or devaluing the other?
- What evidence supports my interpretation of the other's intent?
- What alternative explanations remain live?

## Sexuality, love and selfhood

Sexuality is kept brief here because the full treatment belongs in the later intimacy and couple file. It nevertheless reveals how the theories differ.

Freud made sexuality foundational and linked object choice, narcissism, identification, and idealisation. Later relational approaches do not need to deny sexual desire in order to reject drive reductionism. Mitchell (1988) argues that sexual experience can be understood within the relational matrix, carrying meanings about identity, attachment, power, fantasy, recognition, and continuity.

Kernberg (1995) gives sexuality an especially important place in mature love. Sexual passion can involve crossing the boundary of self while retaining enough identity to encounter a distinct other. Desire brings vulnerability because the other matters and cannot be fully controlled. Aggression, idealisation, dependency, tenderness, rivalry, and fantasy can all be activated. Mature love does not purify sexuality of aggression; it integrates aggression sufficiently that desire does not require degradation or destruction of the other.

Benjamin (1988) adds recognition. Erotic union can promise an intense experience of connection without requiring the disappearance of difference. The other is desired partly as an other. When recognition collapses, sexuality can be pulled into complementary structures of domination and submission, but Benjamin's broader point is not that power explains every erotic relation. It is that freedom, dependence, difference, and recognition are psychologically entangled.

The useful bridge to later couple reflection is therefore: sexual difficulty may concern desire, physiology, stress, opportunity, relationship conflict, shame, recognition, aggression, attachment, identity, or ordinary incompatibility. A psychoanalytic interpretation is one possible level, not a licence to ignore medical, situational, relational, or preference-based explanations.

## A disciplined relational method

The following sequence is a compact reflective heuristic. It is not a diagnostic algorithm and it does not establish developmental history.

**Event  
-> threat to self or attachment  
-> affect  
-> relational expectation  
-> role I move into  
-> role I invite or pressure the other to occupy  
-> actual response from the other  
-> my interpretation of that response  
-> resulting pattern**

The sequence should be slowed down rather than filled in automatically.

### 1. Event

Describe what an observer could have seen or heard. "They looked at their phone twice while I was speaking" is better data than "They dismissed me". Dismissal is already an interpretation.

### 2. Threat to self or attachment

Ask what the event seemed to endanger. Competence? desirability? moral worth? belonging? autonomy? availability of the other? being taken seriously? continuity of an idealised bond? Do not assume that one threat excludes another.

### 3. Affect

Name the closest experience available: hurt, fear, shame, anger, envy, grief, disgust, embarrassment, desire, relief, deadness. If the affect is unclear, uncertainty is legitimate.

### 4. Relational expectation

What did you predict would happen next? "If I ask directly, I will be rejected." "If I admit I need help, I will be diminished." "If I let them succeed, I will disappear." "If I disagree, they will leave." Treat these as hypotheses, not hidden truths.

### 5. Role I move into

Identify behaviour rather than identity labels: critic, rescuer, pleaser, silent withdrawer, interrogator, performer, helpless one, invulnerable one, pursuer, detached expert. Roles are situational organisations, not diagnoses.

### 6. Role I invite or pressure the other to occupy

What response does your move make easier or harder? Repeated checking can pressure another toward reassurance or withdrawal. Silence can pressure another toward pursuit or resignation. This question is about interactional probability, not causation or blame.

### 7. Actual response

Return to what the other really did. The response may fit the expectation, partly fit it, contradict it, or be ambiguous.

### 8. My interpretation

Distinguish the response from its attributed meaning. "They went quiet" is observable. "They wanted to punish me" is an inference.

### 9. Resulting pattern

Ask what the sequence achieved and cost. Did it restore self-esteem? preserve distance? gain reassurance? recreate a familiar hierarchy? avoid an unknown conversation? protect attachment? produce the feared outcome?

Then apply a mentalization check:

- What do I know?
- What am I inferring?
- What evidence supports my inference?
- What evidence contradicts it?
- What might the other person say happened?
- What remains unknown?

If the reflection ends with complete certainty about the other person's motives, return to the final question. The opacity of another mind is not a defect in analysis. It is a condition of responsible analysis.

## Worked examples

These examples are invented. Each deliberately includes competing formulations and an ordinary situational explanation. None is a diagnosis.

### Example 1: criticism after a presentation

**1. Observed event.** After a work presentation, Maya tells her partner, "I think the Q&A got away from you a little at the end." Maya's partner becomes quiet, later says the comment was cruel, and avoids discussing the presentation for the rest of the evening.

**2. Subjective experience.** The partner reports feeling suddenly exposed, fraudulent, and "small", not merely disappointed.

**3. Possible formulation from one framework.** A **Kohutian applied hypothesis** is that competence was carrying an important self-esteem or mirroring function. The comment may have been experienced as a narcissistic injury because it threatened a precariously organised sense of professional worth. Withdrawal may have helped restore cohesion by removing the partner from the source of injury.

**4. Competing formulation.** A **Kernbergian hypothesis** might ask whether the partner had been strongly idealising the presentation and could not easily integrate success with imperfection. The shift from expecting admiration to experiencing the speaker as cruel could represent a temporary idealisation/devaluation split rather than primarily a selfobject deficit.

**5. Ordinary situational explanation.** The person may have been exhausted after a stressful day and simply wanted comfort before feedback. Maya's timing may have been poor.

**6. What remains unknown.** Whether this reaction is recurrent, whether professional competence has special self-esteem significance, how Maya spoke, what feedback was actually wanted, and what happened in earlier relationships.

**7. Reflective questions.** What did I need at that moment: celebration, accurate feedback, reassurance, or space? Did I hear a comment about one performance as a judgement of my whole self? What evidence do I have about my partner's intention? What would repair look like without requiring either praise or self-erasure?

### Example 2: admiration that becomes psychological necessity

**1. Observed event.** Leo has long relied on his partner, Sam, to calm him before difficult decisions. When Sam is unusually anxious and says, "I don't know what we should do either," Leo becomes irritated and accuses Sam of being unreliable.

**2. Subjective experience.** Leo later describes not only worry about the decision but a strange sense that "the floor disappeared" when Sam was uncertain.

**3. Possible formulation from one framework.** A **Kohutian selfobject hypothesis** is that Sam's steadiness has been functioning as an idealising selfobject experience. Leo uses connection with Sam's calmness to organise his own. Sam's temporary uncertainty therefore disrupts more than advice. It disrupts a self-regulatory function.

**4. Competing formulation.** A **Bowlbian formulation** would ask whether the event activated an expectation that an attachment figure will not be available or capable when danger rises. The central issue may be accessibility and security rather than self-cohesion.

**5. Ordinary situational explanation.** The decision may genuinely be high stakes. Sam may usually carry more decision-making responsibility, so Leo's irritation may partly reflect a practical change in division of labour.

**6. What remains unknown.** Whether Leo can self-soothe in other contexts, whether Sam feels burdened by the role, whether the pattern appears only under acute stress, and whether Leo's anger masks fear.

**7. Reflective questions.** What function do I expect my partner to perform when I am uncertain? Can I appreciate their steadiness without requiring permanent steadiness? What happens in me when the person I idealise needs support too?

### Example 3: from admiration to devaluation after success

**1. Observed event.** Priya receives a promotion. Her partner, Alex, initially celebrates. Over the next week Alex repeatedly says the new role is "mostly politics", criticises Priya's colleagues, and complains that she is becoming self-important.

**2. Subjective experience.** Alex reports feeling left behind and ashamed of that feeling, while also insisting the promotion is objectively overrated.

**3. Possible formulation from one framework.** A **Kernbergian hypothesis** would ask whether admiration, envy, dependency, and aggression have become difficult to integrate. Devaluation may reduce the painful gap between Priya's success and Alex's self-evaluation. The relevant issue is not vanity but whether love and aggression toward the same person can remain integrated.

**4. Competing formulation.** A **Kohutian hypothesis** might ask whether Priya's promotion altered a mirroring or twinship equilibrium. If the couple had supported a sense of essential sameness, growing difference could threaten Alex's self-esteem or belonging.

**5. Ordinary situational explanation.** Priya may actually be working longer hours and speaking dismissively about Alex's work. Alex may be responding to a real change rather than primarily to envy.

**6. What remains unknown.** The quality of the relationship before the promotion, actual workload changes, whether Alex behaves similarly with friends, and Priya's contribution to the new tension.

**7. Reflective questions.** Can I admire the other without making their success evidence of my failure? What specific changes am I reacting to? Am I criticising real conduct, or has devaluation become a quick way to regulate shame or envy?

### Example 4: the pursuer and the detached expert

**1. Observed event.** During disagreements, Nina asks repeated questions about what her partner Omar is feeling. Omar responds with increasingly abstract explanations and says Nina is "being irrational". Nina becomes louder and more insistent; Omar becomes calmer in tone and more distant.

**2. Subjective experience.** Nina experiences Omar's calmness as abandonment. Omar experiences Nina's urgency as engulfing and believes detachment is the only way to keep the conversation safe.

**3. Possible formulation from one framework.** A **Mitchellian relational formulation** is that both are entering a familiar configuration. Nina moves toward urgent pursuit, implicitly assigning Omar the role of withholding other. Omar moves toward detached expertise, implicitly assigning Nina the role of uncontrolled emotional other. Each actual response gives the other's expectation more credibility. This is an enactment hypothesis, not a claim that either consciously produces it.

**4. Competing formulation.** A **Bowlby/Fonagy bridge** might emphasise attachment activation and mentalization collapse. Nina may experience distance as threatened unavailability; Omar may experience intensity as threat. Under arousal, both become certain about the other's motive and less able to represent alternative mental states.

**5. Ordinary situational explanation.** They may simply have incompatible conflict styles and be discussing difficult issues at the wrong time of day.

**6. What remains unknown.** Whether one partner is withholding relevant facts, whether there is coercion or fear, how each behaves in other relationships, and whether both want the same outcome.

**7. Reflective questions.** What happens at the exact point the conversation turns? What role do I move into? What response does that make more likely? Can I describe the other's behaviour without assigning motive? Is anyone unsafe or unable to disengage freely?

### Example 5: recognition failure in an intimate decision

**1. Observed event.** Jordan wants to move cities for a career opportunity. Their partner, Elise, says she cannot move this year because of commitments to family. Jordan responds, "If you really believed in us, you'd come." Elise replies, "You only care about yourself." The conversation becomes a contest over whose commitment is more legitimate.

**2. Subjective experience.** Jordan experiences Elise's refusal as proof that the relationship is secondary. Elise experiences Jordan's request as erasing her independent obligations and priorities.

**3. Possible formulation from one framework.** A **Benjaminian hypothesis** is that mutual recognition has collapsed into complementarity. Each person can preserve their own subject position only by reducing the other to an object: the disloyal partner or the selfish careerist. The problem is not disagreement itself but difficulty recognising that two valid centres of desire can conflict.

**4. Competing formulation.** A **Mitchellian hypothesis** might ask whether each is entering a familiar configuration around sacrifice and autonomy. Perhaps one expects love to be proved by self-denial while the other expects intimacy to threaten freedom.

**5. Ordinary situational explanation.** The opportunity may simply create a genuine incompatibility. No deeper pattern is required to explain why the decision is painful.

**6. What remains unknown.** Whether compromise is practical, what each has sacrificed before, the time horizon of the move, and whether the relationship can survive two legitimate but incompatible priorities.

**7. Reflective questions.** Can I state my desire without turning your different desire into a defect? What would it mean to recognise your position without agreeing to it? Is this a pattern to repair or a real incompatibility to decide about?

### Example 6: attachment alarm and loss of mentalization

**1. Observed event.** Chris sends a message to their partner at 6 p.m. and receives no reply until 10:30 p.m. During the gap Chris sends six more messages, checks social media, and concludes the partner is deliberately punishing them. The partner later says they were at a work dinner with a flat phone battery.

**2. Subjective experience.** Chris reports escalating anxiety, anger, and certainty that the silence meant rejection.

**3. Possible formulation from one framework.** A **Bowlbian hypothesis** is that unavailability activated the attachment system strongly. The absence of response became a signal of threatened accessibility rather than a neutral information gap.

**4. Competing formulation.** A **Fonagy et al. hypothesis** is that high arousal reduced mentalization. The felt fear of rejection moved toward psychic equivalence: "I feel abandoned, therefore I am being abandoned." Alternative explanations became less representable.

**5. Ordinary situational explanation.** The couple may have an explicit agreement to message when plans change, and the partner may have broken it. Chris's concern could therefore contain a reasonable grievance even if the inferred motive was wrong.

**6. What remains unknown.** The couple's communication norms, any history of unexplained absence, the partner's actual options, and how often Chris becomes certain of hostile motives under uncertainty.

**7. Reflective questions.** What fact did I have, and what story did I add? What did I need to know to feel safe? What alternative explanations were available? Can I address the broken expectation, if there was one, without claiming certainty about intent?

## Common mistakes and misuses

| Misuse | Correction |
|---|---|
| **"Narcissism = vanity."** | Narcissism in psychoanalytic theory concerns self-investment, self-regard, self-cohesion, identity, ideals, and relational regulation. Vanity is only one possible surface form. |
| **"Narcissistic injury = feeling criticised."** | The concept concerns the meaning and force of an injury to self-esteem or cohesion. Ordinary hurt or criticism is not enough. |
| **"A selfobject is a supportive person."** | Selfobject describes the function or experience of another in maintaining or developing the self, not the person's general goodness. |
| **"Mirroring = praise."** | Mirroring concerns responsive participation in the self's aliveness and value. Praise can be mirroring, empty flattery, accurate feedback, or something else. |
| **"Empathy = agreement."** | Empathy is an attempt to understand subjective experience. Agreement is sharing a conclusion. |
| **"Kohut and Kernberg say the same thing."** | Kohut foregrounds self-cohesion, developmental failure, selfobject functions, and empathy. Kernberg foregrounds internalised object relations, aggression, splitting, identity integration, and conflict. |
| **"Relational = interpersonal only."** | Mitchell's relational matrix explicitly includes intrapsychic organisation, fantasy, internalised relations, actual transactions, and reciprocal influence. |
| **"Enactment = manipulation."** | Enactment can be largely unplanned reciprocal participation in a relational pattern. Manipulation implies more deliberate influence. |
| **"Recognition = validation."** | Benjaminian recognition requires encountering the other as another subject, not only affirming the other's experience. |
| **"Mutuality = symmetry at all times."** | Mutuality can exist with temporary asymmetry. The issue is whether both subjectivities can count. |
| **"Attachment = dependency."** | Attachment is an organised proximity and security system involving particular figures and conditions. Dependency is much broader. |
| **"Secure attachment = never needing reassurance."** | Bowlby's secure base model predicts care-seeking under threat and greater exploration when availability can be trusted. |
| **"Mentalization = analysing someone's motives."** | Mentalization includes uncertainty, multiple possible mental states, and awareness that representations can be wrong. |
| **"Mentalization = intellectualisation."** | Elaborate psychological explanation can itself avoid affect. Mentalization requires representational contact with lived mental states. |
| **"If a pattern is relational, both people are equally responsible."** | Reciprocal influence does not allocate equal moral or causal responsibility. Harm, coercion, and power must be assessed directly. |
| **"If I understand a pattern, I should excuse it."** | Understanding and accountability answer different questions. Explanation does not cancel boundaries or consequences. |

A further misuse deserves emphasis: psychoanalytic vocabulary can become a status weapon. Calling a partner "narcissistic", "avoidant", "projective", "unmentalizing", or "unable to recognise me" can end inquiry rather than deepen it. The corpus is more useful when concepts generate discriminating questions than when they generate verdicts.

## Limits and unresolved questions

The traditions covered here leave major disagreements unresolved.

First, **what is primary in motivation?** Freud's drive model gives sexuality and aggression a foundational metapsychological role. Bowlby gives attachment its own motivational status. Kohut focuses on development of the self through selfobject experience. Mitchell resists reducing relational life to any single superordinate motive. These cannot simply be added together without conceptual cost.

Second, **what causes narcissistic pathology?** Kohut's model emphasises deficits, developmental failure, and precarious self-structure. Kernberg's model emphasises pathological organisation, aggression, splitting, envy, and failures of integration. McWilliams' secondary synthesis is useful precisely because she does not pretend the dispute has been settled for all patients.

Third, **how much can developmental history be reconstructed from adult experience?** All these traditions make developmental claims, but adult relational patterns do not provide a video recording of infancy. Developmental interpretations should remain hypotheses supported by converging evidence, not retrospective certainty.

Fourth, **how much reciprocity is psychologically explanatory?** Relational theory usefully shows that interaction can stabilise expectations. But actual behaviour, social context, power, illness, economics, caregiving burdens, discrimination, work pressures, and random events also shape relationships. Not every repeated pattern is an enactment.

Fifth, **what is the status of psychoanalytic evidence?** Much of the primary psychoanalytic literature is clinical and interpretive rather than experimental. Bowlby and Fonagy and colleagues deliberately build stronger bridges to developmental research, but those bridges do not retrospectively prove every object-relations, self-psychological, or relational proposition.

Sixth, **how universal are the developmental models?** The sources often emerge from Western clinical settings and can carry assumptions about family form, autonomy, gender, sexuality, and normative development. Benjamin directly interrogates gender and domination, but no single source in this file resolves the cultural problem.

Finally, **what counts as enough change?** Kohut may emphasise stronger self-cohesion; Kernberg, greater integration and ambivalence; Mitchell, more flexible relational organisation; Benjamin, mutual recognition; Bowlby, greater security and exploration; Fonagy and colleagues, more robust mentalization. These outcomes overlap but are not identical. A person may improve along one dimension while remaining constrained along another.

## Connections to the rest of the corpus

This file sits between the corpus's accounts of classical theory and object relations, and its later applied work on intimacy and reflection.

- The dedicated classical file develops Freud's dynamic unconscious, conflict, repression, structural theory, and drive model more fully. Here Freud is used only to establish the narcissism, ego ideal, identification, object-choice, and self-regard problems that later theorists transform.
- The defence file provides the fuller treatment of defence mechanisms. Here splitting, idealisation/devaluation, withdrawal, and related processes appear only where needed to distinguish theories of narcissism and relational patterning.
- The object-relations file develops Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, and Bion in their own terms. Here they appear only as bridges into Kernberg, Mitchell, Benjamin, selfhood, and dependence.
- The later couple and intimacy file can use the present distinctions to analyse recognition, self-esteem regulation, attachment alarm, sexuality, aggression, dependency, complementarity, and enactment without collapsing them into one master theory.
- The final reflective-method file can expand the compact heuristic in this file into a more complete practice of self-observation, competing hypotheses, evidence testing, partner opacity, accountability, and outcome neutrality.

The most important cross-corpus rule is framework non-collapse. Attachment does not prove object relations. Mentalization is not a modern synonym for interpretation. Recognition is not mirroring. Selfobject need is not attachment need. Relational reciprocity does not erase intrapsychic conflict or actual external behaviour.

## Questions this framework helps you ask

The following questions are designed for reflection rather than diagnosis.

### About the self

- What exactly feels threatened here: competence, worth, desirability, moral identity, continuity, belonging, or something else?
- Is this ordinary disappointment, or does the event seem to destabilise my whole sense of self?
- What response from the other am I relying on to restore equilibrium?
- Can I receive affirmation without requiring agreement or idealisation?
- Can I tolerate being imperfect without becoming worthless in my own eyes?

### About admiration, idealisation, and envy

- What do I admire in the other, and can I value it without needing to possess or diminish it?
- When the other succeeds, can admiration and envy coexist without devaluation?
- Do I need the other to remain stronger than me, weaker than me, or essentially the same as me?
- What happens when an idealised person shows uncertainty or limitation?

### About recognition

- Am I relating to the other as a separate subject, or mainly through the role I need them to occupy?
- Can I recognise the other's experience without agreeing with their conclusion?
- Can I assert what I want while allowing the other to want something incompatible?
- Has the relationship collapsed into critic/criticised, controller/controlled, pursuer/withdrawer, rescuer/dependent, or another complementary pair?
- Can the positions change without the relationship becoming unsafe or annihilating?

### About attachment

- Under stress, what do I expect will happen if I seek closeness or reassurance?
- Can I move between connection and exploration, or does one seem to threaten the other?
- Is my current alarm proportionate to present availability, or is uncertainty rapidly becoming certainty about abandonment?
- What explicit communication agreement actually exists between us?

### About relational patterns

- What is the observable sequence, step by step?
- What role do I repeatedly move into?
- What response does that role invite or make more likely?
- What does the familiar pattern preserve: identity, connection, certainty, superiority, helplessness, distance, hope, or safety?
- What genuinely new response would feel psychologically risky?
- Am I using a relational formulation to minimise something the other person actually did?

### About mentalization

- What do I know, and what am I inferring?
- How certain am I about the other person's motive?
- What evidence would change my mind?
- What would the other person say the sequence looked like?
- What alternative mental states could explain the same behaviour?
- Am I using psychological sophistication to avoid feeling something directly?

### About responsibility and choice

- What part of this pattern is mine to change even if I did not create the whole situation?
- What behaviour from the other is outside my responsibility and may require a boundary rather than interpretation?
- Does understanding this pattern support repair, renegotiation, distance, or simply clearer decision-making?
- Am I assuming that insight obliges reconciliation? It does not.
