# Applied Self-Analysis and Relationship Reflection

## Purpose of this file

This file is the applied synthesis of the Freudian and post-Freudian corpus. Its purpose is to turn theory into a disciplined method for examining one's own experience, recurring patterns and intimate relationships without converting psychoanalytic concepts into diagnoses, verdicts or claims of privileged access to another person's mind.

The basic stance is practical. Start with what happened. Then separate what was felt from what was inferred. Ask what impulse appeared, what protective operation may have followed, what meaning the event acquired, and whether a familiar relational expectation became active. Only after that should a psychodynamic hypothesis be considered. Even then, it remains a hypothesis. The other person's actual behaviour, practical circumstances, bodily factors, explicit agreements, consent, power and external reality remain fully relevant.

Psychoanalytic reflection is most useful when it increases freedom. It should help a person notice a pattern before automatically repeating it, tolerate contradictory feelings, ask more directly for what is needed, distinguish injury from interpretation, communicate in ordinary language, and make clearer decisions about repair, boundaries or incompatibility. It is least useful when it becomes a way to label a partner, reconstruct childhood with false certainty, explain away mistreatment, or postpone an obvious decision.

The method draws on Freud's conflict model and account of repetition; Anna Freud and McWilliams on defence; Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott and Bion on the internal world, integration, painful relational ties, dependence and thinking; Kohut and Kernberg on self-esteem and narcissistic vulnerability; Mitchell on relational configurations; Benjamin on recognition; Bowlby on attachment; Fonagy and colleagues on mentalization; and psychoanalytic couple literature on reciprocal loops, shared beliefs, sexuality and the couple relationship as a field. These frameworks are compared rather than merged. They do not all make the same causal claims.

This is not a therapy manual, a diagnostic instrument, a memory-recovery method, or a procedure for discovering the hidden truth about another person. It is a framework for reflective inquiry.

Three operating rules should govern every use of the file:

1. **Self before partner.** Begin with your own affect, interpretation, impulse and behaviour. Another person's internal state is less directly accessible.
2. **Reality before depth.** A delayed reply may be a delayed reply. A criticism may be unfair. A boundary may be necessary. A medical or practical explanation may be more persuasive than a psychodynamic one.
3. **Hypotheses in competition.** A useful formulation should survive comparison with alternatives. Depth is not the same as truth.

## The epistemic constitution

Applied psychoanalytic reflection becomes unreliable when different kinds of statement are collapsed. The following five levels must remain separate.

| Level | Question | What belongs here | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Observation** | What actually happened? | Words, actions, timing, sequence, circumstances, agreements, observable behaviour | "My partner answered my message four hours later." |
| **2. Subjective experience** | What did I experience? | Affect, body sensation, urge, thought, image, fantasy, desire, sense of threat | "I felt anxious, then angry. My chest tightened and I wanted to send another message." |
| **3. Interpretation** | What meaning did I give the event? | The story or conclusion attached to the event | "I am not important to them." |
| **4. Psychodynamic hypothesis** | What theory might help explain the intensity or pattern? | Defence, transference, repetition, splitting, internal object, self-esteem injury, attachment alarm | "My current hypothesis is that the delay activated a familiar expectation of being left." |
| **5. Relational/systemic hypothesis** | How might interaction reinforce the pattern? | Reciprocal roles, interpersonal pressure, partner response, feedback loop, couple belief | "My repeated checking may increase their withdrawal, which then confirms my fear." |

These levels answer different questions. Level 1 is closest to public evidence. Level 2 reports first-person experience. Level 3 is a meaning-making act. Levels 4 and 5 are increasingly inferential. Moving upward can add explanatory power, but it also increases uncertainty.

Three principles are mandatory:

> **Experience is real even when an interpretation is uncertain.**

> **A psychologically plausible story is still a hypothesis.**

> **The purpose of reflection is to increase freedom and complexity, not to produce a definitive hidden explanation.**

Several further safeguards follow.

- A feeling does not prove its own cause. Feeling rejected is real; it does not establish that rejection was intended.
- An interpretation can be partly right and still incomplete. A partner may really be irritated, while one's own history amplifies the meaning of that irritation.
- A psychodynamic formulation does not outrank an ordinary explanation merely because it sounds deeper.
- Reciprocal influence does not imply equal responsibility. A loop can be maintained by two people while responsibility for deception, intimidation, coercion or violence remains highly asymmetrical.
- Developmental stories are reconstructions, not recovered historical facts.
- A theory should be revisable. If no possible evidence could count against the explanation, it has stopped functioning as an inquiry.

A useful language rule is to replace **"The real reason is..."** with **"My current hypothesis is..."**. The first closes the question. The second keeps evidence and alternatives in view.

## The core reflective sequence

The central practical framework is:

**Event  
→ perception  
→ affect  
→ body response  
→ impulse  
→ defence  
→ fantasy/meaning  
→ relational expectation  
→ behaviour  
→ partner response  
→ interpretation of partner response  
→ feedback loop  
→ alternative hypotheses  
→ possible different response**

This is an audit trail for reflection, not a claim that mental life always unfolds in a neat linear order. Affect, perception, defence and meaning often develop almost simultaneously. The value of the sequence is that it forces distinctions which are otherwise lost in the speed of an argument.

| Step | What to record | Discipline |
|---|---|---|
| **Event** | What was said or done, when, and in what context? | Remove motive words such as "ignored", "punished", "controlled" unless behaviour directly establishes them. |
| **Perception** | What did you notice or select from the event? | Perception is already selective. Note what captured attention. |
| **Affect** | What did you feel first and then next? | Allow mixed and contradictory feelings. |
| **Body response** | Tension, heat, numbness, agitation, collapse, sexual arousal, urge to escape or attack | Track arousal without turning it into a neurological theory. |
| **Impulse** | What did you want to do? | An urge is not an action and not a moral verdict. |
| **Defence** | What may have changed, muted, redirected or reversed experience? | Treat the defence as inferred and functional. |
| **Fantasy/meaning** | What did the event seem to say about you, the other or the relationship? | Separate conscious thought from deeper inferred fantasy. |
| **Relational expectation** | What did you expect the other person to do next? | Ask whether the expectation is current evidence, familiarity, or both. |
| **Behaviour** | What did you actually do? | Use observable verbs: asked, withdrew, criticised, reassured, checked, apologised. |
| **Partner response** | What did the other actually do or report? | Do not fill in their internal state unless they told you. |
| **Interpretation of response** | What meaning did you assign to their response? | Again separate observation from motive attribution. |
| **Feedback loop** | How did your next move and theirs intensify or stabilise the sequence? | Look for maintenance, not only origin. |
| **Alternative hypotheses** | What else could explain the same sequence? | Include psychodynamic, systemic, ordinary and disconfirming alternatives. |
| **Possible different response** | What is one action that would add freedom rather than repeat the automatic move? | It may be communication, waiting, a boundary, repair, or a decision. |

A compact retrieval version is:

> **What happened? What did I feel in body and mind? What did I want to do? What became hard to feel or admit? What did I make the event mean? What did I expect? What did I do? What did the other actually do? What loop followed? What else could explain it? What could I do differently now?**


## Choosing the right reflection tool

The file contains several tools because different situations require different depth. They are not stages that must all be completed. Use the lightest tool that is sufficient.

| Level | Use | Default tool |
|---|---|---|
| **FAST** | One ordinary charged event; roughly 10 minutes | `The 10-minute reflection protocol` |
| **STANDARD** | A complete but bounded review of one event or interaction | `A compact master worksheet` |
| **DEEP** | A repeated, consequential or still-confusing pattern; roughly 30 minutes | `The 30-minute deep reflection protocol` |
| **OPTIONAL MODULES** | Use only when a particular domain is central | defence; repetition; internal objects; self/self-esteem; attachment; mentalization; sexuality; repair |
| **HYPOTHESIS TEST** | Apply before treating any important interpretation as a working conclusion | multiple explanations + disconfirming evidence |

A recognition or jealousy inquiry can be added when those issues are central, but neither requires running every other module. The aim is disciplined reflection, not maximal analysis. If FAST produces enough clarity to communicate or act, stop there. If STANDARD shows a repeated or consequential pattern that remains unclear, move to DEEP. Use optional modules selectively, then finish with the Hypothesis Test whenever the interpretation matters.

## Start with what happened

The most important discipline is to delay explanation long enough to establish the event.

Compare three statements:

**Observation**

> "My partner answered my message four hours later."

**Interpretation**

> "My partner ignored me."

**Psychodynamic explanation**

> "Their withdrawal is a defence against intimacy."

The first statement is relatively close to what an observer could verify. The second attributes intention and meaning. The third adds a theory of unconscious motivation. Each may eventually prove useful, but they carry different evidentiary weight.

The same distinction applies elsewhere:

| Observation | Interpretation | Psychodynamic hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| "They looked at their phone twice while I was speaking." | "They were bored with me." | "Distance may protect them from emotional dependence." |
| "I received no invitation to the meeting." | "My colleagues do not respect me." | "Exclusion may be touching a self-esteem vulnerability organised around being overlooked." |
| "My partner declined sex twice this week." | "They no longer desire me." | "Sexual distance may be carrying resentment or fear of closeness." |
| "I became quiet after the criticism." | "I shut down because I cannot handle conflict." | "Withdrawal may be protecting me from shame or retaliatory anger." |
| "They raised their voice and called me incompetent." | "They were angry and contemptuous." | A deeper explanation may be unnecessary before addressing the observable disrespect. |

A useful event record answers five questions: What happened? What happened immediately before? What happened immediately after? What explicit agreement or context mattered? What words in my description already imply motive?

Removing interpretation from the event does not make reflection emotionally sterile. It protects the later interpretation from being built into the evidence that is supposed to support it.

## Affect before explanation

Affect should be identified before theory. Psychoanalytic sophistication can become a way of leaving the actual feeling untouched. McWilliams emphasises curiosity, affective contact and uncertainty about content; Bion's work similarly points toward learning from emotional experience rather than evacuating it into immediate certainty or action.

Common affects relevant to relationship reflection include:

- anger;
- sadness;
- fear;
- shame;
- guilt;
- envy;
- jealousy;
- disgust;
- longing;
- loneliness;
- excitement;
- sexual desire;
- helplessness;
- resentment.

Affect is often mixed. Love can coexist with anger. Desire can coexist with resentment. Jealousy can contain fear, rivalry and injured self-esteem. Relief after separation can coexist with grief. Ambivalence is not evidence that one feeling is false. Freud, Klein, Winnicott and Kernberg, in different theoretical languages, all make the capacity to tolerate contradictory affect psychologically important.

Use six questions:

1. What did I feel first?
2. What feeling became dominant?
3. What feeling disappeared?
4. What feeling would be hardest to admit?
5. What bodily sensation accompanied it?
6. What action urge appeared?

The order matters. A person may first feel hurt, then quickly become angry. Another may first feel anger, then collapse into shame. Someone may feel envy and cover it with moral criticism. But no sequence should be assumed in advance.

Anger is a particularly important example. In a defensive formulation, anger can protect against shame, grief, dependency, helplessness or longing. In an attachment formulation, anger can function as protest when a valued bond feels threatened. In a reality-based formulation, anger may simply be justified anger at unfair treatment. The correct starting question is not "What is anger really hiding?" but "What else, if anything, was present before or alongside the anger?"

A useful affect record is concrete: **"Fear 6/10, anger 8/10, shame 3/10; tight jaw, hot face; urge to send a cutting reply."** The numbers are not measurements of psychic truth. They are a way to slow down and differentiate states that otherwise merge into one global reaction.

## Impulse, fantasy and behaviour

Feeling, impulse, fantasy and behaviour are not the same thing.

- **Feeling:** an affective state, such as anger, fear, longing or jealousy.
- **Impulse or action urge:** a movement toward doing something, such as withdrawing, checking, demanding reassurance, attacking, apologising or pursuing.
- **Fantasy:** an imagined scene, possibility or meaning, conscious or inferred, such as imagining retaliation, abandonment, rescue or perfect reunion.
- **Behaviour:** what was actually done.

The distinctions create reflective freedom.

Wanting to withdraw is not the same as withdrawing. Imagining retaliation is not the same as acting aggressively. Wanting reassurance is not the same as demanding it. Feeling sexual desire is not the same as initiating sex, and initiating sex does not create an entitlement to consent.

An impulse can be informative without being obeyed. It may reveal what the situation has become psychologically. The urge to interrogate a partner can indicate anxiety and uncertainty. The urge to disappear after criticism can indicate shame or fear. The urge to criticise can coexist with a wish for recognition. The reflective task is to notice the urge before it becomes the only available response.

Three questions help:

1. What did I want to do immediately?
2. What outcome did I imagine that action would produce?
3. What did I fear would happen if I did not act on the urge?

The third question leads directly to defence inquiry.

## Defence inquiry

A defence is a psychological operation that makes a difficult internal situation more manageable. Anna Freud placed the ego's defensive activity at the centre of observation. McWilliams later emphasises that defences begin as ordinary, adaptive ways of organising experience and remain part of normal functioning. The practical issue is not whether a defence is "bad". It is what the operation protects, whether it fits present reality, how flexible it is, and what it costs.

Use this eight-question inquiry:

1. What feeling or thought became difficult?
2. What happened next internally?
3. What became easier to feel?
4. What became harder to feel?
5. What did the defence protect?
6. What did it cost?
7. Was it proportionate to the situation?
8. What alternative explanation fits?

The following table is a prompt for inquiry, not a self-diagnosis checklist.

| Possible defence | Applied description | Useful question | Common misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Repression** | Something conflictual remains outside ordinary awareness while continuing to have effects | What pattern suggests more than ordinary forgetting or inattention? | Treating every forgotten memory as repression |
| **Denial** | Recognition of a painful reality is limited or warded off | What evidence am I unable or unwilling to register? | Equating denial with conscious lying |
| **Projection** | A difficult quality, feeling or impulse may be experienced primarily as belonging to another | What evidence supports my attribution, and do I recognise any version of it in myself? | Calling any inaccurate judgement "projection" |
| **Reaction formation** | An unacceptable feeling or impulse may be organised around its opposite | Is the opposite position unusually rigid or compulsory, and can both feelings coexist? | Treating strong affection as proof of hidden hatred |
| **Displacement** | Affect is redirected from one target or situation to another that is safer or more available | Am I reacting here to something I could not express elsewhere? | Ignoring genuine reasons for anger at the current target |
| **Rationalisation** | A plausible conscious explanation may cover additional motives or conflicts | Does my explanation account for the timing, intensity and repetition? | Assuming any reasoned explanation is defensive |
| **Intellectualisation** | Abstract analysis creates distance from lived affect | Is my thinking helping me feel and decide, or replacing feeling and decision? | Equating intelligence or conceptual interest with defence |
| **Isolation of affect** | Thought and event remain accessible while feeling is detached | Can I describe the event in detail while feeling strangely absent from it? | Assuming calmness always means defensive isolation |
| **Turning against the self** | Anger, blame or aggression may be directed inward | Does global self-attack replace specific responsibility or anger toward someone else? | Treating appropriate self-criticism as pathology |
| **Regression** | Under stress, capacities for delay, self-soothing or reflection become less available | Which capacities temporarily disappeared, and do they return when threat falls? | Calling ordinary support-seeking childish |
| **Splitting** | Contradictory good and bad aspects become difficult to hold together | Can I remember valued qualities while angry and real harm while loving? | Using "splitting" for inconsistency or disagreement |
| **Idealisation / devaluation** | Value becomes exaggerated upward or downward to regulate dependence, disappointment, envy or self-esteem | Can I value the person realistically, with limitations and strengths present at once? | Treating admiration or criticism itself as pathological |

A defence can be adaptive in one context and costly in another. Intellectualisation can create enough distance to function during a crisis, then later become a barrier to grief. Withdrawal can prevent an argument from escalating, then become harmful if it is used as prolonged punishment. Projection can attach to something real; a person's own anger does not prove that the other person is not angry too.

The most useful endpoint is not **"I have identified my defence."** It is: **"I can see more of the feeling, wish, fear and reality that the protective move was helping me manage."**

## What would I feel if I did not do this?

This is one of the most efficient questions in the whole framework:

> **What would I feel if I did not do this?**

The "this" can be a behaviour, a thought pattern, a defence or a relational role.

- If I did not become angry, would I feel ashamed?
- If I did not withdraw, would I feel dependent?
- If I did not criticise, would I have to admit longing?
- If I did not explain everything intellectually, would I feel grief?
- If I did not devalue the other person, would I have to tolerate disappointment?
- If I did not reassure everyone, would I have to admit that I am resentful?
- If I did not keep checking, would I have to tolerate uncertainty?
- If I did not condemn myself, would I have to acknowledge anger at someone I love?

The question is powerful because it reverses attention from the visible action to the state the action may be preventing. It also works without a technical label. One need not know whether a behaviour is technically repression, rationalisation or displacement to ask what becomes more emotionally exposed if the behaviour stops.

The caution is equally important:

> **The question generates possibilities. It does not prove what is being defended against.**

A person may withdraw simply because a conversation is unproductive. Criticism may be accurate. Intellectual analysis may be genuinely clarifying. The hypothesis earns weight only if it fits affect, timing, repetition, context and disconfirming evidence better than alternatives.

## Fantasy and meaning

Events do not arrive psychologically as neutral data. They acquire meaning. Freud's idea of psychical reality, Klein's unconscious phantasy and later relational formulations all make this point in different ways. A fantasy can be psychologically consequential without being an accurate report of external reality.

Ask:

- What did this event seem to say about me?
- What did it seem to say about the other person?
- What did it seem to say about the relationship?
- What outcome did I immediately imagine?
- What role did I feel placed in?
- What role did I assign to the other person?

Common meanings include:

- "I am unimportant."
- "I am trapped."
- "I am too much."
- "I cannot rely on anyone."
- "If I need someone, I will lose power."
- "If they disagree, they no longer love me."
- "If I am not admired, I am defective."
- "If I do not manage everything, it will collapse."

A **conscious thought** is directly available: "I thought they were rejecting me." A **deeper fantasy** is an inferred organising scenario: "Perhaps disagreement becomes organised for me as abandonment." The second requires more evidence and more caution.

Fantasy should never be used to erase external reality. "I am imagining betrayal" and "my partner has concealed a second relationship" are very different situations. A psychologically meaningful fear can coexist with a real breach of trust.

## Repetition inquiry

Repeated painful patterns are a central psychoanalytic problem, but different theories explain them differently. The observation of repetition should be separated from any one account of why it occurs.

Use this inquiry:

- Have I felt this emotional configuration before?
- What role do I repeatedly occupy?
- What kind of other person do I repeatedly expect?
- What do I repeatedly fear?
- What do I repeatedly do that may help recreate the outcome?
- What familiarity does the pattern preserve?
- What would be unfamiliar about a different outcome?
- Is the current situation actually similar, or does it only feel similar?

A required safeguard is:

> **Repetition does not mean secretly wanting a painful outcome.**

Several competing theories can be kept alive:

| Lens | What repetition may mean | Applied question |
|---|---|---|
| **Freud** | Painful sequences may recur through repetition and transference even when conscious intention seeks something else. Freud's later death-drive explanation remains more speculative than the observation itself. | Am I repeating in action what I have not yet been able to represent or work through? |
| **Fairbairn** | A painful relational form may preserve attachment to an internal object and a familiar mode of connection. | What bond or role does this painful pattern keep alive? |
| **Mitchell** | Repetition can preserve continuity, familiarity and identity within a relational matrix. | What known way of being with another does this pattern protect me from having to relinquish? |
| **Attachment** | Under threat, working expectations about availability may organise proximity-seeking, protest, withdrawal or detachment. | Does this situation activate a familiar expectation about whether care will be available? |
| **Couple theory** | Each partner's protective response can become part of the other's environment, stabilising a recurring loop or shared belief. | What does each person's response make more likely next? |

A repetition claim requires pattern evidence. Three similar incidents over years carry more weight than one intense argument. Cross-relationship repetition may strengthen the hypothesis, but it is still not proof of a specific childhood origin.

A useful final question is: **What would be psychologically unfamiliar about a different outcome?** A person accustomed to pursuing unavailable partners may find dependable reciprocity strangely flat. Someone used to being indispensable may feel unimportant when a partner becomes more capable. Someone who expects criticism may mistrust kindness. The unfamiliarity does not mean the healthier alternative is wrong. It may simply lack the emotional familiarity of the old configuration.

## Internal-object inquiry

Object-relations theory asks not only what the other person is doing, but what version of the other person has become psychologically active. An internal object is not the actual partner. It is a relational configuration shaped by experience, affect, fantasy and expectation.

Ask:

- What version of the other person is active in my mind?
- Are they suddenly all-good or all-bad?
- What qualities have become impossible to remember?
- What familiar relational role have they acquired?
- Is my internal picture consistent with their actual behaviour?
- What evidence complicates my picture?

This inquiry is especially useful under intense disappointment or anger. A partner who was experienced an hour ago as loving may suddenly be experienced as entirely selfish. That shift may reflect real new information, a split mode of perception, or both. The purpose of the inquiry is not to force a positive view. It is to restore complexity where affect has narrowed the field.

Fairbairn adds a second question: **What relation am I preserving by keeping this version of the other alive?** A rejecting internal other can sustain a self organised around pursuit. An exciting but unavailable other can preserve longing. A dependable present partner may challenge that familiar structure.

Do not use this inquiry to invent childhood history. "This feels like being dismissed" is a present observation about experience. "Therefore my mother dismissed me in infancy" is an unsupported reconstruction unless there is independent evidence.

## Splitting and integration check

Klein's concept of integration is more useful in reflection than using "splitting" as a label. The practical test is whether contradictory experience can be held at the same time.

Ask:

- Can I remember what I value about this person while angry?
- Can I acknowledge genuine harm without making the whole person bad?
- Can I acknowledge love without denying disappointment?
- Can I see my own contribution without erasing theirs?
- Can I hold two conflicting feelings at once?

Integration does not mean minimising harm. It does not require reconciliation, forgiveness or continuation of the relationship. A person can hold a complex view of someone and still conclude that their conduct is unacceptable or that the relationship should end.

A practical integration statement might be: **"I love this person and I am deeply angry about what they did. Their good qualities do not erase the breach, and the breach does not require me to rewrite the entire history as false."**

That differs from forced positivity. Integration permits a bad fact to remain bad.

## Projective-process inquiry

Projection and projective identification are among the easiest psychoanalytic concepts to misuse. The safest method is to move slowly from attribution to evidence and then to interaction.

Use this sequence:

1. What feeling or quality am I attributing to the other?
2. What evidence supports that attribution?
3. Is there evidence of the opposite?
4. Do I recognise this feeling or quality in myself?
5. Did my behaviour place interpersonal pressure on the other?
6. How did the other actually respond?
7. Did their response then confirm my original expectation?
8. What alternative loop could explain the same interaction?

Klein's original projective identification concerns an intrapsychic phantasy in which split-off parts of the self are experienced as put into the object. Bion and later couple theorists extend the idea toward communication and interpersonal pressure. These levels should not be collapsed.

For example, if I enter a conversation convinced that my partner is hostile, I may speak defensively and sharply. My partner may then become irritated. Their irritation is real, but the sequence does not prove that they were originally carrying my projected hostility. It shows only that my expectation, behaviour and their independent response may have formed a confirming loop.

Never conclude:

> "I know they are carrying my projected feeling."

A better formulation is:

> "One possibility is that I entered the interaction expecting hostility and behaved in a way that made irritation more likely. I still need to assess what evidence existed before I reacted."

## Self-esteem and narcissistic vulnerability

Narcissism should not be used as a synonym for vanity or selfishness. Freud, Kohut and Kernberg use the problem differently, and McWilliams emphasises dimensional and contextual formulation rather than remote labelling.

For applied reflection, ask:

- What did this event imply about my worth?
- Did I feel humiliated, ignored, inferior, exposed or replaceable?
- Was I seeking recognition, admiration, reassurance or understanding?
- Did criticism become evidence that I am defective?
- Did disappointment lead me to devalue the other?
- Did I need the other person to restore my self-esteem?
- What would it be like to tolerate the injury without retaliating or collapsing?

A Kohutian hypothesis asks whether the event threatened self-cohesion, vitality or a needed selfobject function. A Kernbergian hypothesis asks whether shame, envy, aggression, idealisation or devaluation became difficult to integrate with love and realistic perception. These are not interchangeable explanations.

Distinguish four levels:

1. **Ordinary hurt:** a criticism, rejection or disappointment feels bad.
2. **Shame or humiliation:** the event becomes a judgement of the self as exposed, deficient or lowered.
3. **Narcissistic vulnerability:** self-esteem or cohesion is unusually dependent on external regulation in the situation.
4. **Broader narcissistic organisation:** a more pervasive pattern involving identity, self-esteem regulation and relationships. This cannot be inferred from one event and should not be used as a self-label or partner label.

A practical aim is not to become indifferent to recognition. Human self-esteem is relational. The aim is greater flexibility: being able to receive admiration without needing it continuously, survive disagreement without global collapse, and distinguish a specific criticism from a verdict on the whole self.

## Recognition inquiry

Benjamin's concept of recognition asks whether two people can remain subjects to one another. Recognition is not agreement, validation, praise or compliance. Validation acknowledges that an experience is understandable or real for the person; recognition, in Benjamin's stronger sense, also preserves the other as a separate subject whose independent mind gives recognition its value.

Ask:

- What do I want the other person to recognise?
- Do I need recognition, or agreement?
- Can the other person recognise me while still disagreeing?
- Can I recognise their separate experience without giving up my own?
- Am I treating the other as a subject or as the person who must restore my equilibrium?
- What happens when they refuse the role I want them to occupy?

A common collapse occurs when a disagreement becomes a struggle over whose subjectivity is allowed to exist. One person becomes the knower and the other the one who is wrong; one the controller and the other controlled; one the pursuer and the other the withdrawer. Benjamin calls attention to such complementarity, while warning that role reversal alone may leave the structure unchanged.

A recognition-based communication can sound like: **"I understand that you experienced my comment as dismissive. I did not intend that, and I also still disagree with the decision."** Both minds remain present. Recognition has not been turned into compulsory agreement.

## Attachment inquiry

Bowlby treats attachment as a distinct motivational system that remains active across life. Attachment behaviour becomes especially salient under threat, fatigue, illness, separation and uncertainty. The need for reassurance under stress is therefore not automatically immature.

Ask:

- What threat to connection did I perceive?
- Was there actual unavailability, or anticipated unavailability?
- What did I do to restore proximity or control?
- Did I pursue, test, protest, withdraw or detach?
- What did that behaviour evoke?
- What evidence suggests genuine safety or danger?
- Can I ask directly for connection rather than organise behaviour around the fear of losing it?

Bowlby's secure-base model links reliable accessibility with exploration and autonomy. Security does not mean constant closeness. It allows movement between connection and independent activity.

Avoid converting attachment "styles" into fixed identities. The supplied sources support attachment expectations, patterns and working models, but applied reflection is better served by asking what happens under particular conditions than by saying "I am X" or "my partner is Y".

A useful distinction is between **attachment alarm** and **relationship evidence**. An alarm can be activated by uncertainty, but the relationship may also genuinely be unreliable. The correct inquiry is not to dismiss alarm as irrational. It is to compare the alarm with current evidence and explicit agreements.

## Mentalization check

Before accepting any psychodynamic interpretation, apply a mentalization check. Fonagy and colleagues describe mentalization as the capacity to understand behaviour in relation to mental states while preserving the representational nature of those states. Inner experience matters, but it is not identical with external reality.

Ask:

- What do I actually know?
- What am I inferring?
- How certain am I?
- What evidence supports my inference?
- What evidence contradicts it?
- What are three other possible explanations?
- What might the other person say happened?
- What about my own mind is uncertain?
- What new information would change my view?

The central rule is:

> **Strong emotional certainty is not evidence of interpretive accuracy.**

Under high affective arousal, reflective capacity often narrows. A person can move toward what Fonagy and colleagues describe as psychic equivalence: "I feel abandoned, therefore I am being abandoned." Alternative explanations become harder to hold, while conviction can increase. This is a psychological and epistemic caution, not a claim that every intense emotion is inaccurate.

Use the habitual phrase:

> **"My current hypothesis is..."**

rather than:

> **"The real reason is..."**

Mentalization also applies inward. Introspection can mislead. A polished account of one's motives is still an account, not perfect access to the mind. Ask what about your own interpretation might be defensive, incomplete or state-dependent.

## Body and arousal

Body awareness belongs near the beginning of reflection because affect often becomes visible physically before it is clearly named. Notice:

- heart rate;
- muscular tension;
- heat;
- numbness;
- agitation;
- collapse;
- sexual arousal;
- urge to escape;
- urge to attack.

The purpose is descriptive. Do not infer unsupported neuroscience or treat a bodily state as proof of trauma, attachment style or hidden motive.

A high-arousal state can reduce the practical availability of reflection. When the body is mobilised toward attack, escape, urgent pursuit or collapse, the mind may become more certain and less capable of holding alternative representations. The useful move is often to postpone interpretation until enough arousal has fallen for complexity to return.

A brief body check can be: **Where is the strongest sensation? What action is the body preparing? What happens if I delay action for two minutes? Can I name the affect without explaining it?**

The aim is not suppression. It is to create a small interval between state and action.

## Mapping the couple loop

For the substantive theory of reciprocal couple dynamics, see `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md`. Here the task is operational: map the sequence without turning either person into a character diagnosis.

Use:

**Trigger -> my experience -> my protective response -> partner response -> my interpretation -> intensified response -> loop**

A simple example is: partner becomes quiet after work -> I fear something is wrong -> I ask repeatedly for reassurance -> partner asks for more space -> I interpret that as withdrawal -> I pursue harder. The map does not establish why either person acted as they did. It only makes the feedback sequence visible.

## From who started it to what maintains it

"Who started it?" and "What maintains it?" are different questions. An initiating breach should not disappear inside a symmetrical diagram, but later responses can still create additional breaches or prolong the sequence. File `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md` contains the fuller dyadic theory.

The governing safeguard is:

> **Understanding reciprocal maintenance must never erase asymmetry in responsibility.**

This is especially important with coercion, threats, violence, stalking, serious deception and non-consensual sexual behaviour. Those should not be reframed as a neutral "dance" produced equally by both people.

The practical question is narrower: **Whatever started this, what response of mine is now within my control?** The answer may be a different communication, waiting, disengagement, a firmer boundary or ending the interaction.

## Desire and sexual reflection

The theory of desire, attachment, separateness, erotic meaning and sexual withdrawal belongs primarily in `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md`. In this file, use sexuality as a plural inquiry rather than a code to decode.

Ask:

1. **What is happening physically?** Desire, arousal, pain, fatigue, sleep, bodily comfort, hormonal change, medication and general health all matter.
2. **What is happening relationally?** Closeness, resentment, mistrust, conflict, pressure, distance or unresolved injury?
3. **What is happening emotionally?** Shame, longing, anger, fear, tenderness, boredom, excitement, grief, performance anxiety or vulnerability?
4. **Is desire inhibited generally or specifically in this relationship?**
5. **What meaning has sexual contact acquired?** Pleasure, reassurance, proof of desirability, obligation, play, reconciliation, achievement or exposure?
6. **What does refusal or initiation seem to mean?** Distinguish the event from the meaning assigned to it.
7. **What ordinary or bodily explanation competes with the psychodynamic one?**

Always preserve medical factors, hormones, medication, pain, fatigue, stress, libido difference, preference, orientation and **consent**. Consent is not open to psychodynamic bargaining. A persistent libido difference may be a compatibility issue rather than a symptom.

A useful question is: **What burden is sex currently being asked to carry that could be discussed elsewhere in ordinary language?**

## Jealousy inquiry

The fuller theory of jealousy, rivalry, attachment threat and triangularity belongs in `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md`. Applied reflection starts with reality testing because jealousy can be either amplified by internal expectations or proportionate to secrecy, boundary ambiguity or actual betrayal.

Ask:

- What actual evidence exists?
- What loss do I fear?
- What comparison threatens me?
- What does this imply about my worth?
- Is there a realistic threat?
- Am I confusing uncertainty with betrayal?
- Am I trying to eliminate uncertainty through control?
- What reassurance would be reasonable?
- What boundary issue, if any, actually exists?

Keep the terms distinct: **jealousy** is usually triangular fear of losing a valued relationship or place; **envy** concerns another person's possession of something valued; **rivalry** concerns competition for status, attention, love or achievement.

The practical boundary is conduct. Asking for clarity about an agreed boundary can be reasonable. Surveillance, threats or attempts to abolish all uncertainty raise a different problem.

## Shame, guilt and repair

For the fuller theory of shame, guilt, concern and reparation in couple life, see `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md`. Here the distinction is practical:

**Shame:** "Something is wrong with me."

**Guilt:** "I may have done something harmful."

Ask:

- Am I collapsing into global self-condemnation?
- Am I using shame to avoid specific responsibility?
- Can I tolerate guilt without demanding immediate absolution?
- What impact needs to be acknowledged?
- What behaviour needs to change?
- What would repair look like in observable terms?

A statement such as "I am terrible" can sound morally serious while shifting attention from the harder task: naming what happened, its impact and what will change.

## Rupture-and-repair protocol

The relational theory of rupture and repair is developed in `06_LOVE_DESIRE_INTIMACY_AND_COUPLE_DYNAMICS.md`. Use this protocol to move from interpretation to observable responsibility and change:

1. **Establish what happened.** Separate agreed facts, disputed facts and interpretations.
2. **Describe impact.** Emotional, practical, sexual, financial or relational.
3. **Separate intention from impact.** Neither automatically cancels the other.
4. **Identify your defensive response.** Anger, explanation, withdrawal, self-attack, counter-accusation or minimisation?
5. **Mentalize tentatively.** Describe the other person's possible experience and invite correction.
6. **Take responsibility for observable behaviour.** Be specific.
7. **Clarify what remains disputed.** Repair does not require a false shared narrative.
8. **Identify the needed boundary or behavioural change.**
9. **Allow time.** Trust may lag behind apology or insight.
10. **Watch behaviour.** Repeated reliability is stronger evidence of repair than repeated explanation.

Repair is not forced agreement, immediate forgiveness, reassurance, self-abasement or psychoanalysing the other. Some ruptures reveal incompatibility or make continued relationship unsafe or unwanted; understanding does not create an obligation to reconcile.

## Reflection before communication

The purpose of private reflection is not to prepare a stronger prosecution case. It is to reduce the amount of untested interpretation carried into the conversation.

Before speaking, complete this short worksheet:

1. **What happened?** One or two observable sentences.
2. **What did I feel?** Name the main and secondary affects.
3. **What do I want?** Connection, explanation, apology, behavioural change, space, sex, reassurance, information, decision?
4. **What am I afraid of?** Rejection, humiliation, conflict, engulfment, uncertainty, loss, being wrong?
5. **What am I assuming?** Identify the main inference about motive or meaning.
6. **What part is hypothesis?** State the current working hypothesis and keep it provisional.
7. **What can I ask rather than infer?** Convert mind-reading into a question.
8. **What am I responsible for?** Name your behaviour without taking responsibility for theirs.
9. **What boundary or request is needed?** Make it observable and actionable.
10. **What outcome am I trying to control?** Agreement, reassurance, forgiveness, confession, closeness, continuation of the relationship? Which of these are not yours to command?

If the worksheet produces a long account of the partner's unconscious and very little about your own behaviour or request, return to the beginning.

## Communicating without psychoanalytic jargon

Psychoanalytic concepts are usually more useful in private reflection than in an argument. Technical language can become an authority move: it converts a disputed interaction into a diagnosis-like claim about the other person's mind.

Avoid statements such as:

- "You are projecting."
- "This is your attachment trauma."
- "You are splitting."
- "You are narcissistically injured."
- "You are enacting a bad object relationship."
- "You are making me carry your feelings."

Prefer ordinary language:

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "You are projecting." | "I think you are attributing anger to me that I do not recognise in myself. What are you noticing that makes you think I am angry?" |
| "Your attachment system is activated." | "The delay seemed to make you worry that I was pulling away. Is that close to what happened?" |
| "You are splitting me." | "Right now it sounds as if everything about me feels bad. Can we stay with the specific thing I did without rewriting the whole relationship?" |
| "You are narcissistically injured." | "My criticism seems to have landed as much bigger than I intended. What did it seem to say about you?" |
| "You are enacting a rejecting object." | "When you expect me to reject you, you sometimes pull away before I have answered. Then I feel shut out and stop trying. Can we look at that sequence?" |
| "You are intellectualising." | "I understand the analysis. I still do not know what you felt when it happened." |
| "This is projective identification." | "I notice I became defensive very quickly. I want to work out what you did, what I assumed and what I brought into it." |

The communication test is simple: **Does the sentence describe experience, behaviour, need or uncertainty, or does it claim superior knowledge of the other person's mind?** If it does the latter, rewrite it.

Useful ordinary-language forms include:

- "When that happened, I felt dismissed."
- "I noticed I became very defensive."
- "I think I assumed you meant something you may not have meant."
- "Can you tell me what was happening for you?"
- "I need some reassurance about..."
- "I am angry, but I also think I am hurt."
- "I want to understand what happened rather than guess."

## The 10-minute reflection protocol

This protocol is for an ordinary emotionally charged event when there is enough time to reflect but no need for a full review. Ten minutes is approximate. The point is to move through the sequence once without turning reflection into rumination.

1. **Event:** What happened, in one observable sentence?
2. **Affect:** What did I feel first, and what became dominant?
3. **Body:** What happened physically?
4. **Impulse:** What did I want to do?
5. **Meaning:** What did the event seem to say about me, them or us?
6. **Defence:** What may have become easier or harder to feel after my protective response appeared?
7. **Repetition:** Does this emotional configuration feel familiar, and is the current event genuinely similar?
8. **Alternatives:** Name at least three other explanations.
9. **My contribution:** What did I actually do that affected the next step?
10. **Next response:** What one action would add information, freedom or clarity?

### Completed 10-minute example

**Event:** My partner read my message at lunch and replied four hours later with "Sorry, hectic day."

**Affect:** I first felt anxious, then angry and resentful.

**Body:** Tight chest, restless checking of the phone, hot face when I saw the reply.

**Impulse:** Send a cold response and wait four hours before replying.

**Meaning:** "I am lower priority than everything else. If I matter, I should not have to ask for contact."

**Possible defence:** Anger and retaliatory distance may be protecting against the more vulnerable wish to feel remembered and important. This is only a hypothesis. The anger may also reflect a real communication expectation.

**Repetition:** I recognise a pattern of becoming certain I am unimportant when communication is delayed. I need to check whether it appears across relationships and whether this relationship has actually been unreliable.

**Alternatives:** They may genuinely have been busy. They may assume non-urgent messages can wait. We may have different norms for contact. My fear of being unimportant may be adding intensity.

**My contribution:** I checked repeatedly, drafted a sarcastic reply and almost converted uncertainty into punishment.

**Next response:** Ask later: "When replies take several hours, I sometimes start telling myself I am being ignored. I know that may not be what is happening. Can we agree what we each expect on busy workdays?"

The completed protocol does not reveal the hidden cause of the reaction. It produces a more testable conversation and interrupts the automatic retaliatory move.

## The 30-minute deep reflection protocol

Use this for a consequential event, a repeated loop or a decision that still feels emotionally confused after the shorter protocol. It should end with clearer questions or action, not with a longer theory.

### A. Event reconstruction

- What happened, in sequence?
- What was directly observed?
- What context mattered: timing, stress, work, health, sex, money, family, sleep, alcohol or other practical conditions?
- Which facts are agreed and which are disputed?

### B. Emotional sequence

- What did I feel first?
- What came next?
- What feeling disappeared when another became dominant?
- What was hardest to admit?
- What happened in the body?
- What action urge followed?

### C. Defence inquiry

- What internal state became difficult?
- What changed after my protective response appeared?
- What did the response protect in the short term?
- What did it cost?
- What would I feel if I did not use it?

### D. Fantasy and meaning

- What did the event seem to say about me?
- What did it seem to say about the other?
- What did it seem to say about the relationship?
- What role did I feel put into?
- What outcome did I immediately imagine?

### E. Internal-object check

- What version of the other person became active?
- What contradictory evidence became hard to remember?
- Did the person become all-good, all-bad, rejecting, controlling, helpless, unavailable or idealised in my mind?
- How does this internal picture compare with their actual behaviour?

### F. Attachment threat

- What signal of distance, danger or unavailability did I perceive?
- Was the unavailability actual, anticipated or both?
- What did I do to restore proximity, certainty or control?

### G. Narcissistic or self threat

- Did the event threaten competence, desirability, worth, status, moral identity, belonging or self-cohesion?
- Did I need the other to restore my equilibrium?
- Did shame turn into rage, collapse, explanation or devaluation?

### H. Recognition

- What did I want acknowledged?
- Was I asking to be understood, or demanding agreement?
- Did I leave room for the other's separate subjectivity?

### I. Couple loop

- What did I do?
- What did the other actually do?
- What did I infer about their internal state?
- How did each response make the next one more likely?
- What responsibility is mine, and what is not mine?

### J. Sexuality, if relevant

- What bodily, medical, hormonal, medication, fatigue, pain, stress, preference, orientation and consent factors matter?
- What meaning has sexual contact, initiation or refusal acquired?
- Is the issue desire, relationship meaning, bodily condition, incompatibility, or some combination?

### K. Alternatives and unknowns

Generate at least four competing hypotheses. Mark what evidence supports and challenges each. State what remains genuinely unknown.

### L. History and familiarity

- Have I experienced this pattern before?
- With whom?
- What is actually similar?
- What is merely emotionally familiar?
- What would be unfamiliar about a different response?

### M. Repair, action or decision

- What information should I ask for?
- What responsibility should I take?
- What boundary or request is needed?
- What behaviour change would matter?
- Is this a problem to understand, repair, tolerate, renegotiate or decide about?

A completed deep reflection should finish with no more than three working hypotheses and one or two concrete next actions. If the result is twenty explanations and no action, interpretation may have become avoidance.

## Hypothesis Test: multiple explanations and disconfirming evidence

Use this module before treating any consequential interpretation as a working conclusion. It combines two disciplines: **generate competing explanations** and **look for evidence that could make you revise them**.

Start with at least four hypotheses:

1. **Psychodynamic:** for example, anger may be protecting against shame or dependency.
2. **Relational/systemic:** for example, pursuit and withdrawal may be reinforcing one another.
3. **Ordinary situational:** fatigue, workload, poor timing or a mismatch in expectations may explain most of the event.
4. **Challenging:** include an explanation that is inconvenient to your preferred story, such as the possibility that your behaviour increased the pressure, or that behaviour you are psychologising is unacceptable on its face.

Add a bodily or medical hypothesis when relevant.

| Hypothesis | Evidence for | Evidence against | What it predicts | What information would help? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Psychodynamic | | | | |
| Relational/systemic | | | | |
| Situational | | | | |
| Challenging | | | | |

Then test the leading hypotheses:

- What evidence would make me revise this interpretation?
- What did I predict would happen if it were right?
- What actually happened?
- Which facts do not fit?
- Is there a simpler explanation with equal or greater explanatory power?
- Am I making the theory impossible to disprove by treating every contradiction as resistance or defence?
- Have I asked the other person rather than inferred their mind?
- What new information would distinguish among the competing explanations?

Avoid the self-sealing move: **"If they deny it, that proves the defence."** A useful hypothesis must remain revisable.

A broad verdict may become narrower after testing. "My partner is emotionally unavailable" may become "when conflict becomes intense, my partner asks for several hours alone and reliably re-engages later." The narrower statement may be less dramatic but more accurate and more useful.

The hierarchy is: **establish facts -> generate alternatives -> seek disconfirming evidence -> retain only provisional working hypotheses.** Disconfirmation is not failure; it is evidence that reflection remains connected to reality.

## Distinguishing pattern from event

One event is not a structure.

Before using words such as "repetition", "attachment pattern", "chronic devaluation" or "projective gridlock", ask:

- Has this happened repeatedly?
- Under what conditions?
- With whom?
- Is it stable across relationships or specific to one relationship?
- Has it changed over time?
- Does it disappear when practical stress falls?
- Does it occur only around a particular theme such as sex, money, work or family?
- Is there counterevidence showing flexibility?

Pattern claims should be supported by pattern evidence.

A single missed message can hurt without demonstrating abandonment expectations. One episode of withdrawal can be ordinary self-regulation. One angry argument can be serious without establishing a fixed couple dynamic. Conversely, repeated behaviour should not be dismissed merely because each episode has a plausible excuse. Frequency, context and change over time all matter.

## Developmental reconstruction: strict limits

Psychoanalytic theories often connect adult patterns to development. That can generate useful hypotheses, but retrospective certainty is a major risk.

Keep four categories separate:

1. **Remembered event:** a memory you currently have, still subject to ordinary limits of memory.
2. **Family narrative:** what you were told by relatives or records.
3. **Developmental inference:** a hypothesis based on present patterns, such as "perhaps needing others felt risky in my family environment."
4. **Fantasy or later reconstruction:** an imagined scene or explanatory story that may organise present meaning without being historical fact.

The governing rule is:

> **A plausible developmental story is not recovered historical fact.**

Do not use present relationship patterns to infer trauma without evidence. Do not treat vivid feeling, dream imagery or bodily response as recovered memory. Do not pressure yourself to find a childhood cause for every present difficulty.

A more disciplined formulation is: **"My current reaction is consistent with having learned that direct need was risky, but I do not know exactly how or when that expectation developed."**

Developmental hypotheses are useful when they increase compassion, complexity and choice. They are not useful when they create a false historical certainty.

## Self-analysis versus self-attack

Self-analysis should increase agency. Self-attack reduces the self to a defective object that must be explained, corrected or punished.

Warning signs include:

- endless causal searching;
- global conclusions of defectiveness;
- treating every feeling as pathology;
- assuming every strong reaction proves unresolved childhood material;
- ignoring actual mistreatment because "the real issue is mine";
- compulsive journalling that increases agitation rather than understanding;
- revisiting the same event without new information;
- growing certainty while complexity decreases;
- using psychoanalytic labels as insults against oneself;
- demanding complete self-knowledge before taking ordinary action.

A useful test is: **After reflection, do I have more possible responses or fewer?**

Good self-analysis can lead to specific responsibility: "I became sarcastic because I felt exposed, and I need to apologise for the sarcasm." Self-attack produces global condemnation: "I destroy every relationship because I am fundamentally damaged." The first supports repair. The second may actually avoid the harder work of specific change.

The aim is freedom and choice, not perfect psychological purity.

## When interpretation becomes avoidance

Analysis can become a sophisticated defence. Intellectualisation is one route, but the broader problem is using explanation to avoid experience, communication or decision.

Warning forms include:

- analysing instead of feeling;
- analysing the partner instead of stating a need;
- explaining a childhood origin instead of deciding whether current behaviour is acceptable;
- generating complexity to avoid setting a boundary;
- searching for the perfect formulation before apologising;
- repeatedly interpreting sexual difficulty while ignoring pain, medication or incompatibility;
- treating a relationship decision as if enough theory will remove uncertainty.

The mandatory question is:

> **Am I using analysis to understand the situation, or to avoid doing something I already know I need to do?**

Sometimes the next step is not deeper interpretation. It is sleeping, obtaining medical advice, asking a direct question, making an apology, changing a practical arrangement, declining a demand, or ending a conversation that has become unsafe.

## What growth looks like across the corpus

The following is a **corpus synthesis**, not a claim that all theorists share one definition of health. Each tradition emphasises a different dimension of increased freedom.

| Framework | Growth looks like |
|---|---|
| **Freud** | Greater awareness of conflict, repetition and compromise, with more capacity to choose rather than act under unrecognised compulsion |
| **Ego and defence theory** | More flexible defences, greater affect tolerance, better reality testing and a wider range of responses |
| **Klein** | Greater integration of loved and hated aspects of self and other, tolerance of ambivalence, concern and less persecutory splitting |
| **Fairbairn** | Less compulsory repetition of painful relational configurations and greater freedom to form relationships not governed by old internal bonds |
| **Winnicott** | More spontaneity and aliveness, less compulsory compliance, and greater capacity to be alone while remaining related |
| **Bion** | Greater capacity to tolerate frustration and transform emotional experience into thought rather than evacuate it into certainty or action |
| **Kohut** | More stable self-cohesion and self-esteem, with less fragile dependence on a particular other's perfect mirroring or strength |
| **Kernberg** | More integrated views of self and other, greater capacity to combine love and aggression, and less need for idealisation/devaluation |
| **Mitchell** | More freedom from rigid relational configurations and a broader repertoire of ways of being with others |
| **Benjamin** | More capacity for mutual recognition: asserting oneself while recognising another independent subject |
| **Bowlby** | Greater security and flexibility in seeking connection, using a secure base and moving between attachment and exploration |
| **Fonagy et al.** | More robust mentalization under stress, with better distinction between feeling, representation and external reality |

Growth is therefore not simply "having fewer defences" or "needing no one". It is greater complexity, tolerance, reality contact and freedom of action.

## Decision versus interpretation

Psychoanalytic understanding does not decide whether a relationship, job, sexual arrangement or pattern should continue.

After reflection, return to practical judgement:

- Is the behaviour acceptable?
- Is consent present and respected?
- Is there reciprocity over time?
- Has promised behaviour actually changed?
- Is trust being rebuilt through observable reliability?
- Are our values and life plans compatible?
- Do I want to continue this relationship?
- What boundary is required even if I fully understand the other person's reasons?

A pattern can be psychologically understandable and still unacceptable. Two people can understand each other well and remain incompatible. Repair can be possible without reconciliation. Insight does not create desire, trust or shared values by itself.

The end point of reflection is agency: **What do I know now, what remains uncertain, and what am I choosing?**

## Boundaries of self-reflection

Self-reflection should stop being the primary tool when immediate reality and safety require action.

Do not use psychodynamic interpretation to explain away:

- violence;
- coercive control;
- threats;
- stalking;
- non-consensual sexual behaviour;
- serious substance-related risk;
- acute psychiatric crisis;
- immediate safety risk.

In such situations, safety, consent, clear boundaries and appropriate professional or practical support take precedence. The task is not to discover the unconscious couple pattern that produced the danger. It is to establish what is happening and what is required to protect safety and agency.

The same general principle applies at lower intensity. If a partner repeatedly breaks a clearly stated boundary, the next question may be whether the boundary has a consequence, not which childhood configuration makes the behaviour understandable.

## Worked examples

All examples are invented. They demonstrate method, not diagnosis. Each includes a psychodynamic possibility, a relational possibility, an ordinary explanation and explicit uncertainty.

### 1. Delayed message interpreted as rejection

**Observation:** Alex sends a non-urgent message at 1 p.m. Their partner replies at 5:15 p.m.: "Sorry, back-to-back meetings. What's up?" No prior agreement required faster replies.

**Subjective experience:** By 3 p.m. Alex feels uneasy and increasingly angry. The delay begins to feel deliberate.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Anxiety, then resentment; tight chest and repeated phone checking; urge to send a sarcastic message or delay the eventual reply.

**Possible defence:** Anger and retaliatory distance may protect against longing, dependency or the shame of caring how quickly the partner responds. This is not established by the sequence.

**Fantasy/meaning:** "If I mattered, they would think of me. I am easy to forget."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** A Freudian lens might ask whether a present gap has acquired additional meaning from a familiar expectation. A Bowlbian lens might ask whether temporary inaccessibility has activated attachment alarm. A Fonagy-informed lens would notice movement from uncertainty toward psychic equivalence: feeling ignored becomes evidence of being ignored.

**Relational loop:** Anxiety leads to checking and coldness. Coldness may make the partner less inclined to engage warmly. Reduced warmth then appears to confirm rejection.

**Ordinary explanation:** The partner may simply have been in meetings and may regard a four-hour delay as normal for a non-urgent message.

**Disconfirming evidence:** The partner usually initiates contact, reliably follows through on plans and responds promptly to urgent messages. These facts weaken a broad "unavailable partner" formulation.

**Unknowns:** Whether Alex has similar reactions across relationships; whether the partner has recently become less reliable; whether work demands have changed.

**Reflective questions:** What fact did I have, and what story did I add? What did I want to ask for? Would the same delay have meant as much if I were absorbed in my own day?

**Possible next action:** State a preference without accusation: "I noticed I get anxious when I do not hear back for several hours. I know you may simply be busy. Can we clarify what we each expect on workdays?"

### 2. Criticism followed by anger

**Observation:** Priya tells Morgan, "You interrupted me several times at dinner." Morgan immediately replies, "You criticise everything I do," raises their voice and leaves the room.

**Subjective experience:** Morgan later reports a flash of feeling exposed and "made small", followed almost instantly by anger.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Brief shame, strong anger, hot face, clenched jaw; urge to counter-attack and escape the conversation.

**Possible defence:** Anger may be organising distance from shame or helplessness. Withdrawal may prevent further exposure. But the criticism could also have been delivered contemptuously, which would make anger partly proportionate.

**Fantasy/meaning:** "One mistake means I am fundamentally inadequate. If I stay, I will be humiliated."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** A Kohutian hypothesis would ask whether the criticism struck a fragile area of self-esteem. A Kernbergian hypothesis would ask whether criticism rapidly shifted the other from valued partner to devalued persecutor, making integration difficult in the moment. Defence theory asks what anger made easier and what shame made harder to feel.

**Relational loop:** Priya raises a specific complaint. Morgan hears global condemnation and attacks back. Priya becomes more prosecutorial to prove the original point. Morgan feels increasingly judged and withdraws.

**Ordinary explanation:** Priya may have a habitual critical tone; Morgan may be exhausted; the dinner may have involved alcohol or social embarrassment. These factors need direct assessment.

**Disconfirming evidence:** If Morgan can receive similar criticism from colleagues without global shame, the reaction may be specific to this relationship rather than a broad self-esteem pattern.

**Unknowns:** Tone, prior frequency of criticism, whether interruption is recurrent, and how repair usually occurs.

**Reflective questions:** What did I hear beyond the literal complaint? What would I feel if I did not get angry? Can I address the way the criticism was delivered without turning one complaint into "you always attack me"?

**Possible next action:** Return when calmer: "I can hear that I interrupted you. I also noticed I experienced your comment as a judgement of me, and I became defensive. Can we discuss the specific behaviour and the way we raise complaints?"

### 3. Partner withdrawal followed by pursuit

**Observation:** After a disagreement about household spending, Lee says, "I need an hour before we continue." Sam follows Lee from room to room asking whether the relationship is okay and sends several messages after Lee goes for a walk.

**Subjective experience:** Sam experiences the request for space as the beginning of abandonment. Lee reports that repeated questions feel like pressure and make it harder to think.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Sam feels fear, then anger; agitation and racing thoughts; urge to restore connection immediately. Lee reports feeling overwhelmed and defensive; urge to create more distance.

**Possible defence:** Sam's pursuit may reduce helplessness by converting uncertainty into action. Lee's withdrawal may regulate arousal and protect against saying something aggressive, or it may become a way of avoiding conflict if re-engagement does not occur.

**Fantasy/meaning:** Sam: "Space means they are leaving." Lee: "If I stay in the conversation, I will be swallowed or lose control."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** Bowlby helps frame Sam's protest around perceived unavailability without turning it into a fixed attachment identity. Benjamin and Mitchell help map the complementary positions of pursuer and withdrawer. A Winnicottian question might ask whether separateness can be tolerated without experiencing it as abandonment or impingement.

**Relational loop:** Lee asks for space → Sam pursues → Lee needs more distance → Sam experiences stronger abandonment → pursuit intensifies.

**Ordinary explanation:** Some people simply need more processing time after conflict. A one-hour pause can be a reasonable regulation strategy if there is a clear agreement to return.

**Disconfirming evidence:** If Lee reliably re-engages at the agreed time, the hypothesis that withdrawal means abandonment is weakened. If Lee routinely disappears for days, the external reality changes.

**Unknowns:** Whether time-outs have clear rules, whether either person feels unsafe, and whether both can re-enter discussion.

**Reflective questions:** Can Sam tolerate a bounded pause if return is explicit? Can Lee ask for space without making it indefinite? What behaviour from each person would make the pause safer?

**Possible next action:** Agree a time-out structure: "We stop for one hour, no repeated messaging, and we resume at 8 p.m. unless one of us explicitly asks to reschedule."

### 4. Jealousy around a colleague

**Observation:** Taylor's partner mentions having lunch twice in one week with a new colleague and has exchanged evening messages about a project. Taylor asks to see the messages and becomes angry when the partner refuses.

**Subjective experience:** Taylor feels threatened, replaceable and preoccupied with imagined comparisons.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Jealousy, fear, envy and anger; stomach tension and agitation; urge to inspect the phone and obtain certainty.

**Possible defence:** Control may function as an attempt to eliminate intolerable uncertainty. Devaluation of the colleague may protect self-esteem. Neither formulation determines whether a real boundary issue exists.

**Fantasy/meaning:** "If my partner enjoys someone else, I am no longer special. Uncertainty means betrayal is probably already happening."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** Bowlby makes threatened attachment and jealous protest intelligible. Kohut invites inquiry into replaceability and self-esteem. A triangular Freudian or object-relations lens may highlight rivalry. These hypotheses should follow, not replace, assessment of the actual facts.

**Relational loop:** Taylor's questioning becomes intrusive. The partner becomes more protective of privacy. Increased privacy is then read as evidence of concealment, which intensifies checking.

**Ordinary explanation:** The lunches and messages may be entirely work-related. Alternatively, the couple may have an explicit agreement about emotional or sexual boundaries that is being approached or crossed. The content and agreement matter more than the label "jealousy".

**Disconfirming evidence:** Transparent scheduling, consistent behaviour and the colleague's contact being clearly work-based would weaken a betrayal hypothesis. Evidence of deletion, lies or boundary breaches would strengthen a reality-based concern.

**Unknowns:** The couple's agreed boundaries, message content, prior trust history and whether information has been concealed.

**Reflective questions:** What evidence exists? What reassurance is reasonable? What boundary can be stated without demanding surveillance? Am I trying to solve uncertainty through control?

**Possible next action:** Discuss the boundary directly: "I do not want access to your private messages as a condition of trust. I do want us to clarify what we each consider appropriate contact with colleagues outside work."

### 5. Sexual withdrawal where the situational explanation is stronger

**Observation:** Over three months, Casey's sexual desire falls sharply. Casey recently started a medication associated with reduced libido, is sleeping poorly and reports pelvic pain. Jordan interprets the change as loss of attraction and initiates sex more frequently for reassurance. Casey begins to avoid even non-sexual affection because it feels likely to become a test.

**Subjective experience:** Jordan feels rejected and undesirable. Casey feels pressured, guilty and physically uncomfortable.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Jordan feels shame, sadness and anxiety; urge to seek reassurance through initiation. Casey feels apprehension and resentment; bodily pain and fatigue; urge to avoid situations that could lead to sexual expectation.

**Possible defence:** Jordan's increased pursuit may defend against shame by seeking proof of desirability. Casey's avoidance may partly protect against pressure. These are secondary hypotheses.

**Fantasy/meaning:** Jordan: "No sex means no desire, therefore no love." Casey: "If I show affection, I will have to manage Jordan's disappointment."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** Couple theory can illuminate how sex becomes burdened with reassurance and obligation. Recognition and self-esteem questions may explain the secondary loop. Yet they should not be promoted above the physical evidence.

**Relational loop:** Reduced desire from pain/medication/fatigue → Jordan experiences rejection → initiation carries more reassurance need → Casey experiences more pressure → broader avoidance → Jordan feels more rejected.

**Ordinary explanation:** Here the simple situational and bodily explanation is more persuasive as the starting account. Medication, pain and poor sleep provide direct reasons for reduced desire. Psychodynamic meaning may explain what the couple does around the change, not why the change began.

**Disconfirming evidence:** If desire and comfort return when pain, sleep and medication issues improve, a primary theory of unconscious hostility or fear of intimacy loses weight.

**Unknowns:** Medical evaluation, baseline libido difference, relationship resentment and how desire changes after bodily factors are addressed.

**Reflective questions:** What changed physically before desire changed? Can affection be separated from a promise of sex? Can Jordan name reassurance needs without turning sex into proof of worth?

**Possible next action:** Prioritise appropriate medical assessment and remove pressure from sexual contact while discussing affection, reassurance and desire as separate issues.

### 6. Setting a boundary followed by guilt and over-explaining

**Observation:** Taylor declines a family request to give up a planned weekend and says, "I cannot do that this time." No one argues. Within an hour, Taylor sends several additional messages explaining the decision, offers to solve parts of the problem remotely and begins considering cancelling the original plan anyway.

**Subjective experience:** Relief is quickly followed by guilt, anxiety and fear of seeming selfish or uncaring.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Tightness in the chest, restless checking of the phone and an urge to explain until the other person signals that the relationship is safe.

**Possible defence:** Over-explaining and immediate problem-solving may reduce the discomfort of guilt, dependency and uncertainty. They may also function as a way of undoing the boundary before anyone has actually challenged it. This remains a hypothesis; the extra explanation may simply reflect courtesy.

**Fantasy/meaning:** "If I disappoint someone I care about, I become a bad or unreliable person. If they are unhappy with me, the relationship may be damaged."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** An ego/defence lens might ask whether guilt or fear of disapproval is being managed through undoing or compulsive justification. A relational lens might ask whether Taylor preserves connection by occupying the accommodating role and finds it difficult to discover that a relationship can survive a firm no.

**Ordinary explanation:** The request may genuinely matter, and Taylor may reasonably want to explain the practical constraints. A single detailed message does not establish a pattern.

**Disconfirming evidence:** The family member replies, "No problem, enjoy your weekend," and later behaves normally. That weakens the prediction that setting the boundary would damage the relationship.

**Unknowns:** Whether this pattern recurs across relationships, whether earlier boundaries have actually been punished, and whether Taylor's help was requested or volunteered.

**Reflective questions:** What did I fear would happen after saying no? What evidence did I have that more explanation was needed? Can I tolerate another person's possible disappointment without immediately repairing it? What would happen if I let the original boundary stand?

**Possible next action:** Do not send another explanatory message. Observe what actually happens, and use that outcome as new evidence about whether a respectful boundary can coexist with connection.

### 7. Recurring self-criticism after success

**Observation:** After receiving strong praise and a promotion, Daniel spends the evening listing mistakes from the previous year and tells their partner, "They will realise they made a mistake." Similar self-attack has followed earlier achievements.

**Subjective experience:** Success produces excitement for a few minutes, then exposure, guilt and dread.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Pride, then shame and anxiety; restlessness and stomach tension; urge to minimise the achievement and work excessively.

**Possible defence:** Turning against the self may reduce the danger of pride, rivalry or exposure. Self-criticism may also preserve a familiar sense of control: if Daniel attacks first, others cannot surprise them with criticism.

**Fantasy/meaning:** "If I take pleasure in success, I become arrogant, disloyal or vulnerable to being exposed."

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** Freud's superego model raises the possibility of punitive internal standards. Kohut suggests examining whether achievement is carrying too much self-esteem regulation. A relational hypothesis asks whether success threatens a familiar position in relation to admired or competitive others.

**Relational loop:** Success → self-attack → partner reassures → reassurance gives short-term relief → Daniel discounts praise → partner reassures more strongly → Daniel becomes increasingly dependent on external contradiction while preserving the original self-attack.

**Ordinary explanation:** The promotion may genuinely involve a steep learning curve, and Daniel may be accurately aware of gaps. Realistic preparation should be separated from global self-condemnation.

**Disconfirming evidence:** If Daniel can identify specific development areas, accept praise and work proportionately after reflection, the process may be ordinary humility rather than a rigid pattern.

**Unknowns:** Family messages about pride and competition, workplace culture, whether similar reactions occur outside achievement.

**Reflective questions:** What would I feel if I allowed myself to enjoy success for one evening? What specific evidence supports "I am a fraud"? What does self-attack accomplish that realistic planning does not?

**Possible next action:** Separate evaluation from condemnation: write three genuine strengths, two concrete development needs and no global character judgements; ask the partner for celebration rather than repeated reassurance.

### 8. Rupture, attempted repair and a boundary that interpretation must not override

**Observation:** During an argument, Rowan shouts insults, follows Jamie from room to room after Jamie asks to stop, and blocks the doorway for several seconds when Jamie tries to leave. The next morning Rowan apologises, says fear of abandonment took over and asks Jamie to understand the behaviour as an attachment reaction. A similar incident has occurred before.

**Subjective experience:** Jamie reports fear, anger and loss of safety. Rowan reports panic, shame and fear of being left.

**Affect, body and impulse:** Jamie experiences strong arousal and urge to get distance. Rowan describes panic and urge to prevent separation.

**Possible defence:** There may be psychodynamic hypotheses about panic, shame, control or attachment protest. They are deliberately not pursued as the primary task because the observable conduct has crossed a boundary.

**Fantasy/meaning:** Jamie experiences the event as "I may not be free to leave when I need to." Rowan describes separation as "If you leave the room, the relationship is ending." Both meanings can be psychologically important, but they do not have equal practical implications.

**Psychodynamic hypotheses:** Attachment alarm may help explain Rowan's subjective state. Defence theory may help explain the move from fear to anger and control. Explanation does not justify restricting another person's movement.

**Relational loop:** It would be misleading to frame the incident mainly as a symmetrical pursuer-withdrawer loop. Jamie's attempt to leave an escalating argument does not make Jamie equally responsible for Rowan blocking the exit.

**Ordinary explanation:** Whatever its psychological causes, blocking another person's exit and continuing intimidation after a clear request to stop are unacceptable behaviours on their face.

**Disconfirming evidence:** A verbal apology is not disconfirming evidence of risk. Relevant evidence would be sustained behavioural change, respect for separation, acceptance of boundaries and no recurrence.

**Unknowns:** The broader pattern, severity, available support and whether Jamie currently feels safe.

**Reflective questions:** What happened, without softening it through theory? What boundary is required? What support is needed? What evidence of change would matter over time?

**Possible next action:** Prioritise safety, practical support and a clear boundary. Jamie is not obliged to continue the relationship because Rowan can explain the behaviour psychologically. Understanding a pattern does not create an obligation to remain in an unacceptable situation.

## A compact master worksheet

Use this when you want one integrated page rather than moving through the whole file. Keep answers short. If a box requires an essay, return to the 30-minute protocol.

### A. Reality

- What happened?
- What do I actually know?
- What explicit agreement, context or practical fact matters?

### B. Experience

- What did I feel first and next?
- What happened in my body?
- What did I want to do?

### C. Meaning

- What did the event seem to say about **me**?
- What did it seem to say about **them**?
- What did it seem to say about **us**?

### D. Defence

- What feeling, wish or fear became difficult?
- How did I protect myself from it?
- What did that protection achieve, and what did it cost?
- What would I feel if I did not do this?

### E. History

- Does this feel familiar?
- Is the current situation genuinely similar?
- What evidence supports a repetition claim?

### F. Self

- Was there shame, humiliation or threat to self-esteem?
- What recognition, admiration, reassurance or understanding did I want?
- Did I need the other person to restore my equilibrium?

### G. Attachment

- What threat to connection or autonomy did I perceive?
- Was unavailability actual or anticipated?
- Did I pursue, protest, test, withdraw or detach?

### H. Internal relationship

- What version of the other person became active in my mind?
- What contradictory qualities became hard to remember?
- Is my internal picture consistent with their actual behaviour?

### I. Reciprocity

- What did I do?
- What did they actually do?
- What loop followed?
- What part is mine to change, and what part is not mine?

### J. Mentalization

- What am I inferring?
- What are three alternatives?
- What might the other person say happened?
- What remains unknown?
- What new information would change my view?

### K. Reality check

- What ordinary external explanation fits?
- Are practical stress, fatigue, illness, medication, pain, money, work or timing more persuasive?
- Is there actual mistreatment, incompatibility, deception or a boundary problem that should not be psychologised away?

### L. Choice

- What can I do differently?
- What do I need to communicate?
- What request or boundary is required?
- Is this a problem to understand, repair, tolerate, renegotiate or decide about?

### Tool hierarchy

Use the smallest tool that fits the problem:

- **FAST - 10-minute reflection protocol:** one ordinary charged event.
- **STANDARD - compact master worksheet:** the default full review of one event or interaction.
- **DEEP - 30-minute deep reflection protocol:** repeated, consequential or still-confusing patterns.
- **OPTIONAL MODULES:** add only what is relevant: defence; repetition; internal objects; self/self-esteem; attachment; mentalization; sexuality; repair. Recognition and jealousy inquiries are narrower add-ons when those domains are central.
- **HYPOTHESIS TEST:** finish any consequential interpretation with competing explanations and disconfirming evidence.

Supporting tools remain available when useful: the event-to-loop map, pre-conversation worksheet, periodic relationship-pattern review and personal working-hypothesis map. They support the three main levels rather than constituting additional required stages.

## Questions this framework helps you ask

### About reality and interpretation

- What happened before I interpreted motive?
- Which words in my description are observations and which are conclusions?
- What do I know, what do I infer, and what remains unknown?
- What is the least psychodynamic explanation that fits the facts?
- What evidence would make me change my mind?

### About affect and defence

- What did I feel first?
- What feeling became dominant, and what disappeared?
- What feeling would be hardest to admit?
- What did I want to do?
- What would I feel if I did not use this protective response?
- What does the defence solve in the short term?
- What does it cost in intimacy, reality testing, spontaneity or action?

### About repetition and the internal world

- Have I felt this configuration before?
- What role do I repeatedly occupy?
- What kind of other do I expect?
- What does the familiar pattern preserve?
- What would be unfamiliar about a different outcome?
- What version of the other person is active in my mind?
- Can I remember contradictory qualities while upset?

### About self-esteem and recognition

- What did this event seem to say about my worth?
- Did a specific criticism become a global judgement of me?
- Am I seeking recognition, admiration, reassurance or agreement?
- Can I recognise the other person's separate experience without abandoning my own?
- What happens when the other refuses the role I want them to occupy?

### About attachment and mentalization

- What threat to connection did I perceive?
- Was there actual unavailability or anticipated unavailability?
- What did I do to restore proximity or certainty?
- Am I treating fear as proof of another person's intention?
- Would I make the same interpretation if I were calmer?
- What are three plausible mental states that could explain the same behaviour?

### About the couple loop

- What did I do that made the next response more likely?
- What did the other actually do, rather than what I imagine they felt?
- What role did I move into, and what role did I invite?
- What maintains the pattern even if responsibility for starting it is unequal?
- What one step of the loop is within my control?
- What should not be reframed as mutual because it is a clear boundary violation?

### About sexuality and jealousy

- What physical, medical, hormonal, medication, pain, fatigue or stress factors matter?
- What meaning has sexual contact, refusal or initiation acquired?
- Is desire being asked to prove love, worth or security?
- What actual evidence exists for the jealous fear?
- Am I seeking reasonable reassurance or trying to abolish uncertainty through control?
- What boundary has actually been agreed?

### About guilt, repair and action

- Am I feeling specific guilt or global shame?
- Can I take responsibility without demanding immediate absolution?
- What behaviour needs to change?
- What would repair look like in observable terms?
- Is trust changing because behaviour is changing, or only because explanations are improving?
- Am I using analysis to understand the situation, or to avoid doing something I already know I need to do?
- Is this a relationship problem to repair, or a decision to make?

The strongest use of the framework ends with greater uncertainty about hidden causes but greater clarity about experience, evidence, responsibility and choice.

### Source and application limits

The main source limits carried forward from the corpus remain important. Freud's 1914 *Remembering, Repeating and Working-Through* is not directly supplied in full, so the repetition discussion relies on the supplied 1920 restatement and later corpus synthesis rather than claiming direct access to the 1914 paper. The foundational sources are stronger on guilt than on a comprehensive theory of shame, so shame is handled mainly through later integrative, self and relational material. Developmental reconstructions in Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, Kohut and related traditions remain psychoanalytic hypotheses rather than direct records of infant subjective experience.

No attempt has been made to reconcile genuine theoretical conflicts into one model. Drive, object-relational, self-psychological, relational, attachment and mentalization accounts remain competing lenses. Their practical value here lies in disciplined comparison.
